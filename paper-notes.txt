Small, disc-shaped echinoderms, lacking columns of any kind, probably attached by suction on nearly flat aboral side. Theca
composed of numerous calcareous plates, distinctly separated into central oral and aboral discs, submarginal ring, and mar- ginal ring. Submarginal ring of thick plates, exposed on both oral and aboral surfaces of theca, forming stout frame for attachment of thin oral and aboral discs. Oral disc cir-cular, flexible, contallllllg very numerous small plates, weakly calcified, serving to cover soft parts of animal and incorporating radiating, multiply branched, enclosed am- bulacra. Aboral disc less flexible than oral disc, serving as floor under body. Submar- ginal plates complex; proximal part of each plate radially perforated; distal part bearing facets on oral surface. Perforation connected with ambulacral passageways and aligned with facets. Marginal ring very flexible, covered with tiny imbricating plates. M. Ord.-MDev.

1984-04-00-p Classification of the Echinodermata.pdf
(ix) Ophiocistioids (‘Class’ Ophiocistioidea). Ophiocistioids share a number of synapomorphies with the Echinozoa. The discovery of the Devonian ophiocistioid Rotasaccus by Haude and Langenstrassen (1976) was a most important find, since Rotasaccus has the body wall skeleton of a holothuroid but possesses an echinoid-type lantern. This provides evidence that stem group holothuroids possessed a lantern even though it has been lost in all living holothuroids. Ophiocistioids are undoubtedly a paraphyletic group and an analysis of character distribution within this group will lead to a better understanding of the early history of the Echinozoa.

> A true Chengjiang echinoderm was first reported in 2003 (Shu, 2003, Fig. 3-m). Soon after that, a group of similar, well-preserved samples (vetulocystids) were recovered in two separated localities in Kunming. As a result, another Article entitled “Ancestral echinoderms from the Chengjiang deposits of China” by Shu, Conway Morris, Han, Zhang and Liu appeared in Nature (2004). > ''6.4.1. Vetulocystids as an evolutionary link to echinoderms''
> Vetulocystids have some intriguing similarities to two groups on account of a bipartite body-plan (Shu et al., 2004). The first group is the vetulicolians, interpreted as stem-group deuteros- tomes and possessing segmentation and gill slits (Shu et al., 2001b; Chen, A. et al., 2003; Chen, J. et al., 2003; Luo et al., 2005). The second is an assemblage of early Palaeozoic echinoderms known as the homalozoans, which comprise the cinctans, ctenocystoids, solutes and stylophorans (Lefebvre, 2003; Sprinkle, 1992). Similarities of the vetulocystids to the vetulicolians are generalized, including a body defined by a large anterior section with a presumed pharyngeal opening and a posterior segmented section with a gut in it (Fig. 8-A-C). The body-plan of vetulocystids also has anatomical parallels to the homalozoans: the shared possession of a bipartite body-plan, having a series of thecal openings. In addition, the arrangement in the vetulocystids in dorsal aspect and a clockwise direction of mouth, respiratory organ and anus is similar to the arrangement in homalozoans (Shu et al., 2004). Accordingly, the vetulocystids provide a key link between the vetulicolians and homalozoans, and logically they should be ancestral to extant echinoderms (Shu et al., 2004) (Fig. 8; a similar conclusions are drawn by Smith (2004, 2005) and Conway Morris (2006)).

> ''6.5. Phylogeny of deuterostomes''
> In the terms of both the “root” group vetulicolians and the “top” group vertebrates in the early deuterostome lineage, as well as those members in between, (including ancestral echinoderms vetulocystids, cephalochordates and urochordates, and possible hemichordates yunnanozoans), it has been possible to recover a relatively complete early deuterostome phylogeny, which is compatible with that based on the extant deuterostomes (Shu et al., 2004; Shu, 2005a, 2006) (Fig. 9).
> Plesiomorphic characters to all deuterostomes are segmenta- tion and a bipartite body, the anterior of which possesses gill slits. The posterior is a tail-like structure, and segmented with gut and terminal anus. Vetulicolians may be the most primitive known deuterostomes, showing segmentation of the entire body, and an anterior with five pairs of gill slits. Therefore, the recent common ancestor of the two crown group deuterostomes Ambulacraria and Chordata should be some kind of vetulicolian-like animal. This ancestor most probably evolved toward the most primitive Ambulacraria and the earliest Chordata chiefly by loss of the anterior segmentation and acquisition of notochord respectively. In Ambulacraria vetulocystids are regarded as more derived than the hemichordates, but retain the bipartite body and a respiratory organ that also characterize the most primitive echinoderms (i.e. homalozoans). In more primitive Ambulacraria the gut extended anteriorly along the posterior tail, but eventually became restricted to the anterior body. All echinoderms, including homalozoans, possess a mesodermal calcitic skeleton (stereom), but the most primitive representatives still retain gill slits. The acquisition of a water-vascular system and ambulacra was a subsequent development. The branch of yunnanozoans-hemi- chordates is developed via acquisition of both the dorsal and ventral nerve chords. Towards the crown group Ambulacraria (echinoderms and hemichordates) the continuing loss of the posterior segmentation or even loss of the entire posterior tail is regarded a key step. Although the relationship between urochordates and cephalochordates remains unsolved, acquisition of the neural crest and tripartite brain with well-developed sense organs, including paired eyes, has certainly resulted in the emergence of vertebrates.

> The wider evolutionary significance of T. ignota and the vetulicystids in general remains somewhat problematic. Shu et al. (2002) suggested this group were ancestral echinoderms. Supported in part by relative stratigraphic position, it is possible that T. ignota is more derived than the Chengjiang taxa and in particular the tentacles were coelomic structures that were the distant precursors of feeding ambulacra. In this context, attention should also be drawn to Yanjiahella Guo et al., 2012 from the Series 2 Cambrian (Guo et al., 2012). Although nominally three species, it is possible that these represent taphonomic variants. In any event, the circular anterior bearing arm-like structures, a possible opening (regarded as a “madreporite” by Guo et al., 2012), and elongate “tail”, are reminiscent of the vetulicystids but are also comparable to primitive echinoderms.
Whatever role early vetulicystids may have had in the evolution of echinoderms, it is worth noting that this, the youngest known vetulicystid, lived in an environment in which unequivocal echinoderms thrived locally. These included the eocrinoid Gogia spiralis Robison, 1965, as well as ctenocystids (Ctenocystis colodon Ubaghs and Robison, 1988). In the nearby House Range, equivalent stratigraphy has yielded abundant specimens of G. spiralis, the stylophoran Archaeocothurnus bifida (Ubaghs and Robison, 1988), and the homoiostelean Castericystis vali Ubaghs and Robison, 1985.

> ''Discussion''

> Thylacocercus ignota.—Shu et al. (2002) suggested that vetulicystids may have been capable of limited locomotion, but were predominantly sessile. The possible holdfast of T. ignota indicates that this taxon was most likely a member of the sessile epifauna. The apparently tentacular anterior points towards T. ignota being a suspension feeder. Of the diverse assemblage of other infaunal and epifaunal fossils found at the same locality and meter interval as T. ignota (Halgedahl et al., 2009), none has been identified as a suspension feeder. However, a single 2 m interval about 4m below the T. ignota horizon contains numerous sponges.

> ''Conclusions''

> Absence in T. ignota of features such as oral and anal cones may be artifacts of preservation. The pre- sence, however, of anterior tentacles suggests some significant differences with the Chengjiang vetulicystids, but they are not inconsistent with this group falling within the stem-group ambulacrarians. In this respect, they may be informative as to the appearance of pre-echinoderms.

> 4. Other possible echinoderms from Chengjiang: Eldoniids, Cambrofengia, Dianchicystis and Vetulocystis

> In 2004, Shu et al. described the two new genera Vetulocystis and Dianchicystis (family Vetulocystidae), which they considered as a sister- group to echinoderms within the Ambulacraria. Shu (2008, p. 227) even considered them as the first ‘true Chengjiang echinoderm[s].’ The vetulocystoids did not secret a mineralized skeleton but possessed a cuticle in marked contrast to any echinoderm. Vetulocystids do share some similarities with vetulicolians, other problematic animals from Chengjiang fauna, particularly in their strong division into anterior and posterior parts, possible gill-slits, the cuticle and the tail like segmen- tation (at least on the holotype of the species, Shu et al., 2004). The affinities of the vetulicolians are still under discussion and their deu- terostome affinities are far from certain (Briggs et al., 2005; Gee, 2001). According to Aldridge et al. (2007), if the vetulicolians are protostomes, a parsimonious position would be close to the kinorhynchs; if the vetulicolians represent deuterostomes, it is more likely that they are close to the tunicates. Aldridge et al. (2007) also consider that a placement of vetulicolians at the base of the deuterostomes is not supported by the evidence (Aldridge et al., 2007). These conclusions contrast with the phylogeny proposed by Shu et al. (2004, 2009) who consider the vetulicolians as basal to all deuterostomes. As a consequence of this controversy, the position of the vetulocystids within deuterostomes is also quite problematic as Shu et al. (2004) interpreted them as closely related to and derived from the suggested basal vetulicolian-deutero- stome. Here we agree with Swalla and Smith (2008) that their placement within ambulacraria is quite enigmatic as they do not exhibit any obvious similarities (synapomorphies) with echinoderms (whatever the defini- tion considered). The presence of any synapomorphies shared with ambulacraria is also disputable (see Smith, 2004 and Shu et al., in press for alternative interpretation of vetulocystids features including the possible ‘gill-slits’ as respiratory structures). Only their conical structures show growth similar to some derived echinoderm features, but Shu et al. (2004, fig 4) had to interpret them as autapomorphies of vetulocystids to fit with the next basal echinoderms in their phylogeny (carpoids). Moreover, a basal position of vetulocystids along the echinoderm branch implies the loss of posterior segmentation along both echinoderms (crownward from vetulocystids) and hemichordates (Shu et al., 2004, fig 4), which is not parsimonious. Indeed, as pointed out by Aldridge et al. (2007), within deuterostomes, hemichordates, tunicates and echinoderms lack any sign of segmentation at any stage of their development. Recently, Shu et al. (in press) considered with some scepticism the application of the cladistic methodology to investigate the early deuterostome phylogeny and the use of phylogenetic trees as frameworks to predict or interpret the mor- phological character of possible stem-groups. They suggested that ‘the vetulicystid group could well be ancestral to all these important additions’ (i.e. echinoderm stereom, water vascular system, and symmetry, see Shu et al., in press, supplementary data). Such a statement is difficult to discuss on a scientific base as it could be used in support to any placement along any deuterostome (or even metazoan) branch of the tree of life.
