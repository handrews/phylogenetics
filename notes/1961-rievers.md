A New *Pyrgocystis* (Echinodermata, Edrioasteroidea)
from the Bundenbach Roof Slates (Devonian)

By JOHANNES RIEVERS, Enkirch on the Moselle

With Plate 2

Published posthumously by RICHARD DEHM, Munich

Editor’s Preface

Mrs. Henny Rievers-Brey of Enkirch (Moselle) kindly entrusted me with the posthumous notes of her late husband, Mr. JOHANNES RIEVERS—Retired Senior Postal Inspector, born August 16, 1880, in Hamburg; died December 12, 1955, in Bonn—for review and potential publication. I accepted this task with pleasure; for in JOHANNES RIEVERS, paleontology had a most enthusiastic devotee—let us hear from him in his own words! My wish to be allowed to study geology was denied. Consequently, I joined the *Reichspost* (Imperial Postal Service) in Hamburg as a civil servant and was able to pursue my interest in geology only in my spare time.

As an avid collector of fossils, I maintained close ties with the Geological Institute in Hamburg. Professor Gripp of the local university advised me to specialize in the Bundenbach slates, noting that the slate slabs were flat, making a collection of such fossils easy to house. I followed this suggestion and, during numerous hiking trips through the Hunsrück region, regularly visited the Bundenbach slate quarries. While there, I closely observed the extraction of the slate layers. The village of Bundenbach is situated in the southern Hunsrück. The fossils found in the Bundenbach slates are world-renowned within the field of geology.

On April 26, 1937, I delivered my first lecture on my area of ​​specialization, titled: "The Lower Devonian Roofing Slates of Bundenbach and Their Fossils." In it, I presented my newly formulated theory regarding the probable cause of death and the mode of burial of the animal remains. This theory posits that a catastrophic event triggered a mass extinction; subsequently, agitated sediment—settling back down from the seawater—enveloped the freshly deceased animals, thereby creating the specific conditions necessary for fossilization.

During the war, I discovered several new species, including a previously unknown flatfish.

Tragically, during the first major air raid on Hamburg in July 1943, my home was completely destroyed by bombing, resulting in the total loss of my original specimens, photographs, and written descriptions.

In December 1943, I relocated to Enkirch—situated on the Hunsrück side of the Moselle River—and by 1944, I had already begun building a new collection.

Encouraged by my successes, I decided to establish a research facility complete with a laboratory and an exhibition space.

Happily, the number of new discoveries continued to grow—among them, a large *Pyrgocystis*... — a peculiar marine animal belonging to the group of echinoderms, which existed approximately 400 million years ago — has aroused particular interest among specialists. This specimen has now undergone preliminary preparation, and I hope to be able to publish the details of this find later this summer.” (From the transcript of a report broadcast by Südwestfunk — Studio Mainz — on May 4 and June 11, 1951.)

Among the author’s posthumous papers is a manuscript concerning this newly discovered echinoderm belonging to the group Edrioasteroidea. Mr. Rievers devoted a great deal of time and effort to the meticulous preparation of the specimen and to laying the groundwork for its scientific examination. However, he was not destined to complete the manuscript. The present notes cover the discovery of the fossil, the diagnosis of the new species, and a brief description. A subsequent section consists merely of an excerpt from Bather’s seminal study of 1915 and may therefore remain unpublished.

The editor has limited his contribution to the preface, a few minor editorial adjustments, the insertion of certain measurements, and the provision of the accompanying plate, along with the relevant explanatory notes and references. My thanks are due to Mrs. Henny Rievers-Bley for making the manuscripts available and for entrusting the specimen to me for further study (p. 3). Mrs. Henny Rievers-Bley provided invaluable support for her husband’s work during his lifetime and, following his passing, demonstrated exemplary dedication to preserving his legacy.

I am indebted to Dr. A. v. Hillebrandt of Munich for the photographs featured in Plate 2.

Introduction

While splitting a Bundenbach slate slab from the Eschenbach I mine—which contained embedded detritus from various echinoderms—a portion of a fossil was uncovered that might initially have been mistaken for the tip of an arm of *Echinostella traquairi*. However, during the subsequent preparation of the slab, a fossil was exposed possessing a body form hitherto unknown in the roofing slate deposits. Since I had completely lost my library during my time in Hamburg—and as I currently lack access to a nearby geological institute—identifying the animal proved difficult at first. Several geologists and zoologists to whom I presented a sketch of the fossil were unfamiliar with this particular animal form. It was only through a suggestion from Dr. Doreck-Sieverts (Stuttgart-Degerloch) that my attention was drawn to the genus *Pyrgocystis* Bather.

I would like to take this opportunity to thank Prof. Pfannenstiel (Freiburg im Breisgau) for his valuable suggestions, as well as Dr. Doreck-Sieverts for her active support.

*Pyrgocystis coronaeformis* n. sp.

Pl. 2, Fig. 1–4

Diagnosis: A *Pyrgocystis* specimen measuring 95 mm in overall length, featuring a scaly column (stem) approximately 13 mm in diameter, which transitions at its lower end into a rounded, sac-like structure measuring 27 mm in height and approximately 17 mm in diameter.

**11**

At its upper end, the column supports a crown-shaped theca, from which the ambulacra—formed by five triangular plates—stand out in relief.

Holotype (and sole specimen): The fossil illustrated in Pl. 2, Figs. 1–4; Rievers Collection, Enkirch (Moselle).

Locality: Hunsrück region; Bundenbach near Kirn (Nahe).

Stratum: Lower Devonian; Hunsrück Slate; Roofing Slate facies.

Associated Fauna: Shallow-marine.

Preservation: The animal's remains were completely transformed into pyrite due to the pressure of overlying sediment. During excavation, the left side of the "tower" section was damaged at approximately three-quarters of its height.

Description: The root-like portion of the fossil—which is rounded at the base—has a sac-like appearance; it stands 27 mm tall and has an average width of 17 mm. Due to lateral compression caused by pressure, the actual diameter of the originally rounded animal was likely smaller at this point. Individual small, rounded calcareous platelets and rod-like granules are embedded within the leathery integument, creating a texture of nodules and striations that renders the surface partly granular and partly striated in appearance (Pl. 2, Fig. 4). In the lower half, the punctate pattern predominates, whereas in the upper half, the striation—which generally follows a vertical orientation—is dominant. This arrangement appears to have facilitated the animal's burrowing into the mud (or fine sand) and enhanced its structural stability against the pressure of the surrounding sediment. The upper extremity of the root-like section forms a slightly raised rim, marking the level at which the animal was embedded within the substrate. From this terminal rim of the root—serving, as it were, as a pedestal—the central section of the animal (the "tower") rises with a slight offset, thereby creating a small ledge. This "tower"—measuring 13 mm in width and 50 mm in height—is armored at its upper extremity with scale-like calcareous plates. Over the greater part of its length (approximately 35 mm), it maintains a width of 13 mm—a dimension likely somewhat exaggerated by the effects of compression. The lower part conforms with a gentle curvature to the larger diameter of the root, while the upper part widens slightly in a conical shape, forming a rim—or, to maintain the tower metaphor, a cornice—into which the tower cap is fitted as if into a cuff (Pl. 2, Figs. 2–3).
