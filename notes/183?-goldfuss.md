DIVISION TWO.

REMAINS OF RADIATES.

RADIATE ANIMALS OF PREHISTORIC TIMES.

I. ECHINIDES. SEA URCHINS.

A. Free body, not pedunculated.

The body is free, not attached by a stalk.

The shell of echinoids of all genera, with the exception of the penultimate one, consists of twenty rows of small, mostly pentagonal plates (ossicles), which are of equal size in pairs, lie next to one another with their corners interlocking, and thus form ten fields (areas).

Five broader fields (Areae maiores) and five narrower fields (Areae ambulacrorum) are distinguished. The latter consist of a larger number of smaller plates, and are perforated on both sides with a double or single row of small holes, which are opposite each other in pairs, either connected by an indented transverse line (Pori coniugati), or without this connection (Pori seiuncti).

The rows of these holes are called ambulacra. They run from the apex to the mouth, and are either visible throughout their entire length (Ambulacra completa), or so small at the edges of the dorsal side that they are invisible to the naked eye (Ambulacra incompleta), and can only be perceived through magnification and on fossil casts. There are always ten ambulacra present, and therefore only five have been erroneously counted when they lie so close together in pairs that the space between them forms a narrow line, or only four when the pores of a pair are not visible due to their small size.

II. STELLERIDES. STARFISH.

A. Stilasteritae.

Stalked Starfish.

The body of these animals (calyx) is conical and firmly rooted to the seabed by means of an articulated stalk. It consists of several rows of segments or plates, which lie on top of each other in greater or lesser numbers in circles. It has a proboscis-like mouth at the apex and arms radiating from the circumference.

The stalk or column (Columna peduncularis) consists of pentagonal or cylindrical segments, which articulate with each other by means of joint surfaces. These segments are called trochites, wheel stones, column stones, lily stones, star stones, and screw stones, and pieces of the column consisting of several connected segments have been given the name entrochites. At the lower end, the column is firmly attached to the seabed by root-like processes, is pierced in the center by a nutritional canal (Canalis alimentarius), and is often covered with auxiliary arms (Brachia auxiliaria).

The lowest plates or segments of the body, whose base is attached to the column, form the pelvis. The plates of the next circle of segments are called costal plates, and the pieces inserted between them, with varying regularity, are called intercostal plates. The segments of the uppermost circle from which an arm originates are called scapulae, the pieces lying between them are called interscapular plates, and the elongated pieces located between two pairs of arms have been called clavicles. The articulated arms divide into hands (Manus), fingers (Digiti), and tentacles (Tentacula). The plates located between the shoulder blades are referred to as breastplates (pectorales), and those covering the crown of the head as cranial plates (assulae verticis). The rest of the body cavity, as well as the grooves of the arms and hands, was covered by skin studded with small, calcareous plates.

B. Free Starfish,

Free-living starfish.

The body of these animals is not attached by a stalk, but is free, flattened, with protruding corners or elongated rays at the edge, which correspond to the arms of the crinoids. The body and rays, as in the latter, consist of a skeleton of articulated, calcareous segments. The skeleton of the central part is formed from a ring of rib segments and intercostal segments, to which the clavicle and shoulder segments are attached, which bear simple, long or short arms and dichotomously branched hands, often equipped with fingers and tentacles. The pelvic segments are either entirely absent, and the body is closed in their place only by the general, membranous covering or by a covering paved with calcareous plates (ossicles), or the skin plates are joined together in a pelvis-like manner, or a real pelvis is present.

The arm rays are also covered with a skin, which is often covered with plates, or with half-rings and scales. Their skeleton consists of similar segments as in the crinoids, which also form a longitudinal groove on one side. This groove is either open and radiates from the mouth, or is only open as far as the disc of the body extends, and in its further course is covered with the skin and its scales. From these grooves, the living animal extends fleshy tube feet, while calcareous tentacles are articulated at the edges.

The mouth opening of the starfish is pentagonal, and the tentacle-bearing grooves radiate from its corners. In most starfish, this is the lower side of the body, as they move with the help of their tentacles; In the next genus, however, the mouth is directed upwards, as in the stalked starfish. In all starfish, we call the side on which the mouth is located the ventral surface (superficies abdominalis), and the opposite side the dorsal surface (superficies dorsalis).
