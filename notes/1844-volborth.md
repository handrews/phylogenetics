12. ON THE ARMS OF THE ECHINO-ENCRINITES, HITHERTO CLASSIFIED AMONG THE ARMLESS CRINOIDS; by Dr. Alex. VOLBORTH. (Read April 5, 1844).

(With one plate.)

In my treatise on the Echino-Encrinites (1), I suggested that the five depressions surrounding the mouth opening might be articulation surfaces of soft tentacles that had once been present.

However, given the great delicacy of these parts, it was hardly to be expected that they would ever actually be found; my surprise was therefore all the greater when, upon closer examination of some fossils collected last autumn in Pavlovsk, I came across a specimen with well-preserved, clearly articulated arms.

On the accompanying plate, I have had the entire calyx with the arms depicted in natural size and, next to it, enlarged. Figs. 2 and 1. Even with the naked eye, the boundaries of the individual, almost half a line high, horn-like segments can be seen.

Viewed under a magnifying glass, each individual segment appears, on the slightly notched dorsal surface, to be divided into two lateral halves by a suture running in a vertical direction. Fig. 10. These halves, which seemingly belong to one and the same segment, are, however, slightly shifted against each other in a perpendicular direction, so that the horizontal surface of one half projects over that of the other. Here, too, the segments form alternating rows lying next to each other, as in the fossil genera Encrinus, Platycrinus, Actinocrinus, and Dimerocrinus, which interlock in a zigzag pattern in the middle. The zigzag suture is, however, only clearly visible on the lowest segments, those closest to the mouth; further up, it assumes an increasingly straighter, and finally a completely straight direction. It then sends out horizontal, but always alternating, branches to both sides.

J. Müller (2) very ingeniously explains this alternating two-row arrangement, or distichy, of the arm segments as resulting from the progressive shortening of originally wedge-shaped segments, which, however, no longer reach the opposite side, so that segments come to rest upon one another that should be separated by an entire segment. He refers in this context to the comatulids, in which the segments are often lower on one side than on the other, so that the segments lie alternately in a wedge-shaped manner upon one another.

The dorsal surface of the arms of these Echino-Encrinids is further distinguished by special processes, each half of the segment having one on its aboral side (Fig. 1, a). If one imagines the two halves of the segments connected by elastic interarticular substance and muscle fibers between the opposing dorsal processes, this would allow for the lateral opening of the ventral groove of the arms; a characteristic that would, however, be entirely unique to the Echino-Encrinids, since in all other living and fossil crinoids only flexion and extension of the arms by muscles is assumed.

The ventral groove of the arms is covered on both sides with alternating, curly tentacles arranged close together, with 2 to 3 on each half of the limb. Fig. 9, a. Pinnules are not noticeable, nor is a bifurcation of the arms.

Regarding the number of arms, E. E. granatum appears to have 6, as can be seen in Fig. 1. However, this does not correspond to the 5 tentacle grooves, which both E. E. granatum and Angulosus clearly show on their small ventral disc. Figs. 3 and 4. To resolve this difficulty, two possibilities can be considered: either the sixth arm is merely a fragment of the 5 original arms, or there were 10 arms, of which 4 have been lost; regardless of whether these arms were directly attached in pairs to the 5 depressions of the apical plates or supported by 5 segmented arm stems. However, since nothing is noticeable of the segments of the arm stems, which must have been larger than the arm segments, and 10 arms would hardly fit on the small disc, I believe I am justified in assuming the first case to be the correct one.

But how do the arms of the other species of this genus behave? E. E. angulosus differs from granatum only in the smaller number of pore rhomboids; its apex is entirely identical to that of granatum, it shows the same 5 tentacle grooves and must therefore have had the same number of arms.

With E. E. striatus, however, the situation is quite different. It had long struck me that this species almost never occurs with a well-preserved apex; This is likely due to the fact that the apical plates here are almost twice as long and proportionally narrower, making the proboscis-like apex much more delicate and fragile. The few individuals that are better preserved in this respect only show the rather large central mouth opening, but none of the 5 tentacle grooves or depressions that characterize the other two species.

Here, a collection of discarded fragments destined for disposal proved helpful. I was fortunate enough to find a few apical plates with arm segments still attached, extracted from the surrounding rock, which provided proof that E. E. striatus had the fewest arms of all crinoids; namely, only two.

Figure 6 shows such an apical plate with some attached arm segments. Each arm rests on a radial axillary and also slightly on the two adjacent interaxillaries. The space is so completely closed all around that there would be absolutely no room for a third arm. The arm segments, Figures 8, 9, and 10, are naturally halved and alternating, clearly show the zigzag suture, are generally much larger than those previously described, and also differ from them in their more bony structure. There is no trace of arm stems here; nor are there any indications of pinnules or bifurcation. However, the intricately folded tentacles on the ventral side are already visible to the naked eye (Figure 8).

Now that arms have been demonstrated in the entire genus, all Echino-Encrinidae must henceforth leave the division of armless crinoids and take their own place among the Tesselata of the stalked, armed crinoids. The characteristics of the genus would now have to be modified as follows:

All Echino-Encrinids have separate, rather widely spaced oral and anal openings.

Apart from the characteristic striation of the surface, their calyx is distinguished by special pore-rhomboids confined to specific areas. These pore-rhomboids are present in every individual; they are essential components of their organization and number at least three. The individual pores open into straight channels that penetrate deeply into the interior.

Four basal plates enclose the round stem in a square opening between them. Alternating with these are two circles of parabasal plates (3), between which the round, relatively large, anal opening is located. Each circle of parabasal plates consists of 5 ossicles. The calyx is finally closed by 5 smaller apical ossicles (radialia axillaria) that alternate with the parabasal plates; on their ventral parts, which are fused into a small disc, the free arms surround the central mouth opening. The arm segments are alternately arranged in two rows, equipped with tentacles on their ventral groove, and show neither pinnules nor bifurcation.

Regarding the species of this genus, their characterization is significantly aided by the varying number of arms. Even though the arms are only rarely found, the nature of the apical ossicles and the absence or presence of the 5 tentacle grooves provide an important clue for distinguishing E. E. striatus from the other species. The former is also distinguished by a particularly strong protrusion of the anal region beyond the calyx periphery, as can be seen in Fig. 5, n. The 3 ossicles of the first parabasal circle are therefore insufficient to close the greatly enlarged space, and one of the basal ossicles is considerably elongated upwards to facilitate this closure. For this reason, the basal plates of E. E. striatus do not form a pentagon, as in the other species, but the figure more closely resembles a hexagon, as in Hemicosmites. Individuals of this species are not infrequently found in which the fissuring of the ossicles is very clearly visible because they lack the confused striation; This is by no means a consequence of rolling in the water, otherwise the centers of the woodlice, which protrude the most and form the corners, would have suffered the most; but this is absolutely not the case.

While the other species always exhibit the gray color characteristic of Silurian fossil rings, E. E. striatus is always yellowish-red. Perhaps it therefore belongs to Devonian strata, which I believe I am all the more justified in assuming in the vicinity of Pavlovsk, since I also possess individual fish scales characteristic of this system from the same area.

I am now all the more willing to accept the distinction I previously made (4) based on the number of parabasal plates contributing to the formation of the anus, since my collection, now three times larger, shows some inconsistencies in this regard. While I can confirm that in E. E. striatus only 3 parabasal plates (2 lower and one upper) always contribute to the formation of the anus, the other species show deviations from the stated norm. In U. u/zgu/o5ns, in particular, 75% are normal, while the remaining 25% are abnormally formed.

The discovery of the arms is of the utmost importance not only for the Echino-Encrinites but also for all other armless crinoids. On all the specimens of this division found in the St. Petersburg hills, articulation surfaces for arms and tentacle grooves can be detected on the apex, from which one can conclude with certainty not only the presence but even the number of arms belonging to each genus. Among all of them, Sphaeronites pomum stands out in this respect, of which we now owe such a beautiful illustration to His Imperial Highness the Duke of Leuchtenberg (5). The five grooves extending over a third of the sphere and converging at the mouth are the tentacle grooves, and the wart-like, strong elevations from which they originate were the points of attachment of the arms; their number, therefore, in this genus, must have been more than thirty.
