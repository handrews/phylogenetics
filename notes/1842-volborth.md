On the Echino-Encrinites and the
Identity of the Contractile Part of their
Stem with Cornulites Serpularius;
by Dr. A. Volborth. (Read March 18, 1842.)
(Two plates attached)

The discovery of a complete articulated stem of Echinosphaerites angulosa Pand., about which I had the honor of presenting a preliminary report to the Academy, prompted me to subject this genus to a thorough examination; a task I felt all the more compelled to undertake as my collection offered me some fifty specimens of it. I soon came to the conviction that not only Echino-Encrinites Herm. von Meyer's 1)

and Echinosphaerites granatum Schlottheim's 2),

2) Isis 1826. Heft III. pag. 311. Tab. I fig. 1

but most likely also Sphaeronites granatum and testudinarius Hisinger's 3),

3) Leth Suecic pag. 92.

are identical with our genus from Pavlovsk, and that the many descriptions of these animals, given under various names, are all based on incomplete specimens; therefore, a revision and careful critical comparison of them became necessary. Among the many names available for this genus, I believed I had to give preference to Meyer's; not only because of the priority of his description and illustration, based on a correct analysis of the ossicles, but also because this genus, due to its articulated stem, is the closest to the true Encrinus among all armless crinoids.

Schlotheim and Hermann von Meyer are the only ones who correctly describe the boundaries of the plates, because they most likely had rolled specimens before them, where the surface striation was worn away, and therefore the separation of the plates was clearly visible.

¶

I now also have several specimens that clearly show this original segmentation of the ossicles 10), and through careful comparison I have succeeded in discovering a very simple law by which the original form of the ossicles can be determined with mathematical certainty in any calyx, however complex it may appear.

10) Tab. I fig 10 u. Tab II fig- 6.

If one examines this striped pattern carefully, one easily finds that between every three projecting corners of the calyx, triangles are enclosed, which in turn enclose smaller triangles. (Table I, figures 8 and 12.) (From these, Hisinger constructs the four-sided valves in his Echinus novus by connecting two triangles each). To obtain the true nature of the ossicles, one only needs to find the smallest of these nested triangles and draw perpendicular lines from its center to its three sides; this gives the natural boundary of the ossicles. These smallest triangles form the points where three ossicles touch or meet. A glance at figures 1, 2, and 3 of the first plate will make this clearer. The same network of triangles forms the basis of all three figures. Figure 2 shows the four-sided valves of Echinus novus His. 11), 

11) Vetensk. Acad. Hand]. 1802 Tab VII fig. b.

figure 1 shows the hexagonal stars of Sphaeronites testudinarius 12),

12) Leth. Suec.

and figure 3 shows the actual boundaries of the ossicles as determined by my principle. This law is also confirmed by the calyces of most of the arm-bearing crinoids. I therefore refer in particular to Cyathocrinus geometricus (Goldfuss Table LVIII figure 5), to Marsupites ornatus (Schlotheim Supplement Table XXIX figure 1 a. and Bronn Leth. Table XXIX figure 13), and to Cyathocrinus rugosus. (Murchison Sil. syst. Table XVIII fig. 1.) In all of them, the stripes of their trilobites form triangles in the centers of which three trilobites meet.

All the corners, therefore, which are noticeable on the calyx of the Echino-Encrinites, correspond to the centers of the individual, mostly six-sided plates, which consequently appear more strongly raised in their center than is the case in any other genus. From these centers of the individual plates, the finer and coarser striations, which are further crisscrossed by transverse lines, extend to the edge of the plates, where they unite with those coming from the neighboring plates, thereby completely concealing the actual boundary of the plates. If one focuses one's gaze on only one corner of the calyx, it appears surrounded by narrow rhombuses that unite to form stars (Table I, Fig. 1); if, however, one observes three such corners simultaneously, the aforementioned characteristic triangles become more clearly visible (Table I, Fig. 2), from which the actual shape of the plates (Table I, Fig. 3) can easily be determined according to the given rule.

Mr. von Buch 13) puts forward the opinion that these striations are similar to the tentacle canals that Pander demonstrated in Sphaeronites aurantium.

13) Beiträge zur Bestimmung der Gebirgsformationen in Russland. 1840. pag. 32.

Although I do not know the purpose of these striations, I cannot agree with Mr. von Buch's opinion. Firstly, these striations contain absolutely no pores through which tentacles could have emerged, and Pander himself cites Echmosphaerites angulosa et striata as genera without pores. Secondly, these striations are latticed along their entire length by small transverse lines, and thirdly, I have found special plates with pores in this genus, whose tentacle canals are located above these striations, whereby the striation itself is completely concealed in these areas, but the plates appear thickened. — However, if specific regions of the calyx are designated for pores and sensory canals, then the remaining striations must necessarily have served other purposes.

¶

In this genus, the calyx is formed from four plates joined together to form a nonagon. This nonagon, however, reduces without difficulty to a pentagon, as shown in figures 5 and 6 of the first plate. If one imagines the lines of contact of the calyx plates extended through the center of the stem opening (as in Fig. 4), three of them appear as trapezoids and the fourth as a pentagon. A very similar calyx is also found in Melocrinus hieroglyphicus and laevis (Goldf. Tab. LX, figs. 1 and 2), but with the difference that this crinoid has a round opening for the attachment of the stem, while in Echino-Encrinus it is sharply quadrangular (Tab. I, fig. 11y. Also in Schlotheim, see Isis 1826, Tab. I, fig. 1c). In Hemicosmites, according to Mr. von Buch, the calyx also consists of four plates; however, two of these are trapezoids and two are pentagons, which sufficiently distinguishes it from Echino-Encrinus.

¶

The four basal plates are followed by two rows of mostly hexagonal radial plates, Table L, a and b. They alternate both among themselves and with the basal and apical plates. Only the pentagonal basal plate is an exception, as it directly bears a radial plate. Between these two rows of radial plates, slightly to the left of the pentagonal basal plate, is always the relatively large anal opening x, enclosed by three or four cones, which border it with their bases. These cones arise from the gradual widening of a groove from the center of the plate to the edge of the anus. They are all the more conspicuous because they are smooth and without striations. Each cone corresponds to a particular anal plate, and the number of cones already allows us to determine whether the anus is formed by three or four plates, which provides an important clue for distinguishing the species of this genus. In Echmo-Encrinus angulosus m. (Echinosphaer. angulosa Pand.), the anus is formed by four plates, Table 1, fig. 9, while in Striatus only three plates contribute to it, Table II, fig. 1. — Since the anal opening is very large, I believe that it was internally divided and also contained the ducts for eggs and sperm; just as Müller demonstrated this for the Pentatremites, which he also classifies among the armless crinoids. 14)

14) Jahresbericht der Berl. Acad. 1840. pag. 96.

¶

Five smaller apical ossicles surround the elongated mouth, which is directly opposite the stem opening. In its center, one observes the larger oval mouth opening and, in its vicinity, five to six smaller depressions, which may have been articulation surfaces for soft tentacles that were once present. (Table 1, fig. 7 z.)

¶

Regarding the sensory organs of this genus, several rhomboidal areas of pores are found, each bound to specific regions of the calyx, and connected along their longer diagonals by sensory canals. Two adjacent plates contribute a quarter of their surface to each such rhomboid, and the shorter diagonal of the rhomboid defines the suture between the plates. (Table I, figures 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12). They are analogous to Pander's sensory canals in Sphaeronites aurantium; however, they differ in that they extend to the tips of the plates and are limited to only a few plates, while in Sphaeronites aurantium all plates are provided with them. Such a rhomboidal pore area is depicted by Schlotheim and Meyer (loc. cit.) at the oral margin. The former considers it to be the mouth opening itself, while Meyer interprets these pores as respiratory or reproductive organs. Remarkably, neither Pander nor Hisinger mentions these organs, and Eichwald also seems not to have noticed them. I have consistently found them in all specimens without exception. In Echino-Encrinus angulosus et striatus m. (Echinosphaer. ang. et striat. Pand.), three such rhomboidal pore areas are always present. One, located between the mouth and anus, slightly to the right of the latter, is formed by the largest apical plate and a rib plate of the second row (Table I, fig. 9, d); the other two, on the opposite side near the stem, are formed by the basal plate opposite the pentagonal plate and two rib plates of the first row (Table I, d, figures 4, 5, 8 and 11).

¶

Finally, a third species of this genus even has 5 such pore rhombuses (Table I, figs. 6 and 10). These could therefore probably be compared to the five ambulacral fields of the Echinaceae, which would justify the name Echino-Encrinus all the more.

¶

There can be no doubt that the genera of Schlotheim and Meyer are identical with our Pavlovskian genus. However, whether Hisinger's Sphaeron. granatum belongs here cannot yet be determined with certainty, despite the proven identical striation, because the closely spaced mouth and anal openings in the latter contradict this. 15)

15) Tab. I fig. 1, 2, 3.

One of these openings is situated between two ossicles, the other even pierces an ossicle, which would certainly be an abnormal condition and could raise doubts about the accuracy of the drawing; however, Hisinger's figure also lacks the pore rhomboids, and the entire figure is too uniformly round to be able to decide on this. We must therefore await more detailed reports from Sweden. Until then, however, the third species, provided with five pore rhomboids, may be called Echino-Encrinus granatum, since in this species the anal opening appears to be closest to the mouth. (Tab. I. x of figs. 6 and 10.) We would therefore have three species of Echino-Encrinus: Echino-Encrinus angulosus, striatus, and granatum. The striatus differs from the angulosus by finer striation, larger ossicles, and the fact that the anus is formed by three ossicles, while in the angulosus four ossicles contribute to it. The granatum, however, is distinguished from both by five pore rhomboids, since the angulosus and striatus only possess three of these.

¶

Before I proceed to describe the stem of this genus, it will not be superfluous to preface it with a brief critique of what we generally know about the stem of armless crinoids.

¶

Gyllenhahl 16), who first recognized the organic nature of these creatures, describes Echinus aurantium as a spherical body which, in addition to a separate mouth and anus, also had special rostra. 

16) Vetensk Acad Handl 1772, pag. 245.

Wahlenberg 17) was the first to suggest that these rostra might have served the function of stems.

17) Nova Acta Reg Soc. scient. Upsal. Vol. VIII, pag. 52. Er sagt: "Rostra eorum duo vicibus stipitis quodammodo functa fuisse videntur in globo alendo vel saltim construendo."

We now know that the rostrum inferius corresponds to the mouth opening; however, whether the rostrum superius served these animals to attach themselves directly to foreign bodies, or whether the attachment was mediated indirectly by a special articulated stem, remains completely unknown. Therefore, Hisinger also states in his characterization of the Sphaeronites: "Corpus globosum, liberum vel stipite affixum." Indeed, we find contradictions regarding one and the same genus. For example, Hisinger 18) depicts Sphaeronites aurantium with broad rings of an articulated crinoid stem. But the rostrum of the cross-section given in Fig. 8b does not seem to match the Entrochus of Fig. 8a, and in the note on page 91 he refers to the genera of Schlotheim and Meyer, which clearly do not belong here.

18) Leth. suec. Tab. XXV. fig. 8. a

L. von Buch 19) believes (p. 29) that the regular pentagon of the always extremely thin stem of this genus is only very rarely recognizable.

19) loc. cit.

Eichwald's 20) leathery-fleshy ascidian stalks of Sphaeronites aurantium and pomum must apparently be interpreted as Gyllenhaal's rostra (proboscis- or snout-like extensions of the animal itself), rather than as true stalks.

20) loc. cit. pag. 74 et 75.

I have examined hundreds of specimens of Sphaeronites aurantium without ever finding either one or the other.

¶

We also don't know much more about the stem of Hemicosmites and Cryptocrinites; for the remnants depicted in von Buch's contributions, Table I, figures 7, 8, and 9, are too meager to provide sufficient information. Pander, to whom the credit is due for having contributed significantly to a more precise understanding of the armless crinoids, both through the examination of known species and the establishment of new genera, is also the first to describe and illustrate clearly articulated stem remnants on his Echinosphaerites angulatus et striata. 21)

21) Pander’s Beiträge zur Geognosie des russ. Reichs. Tab. Il fig. 27 et 32

¶

This same genus also includes the complete stem of Encrinus angulosus, which I found last summer near Pavlovsk. It can be seen in natural size in Plate II, figure 4, and enlarged in figure 5.  In terms of structure and texture, it consists of two different parts. One part, closest to the calyx, which I call the neck, consists in this specimen of about twelve rings, which decrease in size quite rapidly from the point of insertion downwards, so that the next ring seems to fit into the preceding one to about half its length. These rings may have been connected in the living state by muscle fibers and tendinous membranes, which would have allowed the neck to be extended or shortened to a certain degree.

¶

This neck is immediately followed by the actual stalk, which consists of narrow cylinders that gradually decrease in size.  Due to its denser texture and simpler arrangement of its parts, the stalk also indicates greater rigidity and strength, serving as the means of attachment to foreign bodies or the ground. Both the neck and the stalk itself are perforated by a round nutritional canal, which is relatively wide in the neck (Table II, Fig. 11) but becomes very narrow and constricted in the stalk.

¶

The neck, which was evidently very elastic due to the characteristics described, allowed the animal, by relaxing it, to turn the mouth opening downwards or, by contracting it again, to straighten it and move it in all directions to search for its prey in the surrounding water. Upon death, relaxation of the neck must have occurred, and therefore, in our specimen, the animal's mouth opening is turned downwards; the neck appears bent in a crescent shape.

¶

This greater mobility and flexibility of the neck is probably also the reason why it frequently detached after death, and why animals with attached fragments of it (Tables I and II) are among the greatest rarities.

¶

On the other hand, the broken-off neck pieces, usually curved like horns due to the aforementioned cause, also had to be found, and indeed, these isolated parts have caused considerable perplexity among paleontologists of recent times. Schlotheim first described them from the transitional limestone of Gotland as Cornulites serpularius (Petrefactenkunde 1820, p. 378, Table XXIX, fig. 7) and suggested that this creature perhaps belonged to a peculiar species of Serpula. Hisinger, in his geognostic description of Gotland (Vetenskaps Acad. Handl. 1826, p. 324), also mentions Cornulites serpularius, belonging to the snail family, although, as he adds, it is not yet known with certainty whether it possesses septa, which the outer surface of the rings seems to indicate. Murchison 22) describes the same enigmatic bodies from English Silurian strata; but frankly admits that he does not know what to make of them. Finally, Eichwald assigns the Cornulites their place between the armed and armless crinoids and lists four different species of them.

22) Silur. System Tab. XXVI fig. 5 bis 9. Pag. 627 sagt er:
"We can scarcely attempt a description of this anomalous fossil,
of which at present but one species is known. So unlike is it to
anything we have seen, that we are unable to assign it a place
in the system of animals, or draw a comparison between it and
any other creature."


He says of them very aptly, loco cit., p. 63: "that these internally hollow, segmented tubes could probably extend and lengthen like a telescope and then retract again, which would certainly be a striking structure, such as we have not yet observed in the auxiliary arms of the crinoids." Immediately afterwards, he describes his Gonocrinites without suspecting their close relationship to the Cornulites, and mentions the screw-shaped (?) stem remnants of the former.

¶

On Table II, figures 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 15, I have had several specimens of so-called Cornulites from my collection illustrated, showing various stages between expansion and contraction and of different sizes. In one of the specimens that has been cut in half (fig. 11), the unchambered lumen of the digestive tract is also visible. A comparison with figures 4 and 5 of the same plate will, I hope, sufficiently demonstrate the identity of the Cornulites with the neck of our Echino-Encrinus stem, thus reducing the already extensive list of fossil names by one.

¶

It struck me as remarkable that, given the otherwise great similarity between the Silurian fauna of England and that of Russia and Sweden, no armless crinoids had yet been found in England. However, the presence of the so-called Cornulites now makes it highly probable that our Echino-Encrinus will soon be discovered there. Indeed, I believe I can already demonstrate their presence, for on Plate XXVI, figure 5a of Murchison's work, a part of a segment of *Echino-Encrinus striatus* is very clearly depicted alongside a Cornulite.
