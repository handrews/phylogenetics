They are distinguished by their unique organs of movement. Their outer covering is perforated by a large number of small holes arranged in very regular series, through which pass cylindrical membranous tentacles, each terminating in a small disc that acts as a suction cup. The part of these tentacles that remains inside the body is vesicular; a fluid is released into their entire cavity and, at the animal's will, flows into the outer cylindrical part, extending it, or it retracts into the inner vesicular part, and then the outer part collapses. It is by thus lengthening or shortening their hundreds of small feet or tentacles, and by attaching them with the suction cups at their ends, that these animals perform their progressive movements. Vessels originating from these small feet lead to trunks that correspond to their rows and terminate near the mouth. They form a system distinct from that of the intestinal vessels observed in some species.

Linnaeus describes three very natural, but quite numerous, genera, comprising species varied enough to be considered three families.

The Asterias (Asterias. L.), commonly known as starfish.
FIRST ORDER OF ECHINODERMS. THE PEDICELLATES.

In the true Asterias (Asterias. Lam.), each ray has a longitudinal groove on its underside, in which all the small holes that allow the feet to pass through are located.

In others, the sides are concave, which begins to give them a star-like shape (1).

The majority have their rays separated by well-marked re-entrant angles.
Such are:

The common or reddish starfish (Ast. rubens. L.) Encycl. CXIII, 1,2, which is extremely common on all our coasts, to the point that it is used in some places to fertilize the soil.

The glacial starfish (Ast. glacialis. L.) Link. XXXVIII 69; Encycl. CXII, often has a diameter of more than a foot. The spines covering the upper part of its body are surrounded by a multitude of small fleshy tubes, which form cushion-like structures around their bases.

The Orange Starfish (Ast. aurantiaca. L.) Link. VI, VII, XXIII; Encycl. CX, is our largest species; the edges of its arms are lined with pavement-like pieces, on which strong, mobile spines articulate. The entire upper surface is covered with other small spines ending in truncated and bristly heads (2).
Some have more than five rays (3).

It has been necessary to separate from the other starfish those species where the rays do not have a longitudinal groove underneath to house the tube feet; generally, these rays are not hollow, and the stomach does not extend into them as ceca, but its appendages remain in the spaces between them.

M. de Lamarck calls Ophiuroids those that have five unbranched rays around a central disc; but we must also distinguish:

Those where these rays are furnished on each side with mobile spines; the small fleshy feet also emerge from each side between the bases of these spines (1).

And those where, having no lateral spines, but being covered with imbricated scales, these rays resemble snakes' tails. The central disc has, in each interval between the rays, four holes that penetrate the interior and probably serve for respiration. There are feet only in five short grooves, which form a star around the mouth (2).

M. de Lamarck calls Euryales those where the rays divide dichotomously. Those where the division begins at the base of the rays present the appearance of a bundle of snakes; they have been commonly called Medusa's heads (3).

But there are also some where the division begins at the end of the ray and is repeated only a few times (1).

Finally, M. de Lamarck calls Comatulids those that have two rows one above the other, both numerous; one of large, simple rays, furnished on their sides with mobile spines; the other, of small rays without spines around the mouth (2).

The Encrinites. (Encrinus.)

These could be defined as eurial organisms borne on a stem, divided into a large number of articulations. Their branches themselves are articulated and dichotomously divided into branchlets, and the stem sometimes bears them at various heights. There are both living and fossil species, and the fossil remains known as entrochites appear to be pieces of the stem and branches of beings analogous to encrinites.
See Guettard, Académie des Sciences, 1755, p. 224.

The Sea Urchins. (Echinus. L.), commonly called Sea Hedgehogs.

They have a body covered with a test or calcareous shell, composed of angular pieces that join together precisely, and pierced with several very regular rows of countless small holes, through which the membranous feet pass. The surface of this shell is armed with spines articulated on small tubercles, which serve for their movements, jointly with the feet that are situated between them. The mouth is furnished with five teeth embedded in a very complicated calcareous structure, resembling a five-sided lantern, furnished with various muscles, and suspended in a large opening of the test. These teeth, in the form of long ribbons, harden towards their root as they wear down at their tip (i). The intestine is very long, and attached spirally to the inner walls of the test by a mesentery. A double vascular system runs along this canal and extends partly over the mesentery. Five ovaries situated around the anus each discharge through a particular orifice; they form the edible part of these animals.

Sea urchins feed mainly on small shellfish,
which they seize with their feet. Their movements are very slow. The inside of their shell is always full of salt water. Sea urchin tests have been preserved in very large numbers in ancient strata, mainly in chalk deposits, where they are usually filled with flint.

Have their mouth in the middle of their lower surface and the anus directly opposite. The small pores are arranged in ten bands, grouped in pairs, which extend regularly from the mouth to the anus, like meridians on a globe. Almost all are spherical.

Some species have large, thick spines of very diverse shapes, borne on large tubercles of their test, and whose bases are surrounded by other smaller spines (1).

It is among these species that, as Messrs. Deluc discovered, those whose olive-shaped spines are quite often found petrified in chalk or other ancient formations, and have received the name of Jewish stones (2).
