March 16th. Meeting of the physical-mathematical class.

Mr. von Buch read about Sphaeronites and some other genera from which crinoids originate.

Few forms may have developed in the scope of a plan generally designed by nature, as varied and manifold as the magnificent lilies of the sea, the Encrinites or crinoids. From a simple beginning, they spread out in all directions, to wonderfully complex, many-limbed forms, and then suddenly contract again in the course of creation to a comparatively small number, so much so that of many genera and species of the past, only the solitary Penzacrinus remains in our present time period. Other forms, however, have developed and spread throughout all the seas. The crown of the lily has closed again, and completely enclosed asteroids and echinoids, capable of greater mobility and development, have taken the place of the crinoids.

Of these creatures of the past, no formation has yielded a greater number of diverse forms than the Transition Formation, from the oldest strata to the Carboniferous. Their distinguishing characteristic in this period lies in the fact that the parts enclosing the body still greatly predominate over the auxiliary limbs that serve to bring in food, over the widely spread, many-fingered arms. This body becomes ever smaller and consists of fewer parts in the Jurassic formation; the arms and fingers, on the other hand, are longer, more complex, and more numerous. With the Comatula or the Euryalids, the body completely separates from the stem, and in the Echinus and related genera, it no longer needs the crown. Before the sea lily opened and spread its arms, it moved on a short stem in its closed state in countless numbers, and only through frequent and very diverse attempts was this breaking open and unfolding achieved. These closed crinoids are still very little and only imperfectly known; however, they deserve attention in every respect. For no species of Encrinus has yet been found in deeper strata, and from them a continuous transition leads to the Pentacrinus of the present-day seas.

These forms have so far appeared almost exclusively in northern regions, in Sweden, in Norway, and in the hills surrounding St. Petersburg on the south side; and among them, the Sphaeronites are the most common.

They are large, round spheres, like oranges, with two poles at the ends. Linnaeus called them crystal apples in his Öland journey. Gyllenhaal, in an exemplary investigation and description (1772), was the first to recognize their organic nature and believed he could place them close to the Echinus, hence Wahlenberg called these bodies Echinosphaerites, a name that Hisinger replaced with the better one of Sphaeronites. These spheres are formed from many polyhedral plates, mostly hexagons, perhaps two hundred on a single specimen. At the top, a mouth opens, covered by a multitude of extremely small, movable shields. Below, a stem, made of thin, pentagonal segments, attaches the body to the ground. The plates are all perforated; In Sphaeronites Aurantium, these small pores are arranged in a row, extending from every angle of the polyhedron towards the center, but not quite reaching the center itself. Each of these pores is connected to the one on the adjacent plate by a deep groove, thus forming rhombuses that always extend across two plates or ossicles; sometimes so prominently that these rhombuses themselves have been mistaken for ossicles, leading to the erroneous classification of a species as Sphaeronites Granatum, because a resemblance was found between these rhombuses and the faces of a garnet crystal. However, Gyllenhaal had long since shown that the true polyhedral ossicles intersect the rhombuses along their shorter diagonal and at right angles to their striations; Pander, however, proves, what Gyllenhaal had not observed, that these striations or grooves, as in the ambulacra of the Cidaris species, connect two pores, or tentacle openings, with each other. Therefore, Zschadites Koenigii (Murchison Silurian Syst. T. 26. f. 11.) is still Sphaeronites Aurantium, in which the rhombuses have been given a boundary that does not belong to them and distorts the whole structure. This discovery by Pander of the tentacle canals, and therefore of the tentacles themselves, is important. They reappear on many crinoids, such as Aezocrinites, Rhodocrinites, and even Marsupites. Bronn Lethaea. T. IV. On the surface of Sphaeronites Pomum, the rhombuses are not visible. Each plate bears a number of small systems, separated from each other. Two pores are always connected to each other, but these systems are scattered without order over the entire surface.  This species has never been seen outside of Sweden.

In the upper half of the Sphaeronites, yet still a whole quarter of a sphere away from the mouth, there is a large pentagonal opening, which is closed by five triangular valves that rise in a flat pyramid. Gyllenhahl and his successors call this opening the mouth.  However, the analogy with related forms requires the mouth to be at the top, and an opening that closes from the outside seems ill-suited for a mouth that takes in food. It is probably an ovarian opening. Very close to the mouth at the top, and always to the right of the valve opening, there appears a third, very small, open opening that penetrates deep into the interior; probably an anus. A similar small anal opening is also found between three plates on Apiocrinites, where it had not previously been noticed, similar to the anus of the living Comatula. Gyllenhahl explicitly states that Sphaeronites Pomum is always found deeper in West Gothland than Sphaeronites Aurantium and in much larger quantities. Therefore, it is striking that nothing of it has yet been seen near St. Petersburg.

Hemicosmites pyriformis. With this beautiful and extremely graceful form, we take a big step closer to the true crinoids. Although still armless and closed, here there are already a few plates or ossicles in a specific number and regularly arranged. The Hemicosmites is shaped like an inverted pear and consists of three parts: pelvis, thorax, and apex. The pelvis, on the thin, pentagonal stem, consists of four pieces arranged in a hexagon. Two of them are pentagonal, the other two are elongated quadrilaterals. The thorax consists of six costal plates in two different groups. Three of these segments are narrower, and between the ones on the left, a pentagonal opening closed by five valves appears at the top, as in Sphaeronites. The other three segments are wider, and the upper point of the elongated hexagon is somewhat blunted. Accordingly, the apical segments, which arch over the entire structure, also divide into two groups; on the side of the wider segments, a long, seemingly inserted piece stands on each blunted point, resulting in three such pieces; they are absent on the side of the valved opening. The extremely small shields that cover the mouth at the apex appear to terminate in three small proboscis-like structures or arms, which are perforated and could easily be separate mouth openings. An anal opening is not visible. The great regularity of this arrangement is even more striking due to the great elegance with which tubercles are distributed in rows over each segment of the thorax and apex. On the rib segments, they extend from the middle to the upper corners of the hexagon, but none towards the lower corners. On the apical segments, however, these rows extend towards the lower corners, none towards the upper ones. Only half of the surfaces are decorated in this distinctive way. The apical and lateral rows thus combine to form a highly elegant wreath running around the entire figure. These tubercles are perforated in the center and appear to be attachment points for spines. The middle row of each segment is double. On the remaining part of the segment surface, only a few similar tubercles are scattered without any particular order.

Cryptocrinites regularis and C. Cerasus. (Pander t.II. f.24-26.)

The pelvis entirely belongs to Platycrinites, the thorax to Poteriocrinites. But the apex is still closed and armless. — Five ribs or rings appear, however, extending from the base to the apex, hidden beneath the ossicles, which are raised in a roof-like manner by them, exactly as observed in Actocrinites before the arms branch out. The nature of crinoids is therefore almost entirely present in Cryptocrinites, but it is still hidden internally. The pelvis consists of three plates joined to form a pentagon, an arrangement that reappears in Platycrinites, Rhodocrinites, and Actocrinites, but only in older forms, not in later Jurassic crinoids. The thorax is surrounded by five costal plates, and the apex also by five small plates that alternate with the costal plates. Very small plates surround the usually open mouth. Between the apex and the costal plates there is another significant opening covered by five valves. In Cryptocrinites Cerasus, intercostal plates are added to the original five of the thorax; this somewhat disrupts the regularity of the upper half, and more than five ossicles or segments may appear at the apex. The side on which the valvular opening is located is inflated in all specimens; the tendency of the hidden arms to break through the sides here is evident. The size of these animals rarely exceeds that of a pea; the stem that supports them has the thickness of a pin. They have so far been found only in the hills of St. Petersburg.
