Regarding Caryocrinus

In 1825, Thomas Say described a genus of crinoids different from all previously known, which had been discovered at Lockport on Lake Ontario. This description,

PAGE 121 (no ¶)

along with an illustration, was reprinted in the London Zoological Journal (October 1825). Later, Blainville (Actinologie, 253) and Mr. de Castelnau published illustrations, which, however, are inferior to those of Say. Many specimens in Berlin collections, however, allow for a more detailed examination of this remarkable form.

The entire animal is enclosed in a shell or calyx composed of plates or ossicles of very regular form, and this is attached to the substrate by a thin stem. At the base of this stem are four ossicles, which form the base of the calyx (pelvis); although of very unequal size, they are arranged so regularly that any two adjacent ones are completely identical. If the larger (pentagonal) ossicles in the middle are divided, the halves are indistinguishable from the smaller plates; the base would then be composed of six ossicles. However, that the larger ossicles are indeed composed of two smaller ones is most definitively proven by the distribution of the tentacle pores on their surfaces, a fusion that one might attribute to the particular expansion of the internal organs in this direction; and indeed, the mouth is located on the upper surface precisely in the direction of the division of the larger ossicles. This remarkable phenomenon is common to all crinoids. Where the basal ossicles are not entirely equal in form and arrangement, the mouth is certainly located on the side, and an interradial or intercostal plate leads up to it. In Actocrinites, the base of the calyx consists of three plates, two of which are considerably larger than the third. Precisely where the two larger ones meet, the interradial plate, so characteristic of Actocrinites, is situated, and precisely in this direction, on the apical side, the mouth is found. This is beautifully and clearly illustrated in Aczocrinites amphora in Portlock, Geology of Londonderry, pl. XV, fig. 4.  The very same relationship between the position of the mouth and the larger ossicles also appears in Pentacrinus, whose calyx base is likewise composed of three unequally sized plates. (See Mr. Müller, Pentacrinus, pl. VI.)

PAGE 122

Upon these four basal plates (of Caryocrinus) rise six lateral plates (cosiales), and the regular cylinder they enclose lends a particular elegance to the entire form, all the more so since their width and height maintain the pleasing ratio of 2:3. The plate supporting the mouth is a regular hexagon with the apex pointing upwards, as are the adjacent plates. However, two other plates, forming a triangle with the oral plate, have their upper apex truncated, forming a heptagon with a very small upper side, which again reveals a completely unexpected symmetry in the arrangement of these parts.

At the edge of the calyx, three double arms and three single arms arise. The former extend the calyx into a triangle, on one side of which lies the mouth, and on the other two sides, the truncated, heptagonal plates. These arms penetrate the center of six radial plates (radialia axillaria Müller); two very small intercalated pieces appear at the truncations of the heptagons. All these radial plates, however, terminate at the edge of the calyx. The very flat, dome-shaped calyx itself is formed by very small plates, a larger one in the center, six others surrounding it, and several smaller ones that completely fill the space up to the edge of the calyx.

Although the claims of the Caryocrinites to be classified among the Crinoids cannot be dismissed, and although they seem to resemble the Poteriocrinites in their cylindrical form, their upright lateral plates, and the radial plates alternating with these, a closer examination shows that they cannot be placed anywhere within the existing classification and that they stand entirely alone, without connection, at the beginning of the series by which the Crinoids separate themselves from the Cystoids.

The dominant influence of the number five, which runs in such a wonderful way throughout all of living nature, is particularly well-developed in crinoids, as in all radiolarians, and this number dictates the position that each individual part is to occupy.

PAGE 123 (no ¶)

Even though the various species of Actinocerinus or Pentacrinus may extend their arms in almost countless numbers, it is always merely a doubling of the five arms that develop from the interior of the calyx. Even though Platycrinus and Actinocrinus rise from only three basal plates, it is easy to show that two of them are fused together, and that here too the entire base of the calyx must be considered as composed of five plates.

This is not the case with Caryocrinus. Here, there is no trace, no hint, that would suggest a division into five. Everything, down to the smallest details, is determined and governed by the number six: a number that cannot be combined with five in any way. The base of the calyx consists of four unequal plates, which can easily be divided into six completely equal and similar plates. Six lateral plates, six arms—three double and three single—rise from its rim. All of this is completely foreign to other crinoids. A calyx base of four plates does not appear again, except for a single instance in the strangely shaped, aberrant Melocrinus (Goldfuss, pl. 60, fig. 1), and six arms emerging from the calyx have never been observed in other crinoids.

Everything determined by the number five develops deeply from within life itself. Everything governed by the number six is ​​a consequence of the surface, the external environment, which is not related to the internal organs in terms of development, but only in terms of protection. — If a scale begins to form on the excretory membrane of the surface, a point or a small knob, then, with continued excretion, this knob will multiply in all directions, thus assuming a circular shape. If these circles touch each other as they grow, they restrict each other, flatten out, and a six-sided scale is formed, which, due to the continuous restriction, can now only continue to grow in the six-sided form. Everything that now appears and forms on the surface—pores and stripes— must conform to this six-sided shape and is determined by it.  However, in the crinoids, the five arms, which spread far beyond the calyx,

PAGE 124 (no ¶)

develop from deep within the base, and now the determining influence of the number six is ​​limited solely to the ossicles—and not even entirely so. For even from the base of the calyx, before they break through, the arms compress the lateral ossicles, raise them in the middle, and give the entire calyx the very prominent form of a pentagon. Therefore, Mr. Müller aptly called these ossicles, which announce the emergence of the arms so long beforehand, radialia, and the one on which the arm actually rests, radial axillare. These arms carry with them the ovaries, previously hidden inside the calyx and so characteristic of all these animals. They are now attached far above the calyx to the pinnules of the arms (Müller, Pentacrinus, t. V. f. 17); they are free and no longer forced to squeeze through a narrow opening, and thus a new form of animal and the essential difference between crinoids and cystoids is established.

From Cystideans.

Cystideans are natural bodies that sit on a stalk that attaches them to the ground. Their more or less spherical surface is covered by a large number of interlocking polyhedral plates or ossicles. Between these plates are the openings necessary for the animal's life, but none from which arms could emerge. The animal is completely armless.

Regarding their surface, the following is common to all genera of Cystideans: 1) that their mouth is located precisely in the center of the apex, usually in a movable tube covered with small ossicles.

2) Next to this mouth, and only a short distance away, there usually appears, though not always, a smaller anal opening, which pierces the ossicles but is not surrounded by any distinctive ossicles.

3) Further towards the center, but still on the half facing the mouth, a five-sided, or less frequently six-sided, pyramid rises above a round or oval opening comparable in size to the mouth.

PAGE 125 (no ¶)

This pyramid appears to be composed of the same number of flaps. It probably covers the animal's ovarian opening.

These openings, apart from the mouth, are absent as soon as arms develop from the upper covering, and this is easily understood when one considers that the reproductive organs, the ovaries, protrude with the arms above the calyx. A separate ovarian opening in this casing would then be superfluous. Their presence is therefore a distinguishing characteristic of all genera of cystoids.

Since almost nothing of the animal's internal parts is visible, and only its external surroundings can be observed, it is not surprising that the number six predominates, and five appears only in very rare cases. However, it does appear in the stem and in the internal alimentary canal of this stem, which I have never seen as anything other than five-sided. The stem itself is remarkably thin in all genera and seems hardly suitable for supporting such a body as it is intended to bear. In Sphaeronites Aurantium, the diameter of the stem opening is only 1/46 of the calyx diameter. In Caryocystites Granatum Wahl., the calyx is still fifteen times thicker, and seventeen times thicker in Crypzocrinites Cerasus. — The St. Petersburg Academy, however, published a paper by Dr. Vollborth in its Bulletin of 1843 (Vol. X, No. 19), which would lead to a different result. According to him, the stems of the St. Petersburg Caryocystites would already reach a full third of the diameter of the shell at their point of attachment. These stems show absolutely nothing resembling an alimentary canal. Mr. Vollborth compares them, perhaps quite correctly, with the enigmatic Cornulites. But Murchison, to whom we owe the best illustrations of Cornulites, himself says that they must be considered parasitic animals, since they are almost always found attached to other shells. This might also be suspected of the St. Petersburg Cornulites. At least Mr. Vollborth has provided many illustrations in which the Cornulite is not attached to the lower side, but is clearly lodged in the mouth. This is also evident from their insertion, as they are embedded in the body of the cystids,

PAGE 126 (no ¶)

and no basal plates rise above their ends, as would surely be the case if they were parts of the body itself. Mr. Vollborth's opinion contradicts all analogy and even all observation, and requires at least a much more thorough investigation and examination than its originator has given it.

Spherical, on a very thin, round stalk with a pentagonal nutrient canal. Six small isopods form the base of the calyx. They are surrounded by other isopods, larger and smaller ones, which stand around in large numbers without any noticeable order, so that one can easily count twenty in a row upwards. Most are six-sided, but seven-sided, eight-sided, nine-sided, and even more can be found without difficulty. The mouth, a small tube covered with plates, is opposite the base of the stalk. Further down, still on the hemisphere of the mouth, appears the large ovarian opening, covered with five valves, and directly to the right of this, but very close to the mouth, is the small, round anal opening, without any elevation above the surface.

The surface of each trilobite segment is covered with lines and stripes that are perpendicular to the lateral edges of the segment; therefore, there are as many directions of stripes as the trilobite segment has lateral edges, and all of them appear to converge at the center. Pander depicted them very well, accurately, and clearly (pl. XXIX, fig. 3a). The stripes of one segment continue without interruption to the adjacent one, and both then appear to form a single whole. A rhombus is formed with strong stripes running in the direction of the longer diagonal. Therefore, these stripes can be described as rhombic stripes.

They terminate towards the center of the segment with an opening, which Pander considered to be the exit point of small antennae. I followed him in this (Beiträge zur Russischen Geologie, p. 27), and even expressed the conjecture that the stripes might delimit channels in which the antennae lie side by side, running parallel to each other from one segment to the next. However, when the outer stripes are worn away, as is usually the case with the sphaeronites found in their original location, and even when the separation of the segments can be clearly traced, no trace of openings through which the segment would be perforated is visible on the surface, which would be necessary if antennae were located at the ends of the stripes. The terminal openings are therefore only superficial, and they appear to be merely the openings of the cavity that runs through the entire stripe. — On the sphaeronites found near Christiania in Norway, the stripes are so prominent that the rhombuses they form are sharply delineated from one another and completely obscure the separation of the segments. In this form, they have often been described under the name Echinosphaerites Granatum. Hisinger illustrated them (Lethaea Suecica, pl. XXV, fig. 8).

The base of the calyx is four-parted, following the same pattern as in Caryocrinites. Specifically, there are two larger, unequal-sided pentagons next to each other, and two smaller quadrilaterals opposite them. If the pentagons are divided in the middle, six completely similar and equal quadrilaterals (rhombuses with one vertex pointing outwards) are obtained; therefore, the larger pentagons here are also probably formed from the fusion of two quadrilaterals. The ovarian opening is indeed located on the extension of the line separating the two larger basal plates. — Six lateral plates are situated on the sides of the basal hexagon. Six others alternate with these; and a third row is superimposed on this. A fourth similar row forms the apex. The ovarian opening between the third row and the apical plates is pentagonal, but rarely retains its covering. The mouth in the middle of the apex is always somewhat broadly compressed; the round anal opening is located to its side, and, as in Sphaeronites, also on the right side of the ovarian opening. — A distinguishing feature of this genus is that the plates are not only completely covered by the rhombic ridges, but are also so well concealed that they only become visible when the surface is deeply abraded and damaged. — This species has been found with certainty only at Bödahamn on Öland and at Wikarby and Furrudal in Dalecarlia. With closer examination, it would probably also be found among the forms from the hills of St. Petersburg.

The resemblance of this genus to Caryocrinites is undeniably surprising. However, the very distinct lack of arms sets it far apart from crinoids, and the large ovarian opening on the side closely connects it to the other genera of cystoids.

The base of the calyx consists of four plates, two pentagonal and two quadrilateral, which can be divided into six equal plates. Six large, long plates form the sides of the calyx, arranged so symmetrically that they divide the entire body into two unequal halves; in each half, the plates have their own distinct form. The three plates that rest on and between the pentagons of the base terminate at the top with the angle of the shorter sides of the hexagon, and two of them enclose the large ovarian opening, closed by five valves, in their upper half. In the opposite half, however, each lateral plate is truncated at the top, and the hexagon is modified into a heptagon. Six apical plates curve into a dome-like vault, and, tapering wedge-shaped, surround the mouth. Only on the side of the truncated plates are there three smaller, intercalated pieces resting on the truncation and extending up to the mouth.

The mouth rises in the center of the apex in a tube covered with very small plates. — All the plates are adorned with distinct concentric growth lines, but remarkably, there is no trace of rhombic striations.

Even more striking is the symmetrical and beautiful arrangement of the rows of sensory pores on the surfaces. From the center of the lateral plate, a double row of pores extends to the upper corner; a single row extends to each corner on the side. On the lower half of the plate, these pores are not visible, only scattered individual pores without any discernible pattern. The opposite is true on the plates of the apex. Here, only the lower half is adorned with pores, the upper half is not. Here, too, the central row of pores is double, while the lateral rows are single. The same arrangement is found on the surfaces of Caryocrinites, and, as in that genus, the rows of pores on the apex, but not those on the sides, are covered with small vesicles. — Found near Pulkovo, near St. Petersburg; Mr. Blasius found it on the Narva River, a few versts from Narva; Mr. Eichwald found it near Reval.

Of an almost round shape. At least the larger diameter is found in the lower half. Three plates surround the thin stem, two larger pentagonal ones, and one smaller rhomboidal one.  This exhibits the characteristic feature of cystoids, namely that the pentagonal plates, if divided in the middle, would separate into two plates exactly like the rhomboidal one. The cystoids undoubtedly belong to the oldest formations on the Earth's surface, the Silurian strata: the transitional formation. Nothing similar has yet been observed in more recent formations, and even less so in living organisms. The fact that they form the starting point of a whole series of radiolarians is strongly supported by this ancient and isolated occurrence, and the Caryocrinites surprisingly demonstrates how the transition from cystoids to crinoids was possible. — Once the arms have managed to break through, the diversity of forms within this group of animals increases enormously. It reached its peak in the Carboniferous limestone.

The rigid calyx, which in cystoids completely encloses and conceals the animal, recedes more and more, and in Pentacrinus forms little more than the base upon which the internal parts find a point of rest. In Jurassic

formations, the number of genera decreases rapidly, but the division into individual species is all the greater. Finally, in the upper Jurassic layers, the animal detaches itself from the stem that still attached it to the seabed; and in the form of Comatula, it is now able to move freely. Apiocrinites ellipticus is the only crinoid of the Cretaceous formation that can still be compared to older forms, and Pentacrinus caput medusae remains in our seas only a sad remnant of the splendor of the magnificent sea lilies in the seas of prehistoric times. Nature has completely abandoned this path of development. But in Pentacrinus europaeus (Comatula rosacea), discovered in 1827, it seems to want to demonstrate to us the entire course of this development in the transformations of a single species. (Müller, Pentacrinus, p. 7.) “In the beginning, the animal resembles a club (it is a cystoid); it is attached by a broad base, and a few transparent pinnules protrude from its tip. No part of the hard parts is visible, only an indistinct appearance of the calyx. In a more advanced stage of development, with the lengthening of the stem, the segments begin to appear; the pinnules become more prominent, and the bases of the arms, as well as the cirri, become visible, whereupon the arms lengthen. (It becomes a crinoid.) Fully developed, the animal completely separates from its stem;” it becomes a Comatula, and now it floats freely in the sea, without being further hindered in its potential development.
