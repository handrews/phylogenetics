In my little letter, April 1738. I have written a little more than the previous ones, on the knowledge of Gräberg, and that in order that a person, from the relationship of Gräberg and the mining region to each other, might be able to make fairly certain calculations as to how many minerals were to be found in this or that place. Gräberg's knowledge is therefore not only necessary for those who want to know the mineral history of a place, but also absolutely indispensable for an ore prospector. Knowledge should not only extend to the solid and unaltered Gräberg rocks and rock cliffs themselves, but also to the loose and scattered Gräberg rocks, which appear everywhere on the ground: for they are all too often found to consist of a completely different rock material than the Graͤberg rocks existing underneath, and often of rich metallic content, although for several miles around there are no signs in the rocks that metal and mineral are present, which experienced miners can probably prove.

That such loose rocks and mineral outcrops, which often amount to many hundreds of ship-pounds in weight, have been the subject of archaeology since the time the Earth was formed: without that they have subsequently undergone all sorts of changes and enlargements, every sensible person, who will look into the matter a little later, will be able to find, and to hold it quite certain, that the decaying loose rocks and outcrops have certainly in some part been the rock from which the former ones were formed.

More evidence to support this idea could be given, but I will for the time being rely on the following findings, which I obtained during a commissioned expedition in Finland in the years 1737 and 1738.
