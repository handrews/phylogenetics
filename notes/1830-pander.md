LITHOZOANS

Among them are some forms which, in view of their frequent occurrence, rival the trilobites, orthoceratites, and even the terebratulites; others, however, are found very rarely in the local limestone; the former include those that are given the name of pore corals and are classified by Lamarck as foraminiferous polyps; the latter, on the other hand, are more closely related to the lamelliferous polyps. The genus Favosites appears most frequently, whose countless forms mainly belong to the local limestone and occur in all its layers, even in the lowest ones. As numerous as they are, their external form is almost equally varied, and it is therefore impossible to attribute a specific, regular shape to them; rather, it seems to us that this was conditioned and determined by external influences, whereby one and the same lithozoan, as an animal attached to other organisms or inorganic parts, was forced to assume this or that external form, and that this depended largely on its substrate as well as on the external environment. In their earliest state, they appear as completely flat, small, round discs, porous on both surfaces; see Plate II, Figs. 5, 6, 7; as they grow larger, they become more elevated with a flat, or sometimes slightly concave, lower surface, but a convex upper surface (see Plate II, Figs. 12, 13, 14), on which the original shape and distribution of the pores always remains the same: only with the difference that these, which initially appeared as flat cells, have been transformed into tubes through vertical growth. (Table II, Fig. 15.) On the lower surface, however, these cells gradually disappear with increasing growth; this surface becomes smoother and denser as the mass increases, but at the same time it grows in width, if space permits, and concentric, parallel, slightly raised circles form on it (Table I, Fig. 10, 6. Fig. 11. Table II, Fig. 10), which increase in number the wider the coral becomes, a phenomenon that is probably related to age. If this free space for growth in all directions is not available, or if it is inhibited by external resistance from one side or another, the coral mass is forced to assume a different shape; the lower surface often remains very small and narrow, even pointed (Table I, Fig. 8), and thus all the manifold external forms arise, of which we have only highlighted a few (Table I, Fig. 6, 7, 8, 9. Table II, Fig. 11), but to which we could add a great many others if further noteworthy differences had been observed; thus, the tuberous, kidney-shaped, mushroom-shaped, etc., forms are not uncommon. This diversity in external form, which we attribute to different ages and external circumstances, was probably partly the reason for the establishment of several genera of extinct lithozoans, which are listed by several zoologists; but this multiplication of genera also probably arose from the fact that the form of the pores was sometimes more, sometimes less clearly discernible; Thus, we find them round in one genus, elongated in another, and hexagonal or pentagonal in a third; here standing closer together, there separated by wider, thicker interspaces—changes that can often occur during the life of the animals through the shifting of the cells, but which most frequently take place after their death, when their remains are exposed to all external influences, through abrasion, filtration, and other circumstances, and can reach such a high degree that in some cases, only with the greatest difficulty is it possible to discern any trace of a former organic existence. In all the specimens we observed, which certainly numbered between six and seven hundred, all the pores on the surface were originally, and undoubtedly, regularly hexagonal, separated from one another by sharp walls, perfectly mimicking the shape of honeycomb cells, and this remains the only characteristic, but also, it seems, the most important one, common to all of them; often this regular structure is only recognizable in individual small areas, while the greater part of the remainder is already altered, but frequently we only perceive this type with the aid of a magnifying glass, where the naked eye only sees round cells. The only difference that struck us was the varying size of these cells. We retain the name Favosites because the lithozoans discussed here, in their fully developed form, possess the characteristic features of this genus, namely, that parallel, hexagonal, contiguous tubes rise from the lower surface and, through their union, form a simple mass whose surface, due to the openings of the tubes, acquires the appearance of honeycomb cells.

The smallest of these lithozoans that we found were approximately two to three lines in diameter and barely half a line high (Plate II, Fig. 7). Both surfaces contain cells, but these are so different in their arrangement that one can immediately distinguish the lower from the upper surface; those of the latter are regularly hexagonal with sides and angles that remain constant (Plate XXIX, Fig. 5 shown on an enlarged scale), a form they always retain, whether the Favosites are a couple of lines or several inches in diameter. On the lower surface, however, which is also marked with six-sided pores, these pores originate from a slightly raised center, are elongated, and increase in length and width from the center towards the periphery, towards which they radiate (Table XXIX, Fig. 6b; Table II, Figs. 5 and 8). During the growth of the favosites, the already formed low cells of the surface rise into tubes, and new cells are constantly added laterally at the circumference, which rise higher the older and larger they become. In this way, it seems that the hemisphere is the shape that, if no obstacles were present, would be considered the original form. During this time, however, the cells on the lower surface disappear, as the septa thicken at their expense, lose their regular shape, and appear sometimes round, sometimes elongated (Table XXIX, Fig. 4); finally, they begin to disappear completely from the center, the lower surface becomes smooth in the same proportion, and the remaining concentric rings only indicate the sequence of increasing size and the associated densification of the mass; this is also why the lower surface is often already smooth in the middle, while the outer circumference is still perforated by pores, because the latter, having been formed later, have not yet undergone this transformation. Here, then, we have in a single animal a series of metamorphoses, the individual stages of which, considered as fixed points, have, it seems to us, been described as different genera. See especially Lamarck on this point, where so much importance is placed on whether both surfaces of the lithozoans are porous or not, and on the shape of these pores.

If the earliest stages of Favosites formation are connected to either the Eschara or Flustra (Table II, Figs. 5-8), and thus belong to Lamarck's polypiers à reseau, we find their resemblance to the Orbulites or Orbitulites of Lamarck and Cuvier very striking at a more developed stage (Table II, Figs. 9, 10, 12, 13), until they finally correspond perfectly to the Favosites, and the upper tubes clearly appear hexagonal; it could even be assumed that the stages of development could be traced all the way to the Alveolites, to which the concentric circles of the lower surface seem to point; in some specimens, even some traces of the concentrically overlapping layers can be observed longitudinally.

Since it is quite impossible for us to definitively separate some forms from others among the large number of these fossils by essential differences, we consider them for the time being as entirely belonging together, under the name Favosites petropolitana.

We were fortunate enough to find only a single specimen of this Hexaporites at Peselowa, specifically in the lowest layers of the limestone, where it is still colored green by the glauconite.

Another division is formed by the reticulate polypiers, because in these the cells do not lengthen into tubes and do not penetrate the entire mass, but only sink slightly into the surface and end blindly. Several forms of these are found, but they cannot be readily assigned to any of the described genera known to us, and we must therefore give them new names.

ECHINOSPHAERITES. Wahlenberg.

Under this name, Wahlenberg describes three different species of fossils, which he classifies with the echinoids based on their spherical shape and the position of the mouth and anus, which he believed he had found on them, but due to the lack of other parts, he felt compelled to separate them from the echinoids. These spheres, which were also erroneously given the name of Alcyonites, occur here, as in the limestone of Sweden, in large numbers; they are most common at Jumalasaari, on the banks of the Pulkovka and in the ravines near Krasnoye Selo, as well as wherever the middle clayey limestone layers, to which they mainly seem to belong, are still present. We examined several hundred in vain in order to learn their structure precisely; we always found only what Wahlenberg had said about them, and in many cases, these characteristics were not even clearly discernible, since in some cases the surface had become unrecognizable due to the calcite crystals that frequently fill them, or where they enclosed dense limestone, this had clogged the openings of the pores, and these fine openings escaped our observations. We therefore possess very few perfectly well-preserved specimens from which one can ascertain their structure.

First, we distinguish four openings of different sizes and shapes on them; Wahlenberg correctly identifies one of them as the mouth, which, however, is only recognizable in Echinosphaerites aurantium and therefore entitles us to give the same meaning to the openings that we see in the same place in the others. It is covered by five triangular, precisely fitting flaps which, when closed, more or less protrude above the rest of the sphere's surface, and in this respect it perfectly resembles the mouth opening which we find described in Cassidulus lapis cancri (Encycl. pl. 143. Fig. 7.) (Table II. Fig. 2, Table XXIX. Fig. 2. a. 3. b.); through it, the lower surface of the animal is partially revealed to us. On the upper hemisphere, not completely opposite the mouth, but always a few lines to the side, is a blunt elevation, which also, and even more strongly, protrudes above the surface, likewise enclosing an opening that is closed by six touching, flat tubercles, which are sometimes closer together, sometimes further apart, and then leave a round space between them. (Table XXIX. Fig. 2 a. 2. b. Fig. 1. a and b. Table II. Fig. 28). We must consider this opening to be the anus for now. The third opening, which is never closed, like the mouth and anus, by parts belonging to the shell, is again located on the lower hemisphere, a few lines away from the mouth and directly opposite the anus, so that the sphere takes on the appearance, as Wahlenberg already noted, as if there were two opposite, raised poles on it (Table XXIX. Fig. 2 a.), since the part of the shell that forms the edge of this opening (Table II. Fig. 21.) often protrudes as a thick bulge and thus forms a tubercle. This opening is the seat or point of attachment of a part that is not present in true echinoids, although similar structures have been observed in animals belonging to the Asteria group from the earliest formations: namely, it is the opening for the passage of a hard, calcareous stem, by which the Echinosphaerites were attached to a specific location. Just as the crinoids are nothing more than sessile, stalked Euryalids (Cuvier, Règne animal, Vol. IV, p. 12), so the Echinosphaerites are sessile, stalked echinoids. Finally, the fourth opening, which Wahlenberg considered to be the anus, we cannot interpret with certainty, since, being unfamiliar with the organization of the Echinosphaerites, we cannot find any point of reference among still-living genera. It is the smallest of all, usually round, completely open, and is located one or two lines to the side of the stem insertion, so that it forms a triangle with the stem insertion and the mouth opening, and occurs only in Echinosph. aurantium. Could it be that some organ formerly required a separate opening? (Plate II, Fig. 21; Plate XXIX, Fig. 2a). The shell of the Echinosphaerites, like that of sea urchins, is composed of angular pieces that fit together precisely, the typical form of which is a regular hexagon. However, due to the displacement of some of these plates, pentagonal and heptagonal shapes also occur, which in turn influence the shape of the adjacent plates.

These plates are more or less striated in all specimens, and both the direction and the nature of these striations provide a convenient means of distinguishing the already different forms from one another at first glance; in some, the plates are perforated by small pores, which are distributed with the greatest symmetry, but in a completely different way than in the known sea urchins, and probably also served here to allow the tube feet or membranous tubes to pass through; in others, we have not yet been able to discover these pores, so that for the time being we must deny their presence in these specimens, and therefore divide the Echinosphaerites into those that possess them and those that do not.

According to Wahlenberg, this form occurs only in West Gothland in the upper shale layers, not far below the trap formation; in this area it is found in the upper and especially in the middle clayey layers of the limestone, from which it is very easy to extract.

The entire surface (Tab. XXIX. Fig. 3 a. 36.) consists of hexagonal, flat, striated shields or plates, on which a star is located, which is formed by the small holes intended for the passage of the membranous tubes. The points of this star usually correspond to the angles of the hexagon and undergo a corresponding change according to the varying shape of the plates, as the hexagon is sometimes shifted, so that, depending on whether the plates have more or fewer sides and angles, the latter are sometimes obtuse, sometimes acute, and the number of points also increases or decreases, their direction is modified accordingly, and their original shape is so altered that they themselves appear as rays emanating from a central point; compare this especially with Plate XXIX, Fig. 5, a. Each plate thus has its own ambulacra, whereas in the sea urchins still living and those found in the mountains of later formations, such as the Cretaceous, etc., only some of them participate in this formation, where they then run continuously either in a straight line from the anus to the mouth, or sit on the upper half of the shell as a so-called rose. The description of the four openings, which has already been given in general, refers particularly to this form. Their size varies greatly; they range from the size of a nut to that of an orange and are present in large numbers. We have only seen the insertion of the stem in Echinosphaerites aurantium once, and even there only a single segment of it seemed to be attached to the shell; however, we suspect that the so-called trochites or wheel stones, which are very frequently found in the limestone in our region, belong to the Echinosphaerites rather than to the actual crinoids, to which they are usually attributed, since we have never found any other trace of the latter's existence in these areas. Therefore, the segments depicted in Plate II, Figs. 17, 18, and 20 would most likely belong here.

The two following forms differ from those just mentioned partly through the greater prominence and larger size of their scales, partly through stronger and broader stripes on them, but mainly in that the arrangement of the pores in these forms has adopted a direction considered normal, which in the previous form occurred only abnormally and extremely rarely, namely, that in the most regular formation of the hexagonal scales, the pores always run alongside each other in a straight line from the raised center of each scale towards its peripheral angles.

Echinosphaerites Malum, Plate XXIX, Figs. 1. a, b and c.
Almost spherical, but rather somewhat conical, like several Galerites, as the lower surface is slightly flattened. The mouth opening is visible approximately halfway up, but more towards the lower surface. The opening in the base for the stem is very elongated in the single specimen we possess.

Echinosphaerites oblonga, Plate II, Figs. 22 and 23.

Shape elongated oval, differs from the two preceding ones in that almost the entire surface is covered with smaller and larger round pits, which probably served as attachment points for the spines, thus quite different from those still living today, in which the movable spines articulate on tubercles of the outer shell. We have not been able to properly distinguish the openings for the mouth, anus, and stem in this form, but we have placed it here because of the distribution of the pores, although the articulation pits, which are absent in all others, already suggest an approximation to later forms.

Echinosphaerites without pores.

Echin. angulosa, Plate II, Figs. 27, 28, 29. Plate XXVIII, Fig. 11.

In this and the following form, we mainly observed the presence of the stem, as we found several specimens that still had several segments of it attached. It consists of individual narrow, hollow segments, which lie on top of each other like those of the crinoids, and probably assumed various shapes depending on the different forms. The ratio of the mouth and anus is as in the previous one. Shape elongated, oval; the plates rise sharply towards their center, and the entire shell is therefore very angular. From the center of each shield, broad, raised ribs diverge towards the circumference, and unite with those that come towards them from the neighboring shield; the narrow spaces between them are filled and, as it were, latticed by raised transverse stripes.

The external shape is very angular for the same reason as in the previous one. It differs from the preceding one by having larger plates and much finer longitudinal and transverse striations. Externally, there is a great similarity to the illustration given by Hisinger (Kon. Vet. Acd. Hand. 1802, Plate VII), but the position of the mouth and anus is quite different.

Echinosph. laevis, Plate II, Figs. 24, 25 and 26.

Mouth opening on the lower surface very close to the stem, surrounded by countless small warts. Anus located more opposite the mouth than the stem. Surface smooth, without longitudinal and transverse striations, but with concentric flat circles increasing in size from the center of each plate, which they surround, to the periphery, and finally, the closer they approach the circumference, the more they imitate its hexagonal shape.

The fragments depicted on Plate II, Figures 33, 34 and 35, probably belong to similar Echinosphaerites, which, judging by their curvature, must have been parts of much larger individuals; Fig. 35 shows a single displaced six-sided plate, from the center of which six sharp, protruding lamellae run in a straight line towards the corners. Fig. 33 is the upper part of another Echinosphaerite, whose plates were completely smooth and without pores. Fig. 34 differs from all the others due to the great variation in size among the touching plates; one sees two large, displaced hexagonal plates, and the others, while retaining the same shape, suddenly become much smaller.

Shouldn't the specimen depicted in Table I, Fig. 22 perhaps also belong here, about which, unfortunately, we have nothing more to say than what is visible in the three illustrations? The insertion of a stem is clearly recognizable; a segment of the latter is even still stuck in the opening intended for it. The surface is slightly convex and covered with a multitude of small pits; at first glance, it appears to represent a bivalve mollusk, but nowhere is there a separation into two parts.

Now, there remain some distinctly organically formed bodies, which, however, we have not yet been able to classify, but which we cannot ignore either, because they occur quite frequently. They are depicted on the second plate in Figs. 36-40. Could they perhaps belong to Wahlenberg's Phacites? Their shape is round, the upper and lower surfaces are slightly curved, while the sides are very strongly curved, so that they more or less approach a spherical shape. The middle of their height is usually marked by a sharp, circular, slightly raised edge, and in the center of both surfaces there is a depression, which often has a round, sometimes also a pentagonal shape, and is pierced by a small hole that passes through the entire mass and reappears on the opposite surface. The entire mass of these bodies is perfectly dense; however, when grinding them down, one can see that five diverging vertical lamellae extend from this hole towards the circumference. This, however, was the only thing we could discover about them; It is highly probable that they are in some way related to the echinospherites, since the harder parts of starfish and sea urchins usually occur in fives; however, whether they are independent entities or merely parts or segments of the stems remains undetermined for the time being.

Finally, we will only mention a fragment that is depicted in the fourth figure of the first plate. A hard outer shell is clearly visible, forming a curved tube that is wider on one side and narrower on the other, and filled with dense calcareous material. On this surface, however, nothing further can be distinguished except for very fine, raised, but unsegmented transverse striations running parallel to one another.
