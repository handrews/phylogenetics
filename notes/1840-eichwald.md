RADIATA [STRAHLTHIERE]

In the Silurian limestone of Estonia, crinoids [Crinoideen] are found in abundance, mostly stalked genera, but also others that were probably unstalked; on the whole, they are less common here than on the island of Gotland and in England; they are rarest around Pavlovsk. I observed most species of stalked crinoids on the coast of Spitham, lying loosely in the sand among many other fragments, so that they could easily have been washed ashore from a distance, perhaps even from Gotland, by the ocean waves. Also, in the middle of the road between Hapsal and Reval, there is a very hard, crinoid-rich limestone, which, like the one at Dolgaya, south of St. Petersburg, in the Gdov district, contains striking forms of crinoids. Most remarkable in Estonia, and especially around Pavlovsk, are the genera whose calyx (or the so-called crown), whether unstalked or short-stalked, could not open at the top and therefore probably did not yet possess calyx arms.

Cornulites serpularius Schloth.

These peculiar bodies, also found near Reval and Pavlovsk, seem most appropriately classified with the stalked crinoids; several species apparently belong to this group; I will mention the most notable one here.

Some are small, slightly curved, and consist of 6–8 tubular segments that are telescoped into one another like a telescope; they become progressively narrower towards the thinner end and are hollow inside (although always filled with calcareous material); the wall is thin due to the large internal cavity. This fragment was found near Pavlovsk and resembles the lower, broken piece of Cornulites serpularius Schloth. (l.c. Vol. I, Plate XXIX, Fig. 7) from Gotland; the tip is not preserved.

Another specimen from Pavlovsk is much wider, but accidentally flattened; it is 3 lines wide and 2 lines thick and also consists of 7–8 segments that are strongly extended and therefore show a narrowed and transversely striated upper part, while the lower part appears wider, thicker, and inflated; this probably accommodated the upper part of the segment below it, and therefore this internally hollow, segmented tube could probably expand and lengthen like a telescope and then retract again. This would certainly be a striking structure, unlike anything we have observed so far in the arms of crinoids.

I also possess a fragment from Pavlovsk consisting of 18 segments, which, almost the shape of a Tentaculites, is only slightly curved, showing a width of 2 lines at the bottom and only 1 line at the top; the segments are regularly spaced and transversely striated. Here, too, the internal cavity is very large.

Gonocrinites m.

This is the name I give to a stalked crinoid genus that Pander describes as Echinosphaerites; it is distinguished by having an angularly rounded crown with a distinct stem and a large mouth opening opposite to it, as well as a lateral anal opening. The calyx consists of 4 plates that are very unequal, so that one of them appears very large, while the others gradually become smaller; the smallest is almost as large as the largest plate. These are followed by 5 almost equally sized radial plates, and then 5 others, also almost equal in size, and at the very top of the apex, 5 other smaller plates surround the mouth opening, so that 3 rows of 5 radial plates each form the crown, excluding those 4 calyx plates. The corners of the individual plates form the center from which more or less projecting ridges or strongly protruding ribs radiate outwards in all directions, between which numerous fine transverse striae are observed. It has only been found near Pavlovsk and contains 2 species.

Gonocrinites angulosus Pand.

This species shows clearly separated, 5- to 6-sided plates, only visible in broken specimens, with strongly projecting corners in their center, from which the radially spreading ridges, usually dichotomously branched, continue into the angles and sides of the plates; fine transverse striations are visible everywhere between these ridges. The mouth opening is very large, located at the apex, and opposite it, on the base, a short, spirally coiled stem is visible. Most specimens are 8 lines high; however, much larger ones also exist.

Gonocrinites striatus Pand.

This species is much larger than the previous one, and is particularly distinguished by the fact that the corners are less numerous but project more strongly, and that a multitude of equally sized, fine ridges originate from the corners in four directions, thus forming an elegant striation that covers the entire Gonocrinites; this is made even more elegant by the multitude of fine transverse striations visible between them.

A large mouth opening is also observed in this species, surrounded by three flattened plates; this forms a triangle with which the body ends; towards the center is the second, much larger opening, which is to be considered the anus.

The base projects strongly and probably had a stem, although it is never as clearly preserved as in the other species; however, the stem appears to have been unsegmented and, in this case, short; it always appears only as a small appendage, somewhat curved and irregular; it is also somewhat compressed and shows a channel in the middle, which probably corresponds to the food channel of the crinoid stem. The lattice-like striped pattern of the individual platelets is clearly visible on the surface of this short stem; here, they are separated from one another at their ends by smooth interspaces.

This pattern is also found only around Pavlovsk.

Hemicosmites pyriformis Buch.

Echinosphaerites malum Pand.

This beautiful genus, named and first described very accurately by Mr. von Buch, is also found only at Pavlovsk; it has 4 pelvic plates, like Gonocrinites, and above these two rows of brachial plates, six in each row; the uppermost row forms the apical plates, and between them are intermediate plates, which are much narrower and proportionally longer than the others. These are absent in Gonocrinites; it also lacks the many small plates in the oral opening and the many rows of tubercles on the brachial plates. The apex is particularly remarkable. On it, as on other crinoids, such as Melocrinites hieroglyphicus and Eugeniacrinites mespiliformis, I observe a projection or elevation on 3 apical plates, which are arranged in a triangle surrounding the oral opening. An intermediate apical plate and two apical plates are elevated, and this prominence appears deeply indented on the surface; the depression continues into a canal that leads to the oral opening, thus creating a distinctly triangular oral opening, which in small specimens is hardly formed at all or only by a few small plates. Could these three depressions in the apical plates have been the attachment points for the tentacles?

The large anal opening of the smaller specimens is located between two large brachial plates and an apical plate, without being surrounded by small plates; I only see these clearly in large specimens, so they probably develop with increasing age.

The openings of the plates were undoubtedly distinct tentacle canals, as they are all perforated and appear without any denticles on my specimens; sometimes, however, they are raised as tubercles, but were probably only closed later. Its original meaning may therefore have corresponded to the sensory organs, as the following species demonstrate more clearly. If the species was stalked, it probably had a leaky stalk, as indicated by the leaky opening of the calyx.

Hemicosmites extraneus m.

If that species was stalked, then this one was too, for a distinct, large opening is also observed at the base of its calyx. Surrounding this opening are four unequal, pentagonal plates, which begin with a narrow base and widen upwards. Below, two angles are visible, and above, three; the middle one is the highest, the other two to the sides, somewhat lower than the middle one. Next follows a row of six large, hexagonal plates, and above them six other, much smaller apical plates with an equal number of intervening plates, so that the total number of plates may well have been the same as in Hemicosmites pyriformis. However, since a large part of the apex is hidden in the rock, neither the mouth nor the anus is visible. On another fragment, however, I see above the apical plates another (albeit incomplete) row of hexagonal (?) plates, one of which shows a round opening, probably the anus, since this is not located at the highest point of the apex. The mouth opening was probably situated close beside it, in the middle of the apex, as in Comatula.

The surface of the individual plates shows many pits or small depressions, which radiate like channels from the center, so that one usually observes six regular channels, each lined with a single or double row of pore openings; these channels spread from the center to the corners of the plates. The arrangement of the openings is different in the calyx plates, which have a different shape; however, they always show only single rows of pores.

In this species, I also noticed a raised area on some of the intermediate and apical ribs, which may have served as an attachment point for the arms, if these were present.

This species was found as a fossil on the beach not far from Spitham.

Hemicosmites porosus m.

In a very hard, crystalline, dense limestone not far from Hapsal, I observed individual, 6-sided, but mostly 7-sided plates of considerable thickness, which I can only assign to the genus Hemicosmites, although this genus generally seems to have had thinner rather than thicker plates.

The plates are longer, and therefore narrower, than those of other species; their edges are slightly sloping and smooth, the center of each plate is elevated, and from there, seven slightly raised ridges with pore openings extend to each of the corners. Since the corners are somewhat far apart, similar pores are also found at right angles to each other, making the rows of pores appear even more numerous. The two longer sides of the plates lie parallel to each other; two other sides converge at an obtuse angle at the bottom, and the remaining three sides meet at two obtuse angles at the top. These are clearly the rib plates of a Hemicosmites. Individual calyx plates are also found, though more rarely, and almost identical in shape to the plates of Hemicosmites extraneus. They are thickened at the narrower end and show an articulation surface for the attachment of a stem, fragments of which I have not observed anywhere.

Cryptocrinites regularis Buch.

Echinosphaerites laevis Pand.

The calyx has a base composed of three plates, which are sometimes four-sided, sometimes five-sided, and together form a pentagon; the base is followed by two rows of brachial plates, five in each row; the apex is also surrounded by five plates, which alternate with the brachial plates. The mouth opening is also located at the apex, surrounded by very small plates.

All the platelets are perfectly smooth and raised in the center, thus forming a spherical shape with a bulging curvature in the middle of each platelet; however, the younger ones are much rounder and resemble the figures in Mr. von Buch's work, 1. c. Plate I, Figs. 9–12. The large, convex specimens clearly show concentric growth lines along the 5 or 6 edges of the individual platelets; these appear as angular streaks that resemble clouds and indicate the growth of the platelets. It is probable that in even older specimens, more smaller platelets develop upwards, and these then seem to form the other species, Crypt. cerasus Buch.

Cryplocrinites cerasus Buch.

This equally smooth species differs only in having intercostal plates, which significantly increases the total number of plates. The anal opening is visible on the apex below the oral opening. The stem opening is very small and delicate, so that the stem itself, if present, must undoubtedly have been very thin; however, I never saw it, not even a small fragment attached to the calyx. The size usually reaches that of a hazelnut. Both species were found only near Pavlovsk.

Protocrinites oviformis m.

This is also a peculiar genus, differing in many respects from all other crinoids; I only know the calyx without stem segments; it is egg-shaped, over 1 inch 2 lines long and 11 lines thick, and consists of 5 rows of longitudinal plates, bounded by wavy grooves or channels; these channels run from the apex down to the base. These 5 longitudinal strips of very diverse plates meet at the bottom and form a very irregular pentagon, whose long or short corners surround the opening, which probably accommodated a very irregular stem. The calyx also seems to consist of 5 equally irregular plates, which, however, surpass all the others in size. The higher the plates extend, the smaller they become, but they almost always remain pentagonal, only so varied in shape that no two are alike. Most of them, especially the middle, lower ones, seem to have possessed a wart-like elevation in the center, and the uppermost ones seem to consist entirely of small, adjacent warts, so that they appear rough and uneven. The apex is occupied by a small opening, which, as the mouth opening, is surrounded by 4 unequal, very small plates, which could probably open and close; 2 lines below this, to the side in the longitudinal line itself, is the almost 2 lines wide anal opening, and § inch lower, slightly to the side of this longitudinal line, an even larger opening, which might be considered the genital opening, if it were constant. The 4 small oral plates are surrounded by 6 other, somewhat larger plates in a regular ring-shaped arrangement. The species was probably stalked, as can be inferred from the pentagonal opening at the base. I believe I can discern small openings on the cup-shaped plates, which seem to belong together in pairs, suggesting a resemblance to Sphaeronites pomum, although it is otherwise completely different from it.

Sphaeronites aurantium Wahl.

Echinosphaerites citrus His.

Leucophthalmus Straugwaysii König.

This is a very common fossil from Pavlovsk and Reval; it is found sometimes the size of a hazelnut, sometimes the size of an apple, thus about 2 inches in diameter. The calyx consists of a multitude of irregular plates, some smaller, some larger, the pattern of which neither Wahlenberg nor Pander have described entirely accurately. In a remarkably well-preserved specimen from Reval, I observe that two stripes (probably canals) above and below unite under an arch, without any opening appearing anywhere; these ellipses are always arranged in regular rows in such a way that the smallest ones are noticed stepwise on both sides, and their length gradually increases towards the middle. In the middle, the entire row of elliptical tubules is intersected by the boundary of two plates. The oral opening at the apex appears to be five-lobed, and almost in the middle between this opening and the base, a large opening is visible on one side, which could be closed with five leaflets and is quite rightly considered by Mr. von Buch to be the genital opening. Above this central opening, to the side, is the small anal opening.

The base ends in a distinct projection, which perhaps itself formed the stem, or had a stem attached to it. I only see in individual specimens, more clearly in the smaller ones, a knobby projection that shows a canal in the middle, but no segments, and therefore could not have formed an articulated stem, which was perhaps leathery-fleshy, like in ascidians, and attached the body to rocks. This is further confirmed by another specimen; In this specimen, embedded in solid limestone, I observe that the short stem tapers to a point, thus truly ending there, so that it would be impossible to assume it extends any further. Since the entire specimen is split lengthwise, just like this stem, a cavity is also visible within it, extending all the way to the tip.

I possess the finest specimens from Reval; in them, the pattern of the plates described above is very clearly visible. Rare, equally well-preserved specimens are also found at Pavlovsk, similar to those depicted by Hisinger in his Letliaea suecica, Plate XXV, Fig. 8, but without the segmented stem, which could hardly belong to this genus.

Sphaeronites pomum Wahl.

This rare species also occurs near Pulkowa and on the Ishora River; I have not yet observed it in Estonia; I know of specimens that are over two inches in diameter, but are strongly compressed; they beautifully display the form depicted by Ilisinger in the Lelhaea suecica, Plate XXV, Fig. 7; two small, opposite pores are surrounded by an oval ring, and these are evenly spaced from one another; the surface is almost flat and smooth, by no means as bumpy as depicted by Ilisinger, and certainly not as uneven and tapering into blunt points as shown by Gyllenhaal and Wahlenberg. In this species, too, I observe a distinct, fairly long, but completely unsegmented stem that gradually tapers to a point; I cannot discern the other openings in my specimens.

Heliocrinites balticus m.

Echinosphaerites balticus Zool. spec.

The entire surface is covered with hexagonal plates; flattened rays originate from their corners and converge at the center; sometimes only one ray is observed, but sometimes five, or even eight rays are present. These rays protrude strongly and simultaneously form the sides of the individual fields, which therefore do not show distinct boundaries, as in Actinocrinites triacontodactylus Mül., but rather merge into one another. Nowhere are the individual openings clearly visible, but at one end, where the rays become finer and smaller and closer together, a tubercle-like projection is noticeable, which might be considered a rudimentary stem. Above it, the apex is characterized by very wide, large fields; however, the specimens here are usually abraded, so that no distinct opening is visible anywhere. On the side, I occasionally observe one or two small tubercles, which might belong to similar openings, but they are so indistinct that nothing definite can be said about them.

In an almost one-inch wide, and therefore very large, specimen, the end with the large fields is preserved, and there one can clearly see a large, h-line wide opening, which is completely enclosed by five radiating fields. These rise slightly into a raised rim, thus forming the mouth opening. The rays are finely striated lengthwise on both sides, although only indistinctly, so the individual rays mostly stand out with a simple, sharp edge.

This species is found around Reval and in other parts of Estonia, rarely on Odinsholm, and it is said to have been observed only once around Pavlovsk.

I also know of fragments of a species that is probably different from this one. The plates are also angular, mostly pentagonal; on them, usually 4-5 rays radiate from the center; they form angular, slightly protruding, simple projections that create very unequal angles at the center, and are finely transversely striated on both sides where they descend; these fine furrows run parallel to each other, making the rays appear feathered. One fragment also shows an attachment point for a round stem between four rays of a field. It was also found around Reval.

Heliocrinites radiatus m.

This species is distinguished primarily by the fact that it does not have simple, very prominent and somewhat compressed rays, which form the fields in groups of 5 to 9, but rather that these rays are longitudinally divided into finer rays, thus appearing as if split; therefore, each field appears radially striated from the center, so that these striations, close together, form the rays themselves, thus showing distinct boundaries, something not observed in the other species. On other fields, the striations are visible over the entire surface, such that the main rays are always clearly recognizable by their greater prominence, and the striations extend from them on both sides, finally bordering those of the neighboring rays, but meeting them at an oblique angle.

I know of only one specimen, a sphere 1 inch and 2 lines wide, of which only a part of the shell is preserved, and therefore I see no openings anywhere, except for a small wart-like protuberance at one point, which apparently corresponds to the lower end, where a stem may have been attached.

It was also found near Reval, and the variation of the previous species probably belongs to this one rather than to the other.

Cyclocrinites Spaskii m.

These delicate, perfectly spherical bodies consist of small, 5- or 6-sided plates, each measuring slightly over one line in diameter, while the sphere itself is 9 lines in diameter. The surface of the plates is radially striated; in some well-preserved specimens, the striae protrude above the surface, so that they initially resemble Heliocrinites, except that in Cyclocrinites the fields are much smaller and therefore much more numerous. In the center, they are completely smooth, without striae; these striae, usually 15 in number, only appear at some distance from the center and do not extend beyond the boundaries of the plates, which are therefore not formed by them, as is the case in Heliocrinites. Since these spherical bodies are always embedded in solid rock, their openings are nowhere visible. This species was found in a dense limestone near Munelas, not far from Reval.
