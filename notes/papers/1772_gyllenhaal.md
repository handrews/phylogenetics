**DESCRIPTION of the so-called Crystal-apples and Lime-balls, as petrified animals of the genus Echinus, or its nearest relatives: composed by JOH. ABR. GYLLENHAHL, Auscultant in the Royal Board of Mines.**

§ 1. Hitherto these balls have not been regarded as remains of animals, but merely as druses when they consisted inside of calc-spar, and as accidentally formed lime-nodules when the filling was dense limestone. **[p. 240]** They are mentioned:

1. By the Governor and Commander Baron TILAS in K. Sv. Vet. Acad. Handl. 1740, p. 196, where, at Tab. II f. 18, a drawing is added of a split crystal-apple from Osmundsberget in Rättvik parish in Dalarna.

2. By the Archiater and Knight von LINNÉ, in his *Öländska Resa*, p. 136: "Crystal-apples I call the globular stones found in the limestone hills, of the size of apples, which when broken resemble a haematite and consist of nothing but clear and transparent spar crystals that meet in the centre, sometimes leaving a small hollow in the very centre, so that their three-cornered points can plainly be seen. These crystal-apples are fairly common here on Öland, etc." Ibid. p. 156.

3. By the Archiater von LINNÉ in *Mus. Tessin.* p. 80, no. 3; and

4. — — — *Syst. Nat.* 12th ed., vol. 3, p. 179, Gen. 46, no. 4: *Ætites (marmoreus)* marmoreus, embryonibus fixis crystallinis, Natri hyodontis. Compare p. 93, no. 14.

5. By Professor and Knight Dr KALM in the *Västgöta och Bohuslänska Resa*, p. 28: "Crystal-apples were found in great abundance at Västerplana on Kinnekulle, often lying close to one another in a dry clay, on the rough side of the so-called white stone."

6. By Professor and Knight J. G. WALLERIUS, in his *Mineralogie*, p. 62, 5: "Of conical, truncated, six-sided, together with some seven-sided spar crystals most of the spar-globes that are found in Dalarna are composed. — — — Such a conical crystal is shown in fig. 6." **[p. 241]** Ibid. p. 63, 12: "Round spar-globes."

7. By the Mine-master CRONSTEDT, in his *Försök til en ny Mineralogie*, p. 11: "Crystalli pyramidales concreti: spar-globes of eight-sided calc-spar crystals, from Osmundsberget."

The Mining Councillor LEHMANN describes, in his *Versuch einer Geographia Subterranea* of the Brandenburg lands (set as a preface before his *Geschichte der Flötzgebürgen*), p. 46, a kind of calc-spar globe which in structure agrees well with our crystal-apples but so markedly exceeds them in size that they cannot be taken for one and the same kind. Meanwhile I have thought I should mention them here, so that those who have the opportunity may be led to look into whether they do not at least have some similar origin. Mr LEHMANN describes his spar-globes thus: "Nicht weit von Laublingen findet sich in einem Berge eine Art Spat-Kugeln, öfters als ein Kopf gross; äusserlich sehen solche eckig, als so genannte Mergel-Nüsse, aus; wenn man sie zerschlägt, so zerspringen sie allezeit in viereckigte pyramidalische Körper, deren Spitze in den Mittelpunct dieser Kugeln zusammenläuft, nach der Peripherie aber die unterste breite Basin formiren: von Farbe sind sie gelb &c." [Not far from Laublingen there is found in a hill a kind of spar-ball, often as large as a head; outwardly they look angular, like the so-called marl-nuts; when one breaks them they always spring apart into four-sided pyramidal bodies whose points run together in the centre of these balls, but toward the periphery form the lowest broad base: in colour they are yellow, etc.] Baron TILAS mentions, loc. cit., a kind of lime-ball of about 1 foot in diameter and of flat oblong figure, which lie in the same bed of Osmundsberget as the small globular ones, on account of which I have properly cited the passage. These are moreover still as unknown in origin and nature as the great crystal-globes **[p. 242]** described by Mr LEHMANN.

Crystal-apples and lime-balls occur abundantly in certain beds of our stratified (flötz) hills. In Närke and in Skaraborg county I have had the opportunity to see them in their natural position, and also, both on these and on pieces of the same kind from Rättvik in Dalarna, to find such plain traces of organic structure that I can not only affirm that they are petrifactions, but also work out fairly closely the shape, composition and surface of the shells, and from that infer the nature of the originals.

They constitute two distinct, hitherto unknown kinds of animal, furnished with almost all the characters common to the species of the genus Echinus; therefore in their descriptions I have used some of the terms accepted for this genus.

§ 2. Spec. I.

Archiater LINNÉ, *Öländska Resa*, p. 136. Prof. KALM, *Västgöta and Bohus County Journey*, p. 28. In *Mus. Tess.* p. 80, no. 3, and *Syst. Nat.* III p. 179 both species are meant.

DESCRIPTION; compare Fig. 1, 2, 3, Tab. VIII.

BODY globose.

1. In *diameter* usually the length of a finger (rarely of two fingers).

2. Covered with a *skin* thin for its size.

*Swollen* into an oblong hump, small in proportion to the whole; flattened out on every side by a gentle slope; continued into the convex-callous margin of the mouth, which is set at one end of this hump; pierced, on the slope itself, facing the mouth, by the anus (see fig. 1, 2, a). **[p. 243]**

*Built* of very numerous plates (*valvulae*), small in proportion to the whole skin; convex outward, concave inward (a); angular in outline; in one and the same individual varying in size and in number of sides (from III to X, most however VI or V); irregularly disposed (b); mostly inequilateral; distinct on both surfaces of the skin by impressed sutures, fainter outside. Each of these plates has as many others touching it as it has sides.

3. Furnished with two *apertures*, contiguous, small for the size of the animal; of which

a) the *mouth* is pentagonal (with the angles distinct in adult specimens, but worn and rounded in young ones); girt by a convex-callous margin, distinctly pentagonal, one of whose sides is common with the circumference of the anus.

b) The *anus* is oval; separated from the mouth, than which it is a little smaller, only by a narrow rib; **[p. 244]** set on the slope of the hump of the skin.

4. Destitute of *spines*.

5. Furnished without doubt with very numerous *tentacles* (c) (even though the fossil individuals, these being of softer substance and so incapable of petrifaction, cannot but have been entirely deprived of them): for the skin is everywhere pierced by very minute round pores, of which two are always set within a minute pit (*cancellus*) (d), inequilateral-angular, convex at the bottom, mostly oblong and pierced at each end by one of the two pores; separated from the neighbouring pits (which mostly differ little in size, shape and position) by only a very narrow, linear lamina, mostly upright and sharp-edged, more rarely spread out or pressed against the skin and blunt-edged, **[p. 245]** and bristling with very numerous, minute, upright, conical-cylindrical, sharp spinules (e).

> (a) *[p. 243]* What is meant here is a quite different and more visible convexity of each plate separately, beyond that which it must at least have as part of the animal's spherical surface.
>
> (b) This is said by comparison with almost all previously known Echini; for their plates form 20 rows (of which every other pair is broader and every other narrower), which run together at two points, each terminating one end of a diameter of the body.
>
> (c) *[p. 244]* Nothing else is meant here than the soft, tough threads which in all other Echini are attached outside the shell, each over a pair of small holes, and which the animal uses to feel its way and to attach itself to all sorts of other bodies when it wants to lie still.
>
> (d) All Echini discovered hitherto have such pits in the surface of their bony skin, which agree with these in outline, in the convexity of the bottom, and in that the shell within each of them is pierced by two fine holes, one at each end of the pit (which is mostly oblong). These two holes or pores make the communication between the animal's soft parts inside the bony skin and one of its tentacles, whose base occupies the whole pit and consequently covers both holes. This new species indeed has deeper pits, at least in proportion to the size of the shells, than they usually are in all other Echini; but the pits that stand nearest around the mouth in some of the irregular ones agree with these entirely in this too, so that this difference is not essential. But in all previously known species of Echini they stand in regular rows, whereas they occupy the whole surface of this new species and stand close to one another without order.
>
> (e) *[p. 245]* These spines are not like those which the Echini have received to be able to move and to protect their soft tentacles from external injury. The small spines of this species may indeed partly serve the latter purpose, but not at all the former, for they are not movable. The pits also seem, for the preservation of the tentacles, to be deeper in this one than in all previously known kinds of Echini.

§ 3. Spec. II.

Baron TILAS, Sv. Vet. Ac. H. 1740, p. 196, Tab. II, Fig. 18. Prof. J. G. WALLERIUS, *Mineralogie* p. 62, 5) and p. 63, 12). Mine-master CRONSTEDT, *Försök til en ny Mineralogie*, p. 11.

DESCRIPTION (compare Fig. 4 – 9).

BODY globose.

1. In *diameter* usually a finger and a quarter, or a finger and a half, long.

2. Clothed in a thin *skin* (compared with the skins of other Echini of the same size).

*Drawn out* into two beaks (*rostra*), opposite one another on a diameter; unequal in size, and that sometimes so far that one is scarcely any, though the other stands out strongly; closed at the tip; **[p. 246]** conical and at the same time faintly pentagonal; terminated by five plates so disposed and shaped that they make up the tip. Varying in proportion to the whole body as regards size, so that in smaller individuals they are not rarely larger absolutely, and more often, indeed mostly, in proportion to the size of the whole animal, than in larger ones. Of the two, the larger is gradually drawn out, the other more as if abrupt (f).

*Built* of numerous plates; with straight-sided angular margins; in the same individual varying in shape (with III, IV, V up to XIV sides, mostly however VI or V) and in size; slightly concave inside and convex outside, with as many angular sides as the margin of the plate itself has angles (that is III–XIV as said) (g); irregularly disposed (h); each mostly inequilateral; distinct, on both surfaces of the skin, by impressed sutures, fainter outside, which are very fine hollowed lines; swollen at the centre (the larger ones mostly, the smaller ones not rarely) **[p. 247]** into a smooth hemispherical convexity which, if the whole plate for its convex shape be compared to a nipple, answers to its tip.

*Half-pierced* inside by minute, scattered, numerous hollowed points. In those plates whose centre is swollen into a round convexity, the points are more crowded around its margin than next to the margin of the plate, while the disc itself in that case lacks such points altogether. But in those plates that have a simple, even convexity, these points occupy chiefly the disc itself.

*Adorned* outside with a most beautiful sculpture: the centre of each plate is joined to the centre of every plate touching it by a rhombic areole (i), one diameter of which (mostly the longer) runs straight from one of the joined centres to the other, while the other follows the suture of the plates the areole joins. These areoles are striated along the longer diameter; unequal in length, even those that often issue from the same centre, because of the unequal size of the plates and the different distances of the centres arising from it; **[p. 248]** keeled, in some specimens lengthwise (the keel, namely, raised, arched, coinciding with the longer diameter: see T. IX, f. 8, 9), in others crosswise (the keel impressed, straight, following the shorter diameter and the suture of the plates the areole connects), in which case each areole is as it were composed of two triangular planes joined by the impressed keel as a common base (see fig. 6).

3. Furnished with two *apertures*, set not far from each other, always forming a triangle with the lower beak, and that an inequilateral one, because this beak lies nearer to one aperture than to the other. Of these

a) the *mouth* is equilateral-pentagonal, closed most exactly by a lid. This lid is convex (very like in appearance the limpets with angular margin, LINN. Syst. Nat. Tom. I, P. II, p. 1257 **); blunt; of five valves; pentahedral, with the keels or angles blunt and split by a lengthwise slit; the valves equilateral-triangular, arched, smooth, equal (by spreading and drawing together which at will the animal seems able to open and shut the mouth).

b) The *anus* is oval; a little distant from the lower beak, and a little farther from the mouth, than which it is several times smaller.

4. Of *spines* and

5. of *tentacles* destitute.

Most of the individuals of this species that occur in our stratified hills belong to two varieties of it, **[p. 249]** which, compared, differ in the following:

1. In variety α) (see fig. 4, 5, 6, 7) the lower beak is formed from the skin gradually drawn out, and is much larger than the upper, which is as if abrupt. In β) (fig. 8, 9) it is quite the opposite.

2. In α) each beak, or at least one of them, is bounded at the base by a five-lobed outline, within which the surface is smooth, or lacks that sculpture which adorns the rest of the skin's surface — namely the hollowed points inside and the striated areoles connecting the centres of the plates outside; moreover the impressed sutures of the plates within this outline are on both sides so fine that they can scarcely be noticed except with the eye armed. In β) the beaks are adorned on both sides with the same sculpture as the rest of the skin.

3. In α) both beaks are straight; in β) the upper is a little curved.

4. The plates composing the skin of a single individual of variety α) are far more numerous and smaller than those of which the skin of an individual of the same size of variety β) consists. Moreover in single individuals of variety α) the plates vary among themselves more in shape and size than in β). In variety α they are alike over the whole test; in β they decrease in size toward both beaks.

5. The striated areoles that join the centres of the plates on the outer surface are much smaller in variety α) than in β). The cause is that the plates making up the skin of the latter **[p. 250]** much exceed in size those of the former.

I should readily have said that these varieties differ in species, on account of the difference of beaks and plates, which as said is plain enough, had not a variety γ) been at hand at the same time, more rarely met with, of a nature as it were intermediate between them: in which the upper beak is gradually drawn out and much larger than the lower, as in variety β), but the plates are relatively small, and indeed everything else as in variety α).

§ 4. All hitherto known species of the Echinus family agree in this, that each of them is an

ANIMAL molluscous, covered with a

bony SKIN:

α) composed of numerous plates, angular in outline;

β) reticulated on both surfaces by the impressed sutures of these plates;

γ) perforated by two apertures, closable by means of certain bony plates; of which one, serving to take in food and therefore called the mouth, in the living animal mostly faces the bottom, while the other, proper to the voiding of excrement, has a different position in different species;

δ) armed with movable spines, not grown into the test but only tied to it, by means of which the animal performs its movement (*);

ε) pierced by pores arranged in pairs, for governing the tentacles with which the animal, when it wishes to rest, fixes itself to other bodies (*).

> (*) *[pp. 250–251]* Mr de RÉAUMUR has, in the *Mémoires de l'Académie des sciences* for 1712, pp. 136–143, shown in detail for what and in what manner the Echini make use of their spines and pores. The former the animal uses to move itself. Through each of the latter, which are very numerous, it can, he says, push out a tentacle or tough thread, and with these as it were feel its way and fix itself to whatever it lies against. To see this for myself I have examined Echini of several species, such as they had been taken out of the sea (though afterwards dried), having first let them lie in lukewarm water so that the soft parts might swell out and regain their natural shape: but I found that, instead of such a thread being found in every pore according to Mr RÉAUMUR's observation, to each pair of pores answers a single thread, which covers the pair with its base and is attached outside the shell. From this it follows that the number of pores is twice as great as that of the threads, and that the animal cannot draw these within its bony skin. As I moreover, among some thirty distinct kinds of Echini furnished with pores, have not seen a single one on which they were not set in pairs, and the two that made up each pair always sat very close together within a small oval impressed ring, there seems reason to believe that some error crept in during RÉAUMUR's observation. His words are: "Le nombre des petits trous — — est très considérable; j'en ai compté environ 1300: nombre qu'il est bon de savoir, pour connoître combien l'oursin a de cornes. Car chacune de ses cornes tire son origine d'un de ces trous, et réciproquement il n'y a point de trou qui ne donne naissance à une corne." [The number of the little holes is very considerable; I have counted about 1300 of them: a number it is well to know, in order to know how many horns the urchin has. For each of its horns takes its origin from one of these holes, and reciprocally there is no hole that does not give rise to a horn.] The drawings Mr de RÉAUMUR gives, Tab. III, Fig. 5, 6, of the species he actually used for observation, show that its nearest relatives are among the best known species of the genus, and that their nature in this respect really is such as I have just said I found.

**[p. 251]** The characters I have cited above as common to all known species of the Echinus family also hold for the two kinds of animal of which the lime-balls and crystal-apples are petrifactions, **[p. 252]** excepting that neither is furnished with such spines as the Echini use to move with, and that the first-described species is besides without pores, and consequently without tentacles like those with which the Echini try the bottom and fix themselves to other bodies. So it is clear that if these two characters are held necessary in every species of the genus Echinus, the originals of the lime-balls cannot be brought under it; and since they nevertheless agree most nearly with this family, they could still less be referred to any other, but would constitute one of their own. But since the bounds of genera are arbitrary, it is not necessary that the two above-mentioned characters (δ, ε) should form any part of the generic character of Echinus; and if they are left out, the originals of the lime-balls ought altogether to find their place under it.

The descriptions themselves are so arranged that they serve equally well under whatever genus these species may be placed.

If they are taken as species of the Echinus family, the one described in § 2 can easily be distinguished from all previously known species of the genus by *Cute pororum paribus sparsis undique pertusa* [a skin pierced everywhere by scattered pairs of pores]; but the other, whose description makes up § 3, by *cute poris nullis visibilibus pertusa* [a skin pierced by no visible pores]; by which marks both species can also be perfectly distinguished from each other. Although these characters are by no means the only ones by which the prototypes of the lime-balls can be distinguished from all known Echini, more need not and should not be used, since these are now sufficient **[p. 253]** for the purpose, and will remain so until new species are discovered in which the same are also found.

From the descriptions of these species it can be gathered that they are, in several respects, markedly different both from each other and from all known species of Echinus, to which they nevertheless come nearer than to any other kind of animal. Thus there surely remain several species of this family to be discovered, by means of which both these species may be joined, both to each other and to the species of the Echinus family we now know: for *natura non facit saltus*.

For convenience, as well as for conformity with the now accepted way of characterising natural objects, I may perhaps be allowed to mark the former with the name of Echinus Pomum (k), and the latter species with Echinus Aurantium (l).

§ 5. It is wholly unknown in what seas these kinds of animal now live; but without doubt they live under very deep sea-water, for neither they themselves, nor any of the manifold kinds found petrified in their company, are ever cast up on the sea-shores.

Without doubt the petrified animals were bred, lived and died in the same places where their fossilised remains are now found; for if the water had rolled the shells there, gravel and grit of the same weight as they would have come along too; **[p. 254]** but the beds in which lime-balls have hitherto been found consist wholly of a petrified fine silt.

> (k) To retain in some measure the name of crystal-apples, by which both these species have hitherto been marked.
>
> (l) For the likeness of shape to a Pomerans [a bitter orange].

Fossilised remains of Echinus pomum occur in the stratified hills Kinnekulle, Billingen, Mösseberg and Ålleberg, situated in Skaraborg county; in a tract of orthoceratite limestone in Kumla parish and hundred in Närke (where grey limestone is quarried, both for dressing and for burning, in the so-called *halle*-quarries); on Öland, Gotland and several other places. Everywhere in dense grey limestone.

In the stratified hills of Skaraborg county in which beds of orthoceratite limestone (*flo-kalk*) are found, the bed of it that holds petrifactions of Echinus aurantium lies far higher up than that in which Echinus pomum occurs. The latter is called by the stone-cutters of Kinnekulle the *Likhalle*-bed, because only from it can they quarry material fit for gravestones and other large works, such as chimney-cornices, wide stair-stones and the like. The upper side of the bed is rougher than the lower (and is therefore called by the peasants the wrong side), clotted together of nothing but lime-balls and crystal-apples, which are all petrifactions of Echinus pomum and are called by the peasants *kulor* [balls].

Petrifactions of Echinus aurantium are less abundant than those of Echinus pomum. I have found them in Skaraborg county, in the same tracts as these, but hitherto never mixed together in one and the same bed. Each of the stratified hills in that district has, at the boundary between its orthoceratite-limestone and clay-slate beds, one or more layers of a hard, black-grey or black-blue kind of stone **[p. 255]** (composed of silica, lime, iron- and alum-earth), which both surrounds and fills them. In Osmundsberget they are (according to K. Vetensk. Ac. Hand. 1740, l. c.) embedded in loose, brown, bituminous shale, which forms a bed composed of wavy layers; they do not however lie evenly mixed through the whole mass of this bed, but only nearest within its partings, in company with large lime-balls of still unknown nature. Presumably they are found, within the same stratified tract, in some bed of ash-grey dense limestone, for I have seen specimens from there to whose surface splinters of such a kind were still attached.

In all places the shell itself of the fossils of both species is changed to opaque white calc-spar, whose finest laminae within each plate of the shell are set in one and the same way, and that commonly otherwise than in the plates standing nearest around. The effect of this is that when an echinite is forcibly broken loose from the limestone in which it sits, and the petrified shell is thereby as it were split, so that the inner surface stays with the core or filling and the outer with the matrix, and either piece is then turned to and fro against daylight or firelight, the spar glitters within each separate pane or plate in some certain and particular position; without doubt for the same reason as shimmering ores.

The filling in these two kinds of petrifaction is either

α dense orthoceratite limestone, and that for the most part. It is then properly that they are called lime-balls. In as many of the shells as were still whole when the silt, which afterwards hardened into orthoceratite limestone, filled and surrounded them all, **[p. 256]** the filling is always of finer grain than the surrounding limestone, with which otherwise it commonly (m) agrees perfectly in colour, constituents and their proportion; but in those which were then broken, it is in every respect the same as the limestone in which they lie, even when it is studded with shell and coral grit. At first sight it seems very probable that this difference of grain arises from the gentle sifting of the lime-silt into the echinus shells through their openings and pores. But if that were so, the filling in echinites of equal size would always have the same grain, regardless of the different fineness of the surrounding limestone; whereas one finds that it is everywhere, in a certain and equal degree, finer than the limestone in which they lie, so that the difference between the fineness of the echinite fillings in two or more limestone beds of different grain is as great as that between the limestone beds themselves.

β. Often these echinites are also filled with pyramidal calc-spar crystals, and are then called crystal-apples. The pyramids stand at right angles to the inner surface of the shell and meet with their points in the centre of the cavity. They vary in the number of facets from 3 to 14, but most are 5- or 6-cornered, exactly like the plates of which the shell is composed. **[p. 257]** This likeness comes from this, that each plate of the shell is the base of a calc-spar pyramid with as many facets (n) as it itself has sides. **[p. 258]** The figure of the calc-spar is thus determined here by a quite different cause than in all ordinary crystallisation.

Although the six-sided figure is the commonest of the manifold shapes that the calc-spar pyramids in crystal-apples have, it should nevertheless not be regarded as the proper or most natural one, and all the rest as its variations and monstrosities arising from want of sufficient room during the shooting, or from other accidents: for notwithstanding their difference, all the figures occurring here are equally natural, since they are determined by one and the same law, namely that each pyramid has as many facets as its base has sides. Moreover the pyramids have each taken up an equal share of the whole cavity in proportion to the extent of their bases, so that no crowding can have arisen. Each facet of these pyramids, when their points are whole, is an isosceles triangle; consequently not even the six-sided pyramids, much less all the rest, can be referred to that kind of calc-spar crystal called *svin-tänder* [hog's teeth], for the facets of those are scalene. **[p. 259]** What has been said should also suffice to show that these pyramids have wrongly been taken for uniformly or mostly eight-cornered, as someone has done.

γ) But the filling is not always either dense orthoceratite limestone alone or calc-spar pyramids alone, but sometimes both together, each taking up its share of the shell's cavity.

δ) Sometimes the shell is coated inside with a more or less thick crust of calc-spar pyramids, which are either as if cut off square at the ends, or complete, though not so long that the points meet in the centre (o). The space within these is either wholly empty, or crossed by half-clear plates of gypsum-spar which divide it into several (ſ) [the printed letter; the note is lettered (p)]; or filled with scaly yellow *Stahlstein* [spathic iron ore] (t) [the note is lettered (q)], or else with dense grey orthoceratite limestone.

In Osmundsberget their calcareous filling is impregnated with much rock-oil.

> (m) *[p. 256]* In Osmundsberget there are petrifactions of Echinus aurantium that make an exception to this: they are surrounded by loose brown shale, but filled with fine, hard, ash-grey limestone. Governor TILAS, l. c.
>
> (n) *[p. 257]* In other kinds of echinites the calc-spar filling has the same nature. The Archiater LINNÉ has in *Mus. Tess.* p. 94 described, and at Tab. II fig. 9, 9, 9 figured, an echinite in chalk (taken among the shingle on the Gotland shore) under the name Helmintholithus Echinitis, ætitæ embryonibus crystallinis fixis, which was afterwards taken up in *Syst. Nat.* 12th ed., vol. 3, p. 180, and called Ætites (cretaceus) margaceus, embryonibus fixis, crystallinis, muriæ crysolampis. This one is surrounded by fine chalk, which has been broken so that only a part of the concave side of the echinus shell can be seen. Its cavity has begun to fill with spar crystals, and that, by every indication, in the same way as has now been said of our common crystal-apples; namely, that the inner side of each plate has become the base of a spar crystal of equally many-sided figure, and of the same extent as the plate itself.
>
> The calc-spar crystals in the echinites from Stevns Klint, which Mr SØREN ABILDGAARD mentions in his description of that stratified tract, p. 13, and figures at Tab. II f. 1 a and fig. 5, seem also to be of the same nature.
>
> I have seen two kinds of Echini from Balsberget near Kristianstad, which mostly inside and sometimes also outside are coated with a more or less thick crust of clear, yellowish calc-spar, divided by fine but plainly visible cracks into dice, each of which has the same extent and figure as one of the plates of the echinus shell itself, which is its base. Within this calc-spar crust the shells are either empty or filled with the same lumachella of which the whole of Balsberget consists.
>
> In the Court Apothecary ZIERVOGEL's fine collection I have seen very sure proof of the same thing, in a foreign echinite filled with a white shell- *[p. 258]* conglomerate within a coating of the same nature, colour and composition as the one just mentioned in the Balsberg echinites. The plates of that kind are oblong and almost four-sided, but not straight-sided; curved, and so of a figure that cannot belong to the base or the section of any kind of crystal. See fig. 10. But the cracks dividing the calc-spar crust nonetheless follow their sutures, which seems to give my opinion double strength.
>
> (o) *[p. 259]* In a single echinite of the last-named nature, from Mösseberg, I have seen a clear, irregularly six-sided quartz crystal projecting among the calc-spar pyramids. The echinite was a petrifaction of Echinus aurantium, embedded in a kind of stone which, besides lime and silica, also contained some iron- and alum-earth.
>
> (p) (q) These small variations are properly observed on specimens of Echinus pomum from Mösseberg, Kinnekulle and several other places.

Its anus c) sits on the slope of the elevation toward the *os*.

Fig. 3. Shows a piece of the same animal, seen through a microscope.

Fig. 4. Depicts the commonest variety (α) of Echinus Aurantium. a) *Rostrum inferius*. b) *Os*, covered with its five-parted lid. c) *Anus*.

Fig. 5. The same in another position. a) A part of the *rostrum inferius*. b) *Rostrum superius*.

Fig. 6. A segment of the same variety, seen through a microscope. a) *Rostrum inferius*. b) *Os*. c) *Anus*. d) *Glandula valvularum* [the boss of the plates].

Fig. 8 and 9. Show another variety (β) of Echinus aurantium in different positions. a) *Rostrum inferius*. b) *Rostrum superius*.

Conversely, variety α) also occurs with such sculpture as is seen in fig. 8, 9, though more rarely. Variety γ behaves in this and in every other respect like variety α), except that the *rostrum superius* is larger than the *inferius*. So it need not be shown in a separate figure.
