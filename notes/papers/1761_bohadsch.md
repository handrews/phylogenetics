**[PDF p. 11]** The unaccustomed office of teaching Natural History in this most ancient University, most graciously conferred on me eight years ago by the MOST AUGUST EMPRESS, I valued highly, as was fitting; but I also judged and reckoned it to be far beyond my modest powers, and that I should have to strive with all my strength so that I might one day answer, as far as my weakness allowed, to whatever judgment anyone might pass on me. **[PDF p. 12]** Hence I took up the burden laid upon me, and bore it with every effort, so as to show everyone, by the most earnest performance of my duty, how much the memory of so great a favour weighed with me. And so, having discharged the public office year by year, in order to collect objects of every kind from the three kingdoms of nature, so that by furnishing a Museum in this University I might spur more keenly the studies of the best young men, I undertook various journeys through the Kingdom of Bohemia, and at no small expense, certainly, caused the furniture of this kind of natural science to be brought from distant regions. Most recently, lest on account of the raging arms of Mars I should let any rest interrupt my most intense labour, I once more undertook a journey into distant lands, and that into a region by far the richest in natural things. There I beheld very many things with the greatest admiration, gathered many and most beautiful ones, **[PDF p. 13]** and examined, drew and explained some that were less known or not yet described. It remains for me to make these labours of mine, such as they are, these delights of mine, public property; and under whose auspices, there is no reason for me to deliberate. For it was above all by YOUR powerful aid, MOST ILLUSTRIOUS BARON, that I obtained this office which I hold; placing my greatest hope in YOUR kindness, with which you are wont to follow those who make the attempt, I strained every nerve so that I might one day offer YOU some specimen, however slight, of my diligence. Therefore I have thought it consonant both with my devotion and with YOUR celebrated kindness and YOUR love of the Muses, known throughout the world, that I should inscribe YOUR great name upon this present monument, slight though it is. Nor did I wish it to go out into the Republic of Letters otherwise than under those auspices and fortified by that name: so that, while of itself it is worth little **[PDF p. 14]** or nothing at all, yet adorned and strengthened at least by YOUR most splendid name it might bear without danger the great light of letters of our day.

Wherefore receive, I humbly beg, MOST ILLUSTRIOUS BARON, with a gracious heart this work, such as it is. If it be too mean, and not suited to so great a MAN, at least do not spurn the spirit which attempts and desires those things that could render me worthy of the untiring cultivator and fair judge of all the sciences and virtues, and that could win me YOUR further goodwill. You see now, in YOUR infinite wisdom and weightiest judgment, how much my shoulders can bear; which, though it be little, yet hereafter, in proportion to my age and strength, I shall try to bear heavier burdens, and to bring it about that you do not think YOUR benefits ill bestowed on me.

**[PDF p. 15]** **PREFACE.**

I am not the only one whose desire to write a severe criticism ought to weaken, diminish or wholly extinguish. The author of the proceedings which appear every year at Leiden in the French language under the name of the Bibliothèque impartiale argues that I erred greatly in denying, in my rough dissertation on them, that the sea-grape (*uva marina*) is, as commonly held, the eggs of the *sepiae*. But since the author cited does not rebuke that dissertation of mine so severely that all spirit for writing should fail me, I seize the occasion of coming before the public with the present little work all the more gladly, as I am thereby able the more truly and faithfully to confirm the observations made by the celebrated NOZEMANN on the eggs of the *sepia*, to confess my own error, and openly to beg pardon for the injury done to ARISTOTLE. Before, however, I set about this work itself, I see myself bound: first, to render everlasting thanks to the author of the Bibliothèque impartiale for his kindly judgment of my feebleness, which shortly after the beginning of his critique, p. 434, he expresses in the following words: "Nevertheless the author of this dissertation, who distinguishes himself very advantageously by his fine knowledge in natural history and in the different branches of medicine, has the courage to rise up against all his authorities."

**[PDF p. 16]** The second thing is this: I most lovingly ask that no one to whose lot it falls to read this little work, such as it is, take it ill if he finds it written with a less than polished pen.

Now I shall briefly recall what concerns my dissertation on the eggs of the *sepiae*. Ever since I read, with the greatest delight, in the Bibliothèque impartiale that the celebrated NOZEMANN had demonstrated before several most weighty men that the sea-grape is the true eggs of the *sepia*, I anxiously wished to find an opportunity of confirming the truth of the matter by my own experience; and this chiefly because the celebrated NOZEMANN himself has not laid his observations before the learned world in any particular dissertation, but has only supplied a few words on the subject to the author of the Bibliothèque impartiale. Wherefore I often thought of seizing the pen and playing the advocate in my own cause: for the fact that with my own eyes, in so many sea-grapes examined, I had not detected a single embryo of a *sepia* so occupied and tormented my mind that I could neither give ready credence to the assertions of the celebrated NOZEMANN, nor yet wished boldly to deny his observations. For at the same time a certain suspicion arose in my mind that the gelatinous catkins which I had passed off as the eggs of the *sepiae* were the eggs of the *loligo*, as RUYSCH (\*) had once indicated. Hence I hoped to be able to obtain at least this way of escape, after the manner of the jurists: that the title of my dissertation, which the author of the Bibliothèque impartiale chiefly attacks, **[PDF p. 17]** was not groundlessly chosen.

> (\*) *[PDF p. 16]* See Thes. Animal. I. p. 8. N. XXX. Tab. 2. Fig. 1.

For according to the celebrated LINNAEUS, *sepia* is a generic name, under which *Polypus*, *loligo* and the *sepia* properly so called are contained. But the title of my dissertation is likewise generic, namely "on the eggs of the *sepiae*": whence it can be understood either of the *Polypus* or of the *loligo*, or of the *sepia* itself properly so called. Since, then, it is established from the observations of RUYSCH and of the celebrated NOZEMANN that the catkins I described are the eggs of the *loligo*, my dissertation is to be understood only of that species of the *sepiae* which comes to the curious in nature under the name of *loligo*.

That I spoke generically of the eggs of the *sepiae* anyone can gather from these words of mine: *Now I readily hope that, even though I am silent, you understand that the celebrated NEEDHAM had only a suspicion about the eggs from the sight of the scarlet spots in the loligo, and by no means knew the eggs as laid by the fish and as they are cast out of the sea.* A little further on I put forward this: *Now I shall say what sort of eggs of the sepiae I have found.*

Moreover, in the same §, p. 13, these words of mine are to be read: *This, however, which I dare not pass over, I have read to my great consolation, that the celebrated NEEDHAM observed in the female loligo ovaries not unlike those which I have undertaken to describe.* If, therefore, I myself openly confessed that the ovaries of the *loligo*, seen by the celebrated NEEDHAM only in the womb of the mother, were similar to the ovaries described by me, and nevertheless called these the eggs of the *sepiae*, anyone can conclude either that my discourse about them was generic, or that I described outright **[PDF p. 18]** the eggs of the *loligo* under the generic name of the *sepiae*. Nor was I at that time at all able to treat of them specifically, both because I understood nothing from the writings of ARISTOTLE as to which species of *sepia*, properly, he took the sea-grape to be the ovary of; and because in embryos so tiny, which lay hidden in the gelatinous catkins, I could distinguish nothing beyond the habit common to the genus of the *sepiae*, whether the aforesaid catkins were eggs of the *Polypus*, of the *loligo*, or indeed of the *sepia* properly so called. Whence I was persuaded that I should by no means stray from the right path if I set a generic title, "on the eggs of the *sepiae*", upon my dissertation: in this way I could surely neither deceive nor be deceived, being certain that they were the ovaries of one of the three species of *sepia*. Indeed I seem to have committed no error in this way, or else the celebrated NEEDHAM erred in the same manner: for he (\*) treats of the eggs of the *Raja* in such a way that it comes into the reader's mind that the author is persuaded either that there is only a single species of *Raja*, or that all species of *Raja* deposit a similar ovary. But whatever the celebrated author supposed, he supposed it erroneously. For it is known to all that there are several species of *Raja*: whence he would have performed a service most dear to the curious in nature had he named the species whose egg he treated. That not all species of *Raja* deposit a similar egg you will perceive from Chapter IX of this little work, and at the same time you will understand that the celebrated RUYSCH was caught in the same error as the celebrated NEEDHAM, **[PDF p. 19]** since in exhibiting an egg of a *Raja* (\*) he too describes it under the generic name of *Raja*. In this, however, he did better, in that he gave a figure of the same *Raja* whose ovary he explained in a brief description.

> (\*) *[PDF p. 18]* See Nouvelles découvertes faites avec le microscope. p. 108.
>
> (\*) *[PDF p. 19]* See Thes. Anat. I. Tab. III. Fig. IV.

And these are the things which I had often thought to myself ought to be answered to the author of the Bibliothèque impartiale. But when I considered a little more maturely that it does not become a philosopher to play the advocate, I desisted from this trifling self-justification, and patiently awaited a more fitting occasion either of attacking my assailant or of confessing my error. And behold, what Pallas denied for several years, Mars unexpectedly offered. For when four years ago, throughout the whole of Germany, the bloodiest arms threatened almost everyone with destruction, and were troubling the Muses, languishing as they were in any case, in our most ancient University, I resolved, a fugitive by fate, to examine the various shores of Italy. On which occasion not only did I find the most weighty and most celebrated men GRANDI at Venice, MORGAGNI at Padua, MONTI at Bologna and GIRALDI at Rome, formerly my most honoured teachers, in the best flower of health, which greatly consoled me; but also in the sea of Naples, which was my principal aim, I detected and collected several marine bodies less known. To these is to be added the sea-grape, in whose grains, on the 23rd of July, for the first time, when I drew off the black rind, I saw young *sepiolae* swimming, **[PDF p. 20]** a most delightful spectacle. All the parts of each *sepiola*, swimming in the mucilaginous liquor, were so perfect that no one who has ever seen a larger *sepia* could have any doubt that these were true embryos of the *sepia*, and consequently that the sea-grape is its ovary.

I congratulate myself, therefore, as much as possible, that by my own experience I have obtained the means of correcting the error I committed when, in my rough dissertation, I tried to thrust the ovaries of the *loligo* upon the learned world as the eggs of the *sepia*. But I earnestly ask all good men not to impute this fault to my negligence, still less to an ill-disposed mind, but to ascribe it to shortness of time and to the want of a fitting opportunity of examining the sea-grape. Indeed, in the years 1748 and 1749, examining several sea-grapes, as I recalled in my dissertation, I found no *sepiolae* in them, nor could I find any, since in the former year I made my observations at Naples in the month of March, when the embryos are immature, and in the latter year in the month of August, when the embryos have already gone out, in Holland.

This error of mine, if it is one, offended ARISTOTLE most of all, whom I accused of having handed down to memory false things about the eggs of the *sepiae*. But my recent observations have taught me that he wrote the truth on this matter; hence I beg pardon with these words for the injury done to that excellent old man: and I beseech all who desire to become known to the world by their writings in natural history **[PDF p. 21]** not to be hasty in denying what the Ancients have left written about any natural body whatever, unless they have first themselves examined it repeatedly and at every season of the year; and only when that has been done, let them communicate to the learned world what truth and what falsehood the Ancients have handed down to us. And in this way I hope that within a short time we shall arrive at a most ample and true knowledge of things. For which reason, too, I have no doubt that my error will be counted as nothing, since through it I have uncovered a truth about which I myself should have been in doubt to this very day, had I not given the celebrated NOZEMANN the occasion of confirming what ARISTOTLE wrote on the eggs of the *sepia*, and had I not myself most recently found by experience that the celebrated NOZEMANN's observations are true.

But someone will ask me what I have done with my dissertation on the eggs of the *sepiae*. I shall say: I have followed the advice of the author of the Bibliothèque impartiale, who at the end of Article VIII exclaims: "Mr. Bohadsch's work will win general approval if in a second edition he entitles it *Dissertation on the true eggs of the Squid*." I have not, however, put a separate dissertation on the true eggs of the *loligo* to the press; but have comprised this matter in a single chapter only of this book.

The marine bodies that are described in the remaining chapters are either such as are not found treated by others, or such as have in the writings of other authors **[PDF p. 22]** only figures that are less elegant and do not correspond to the natural structure, and descriptions that are merely brief and less accurate.

The figures are engraved on copper plates, after drawings made with my own hands from the living animals. In measuring the animals and their parts I used the Paris foot. Both the animals themselves and their parts I drew, as far as could be done, in their natural size, so that the form of each might be the more clearly set before the eyes.

I did not use a microscope in investigating minute details, because I had brought none with me, not foreseeing that I should be making observations of this kind. Moreover, I could very well discern the configuration of all the parts, with the naked eye or with the eye armed with a lens, to be such as I have expressed in the figures. Wherefore let no one wonder that the tentacles of the fourth *Penna* as drawn by me differ much from those which others have set before us. Perhaps when observed with the microscope they appear such as we have them noted by SCHAEFFER after MARSIGLI; yet considered with the naked eye they are furnished with no other form than that which I have given. If, therefore, kind reader, you do not find these labours of mine, such as they are, complete in every respect, grant pardon, and impute the defect to a mind disquieted by time of war, and consider that in the four months' space during which I stayed at Naples in the year 1757 I could not, however gladly I wished it, institute all the necessary observations upon the marine animals.

**[p. 54]** **CHAPTER II. ON THE FIMBRIA.**

**§. I.** [Tab. V. Figura 1.] The marine animal that is now to be described was brought into my hands on the 10th of August, under a most burning sun. It shines everywhere with whiteness, except for the margin of the lip, and reaches six inches in length. The lip *a.*, spread out in the anterior part of the head like a fringed membrane, is no small ornament of the animal; it equals four and a half inches in breadth, but does not exceed three inches in length. Its margin *b.* is crenate on both sides, and thicker than the remaining portion of the lip, above which it stands out on both sides, just like a gold or silver fringe set round a cap. Whence it also seems to be something other than a continuation of the membrane that makes up the remaining portion of the lip. The colour of the fringed margin is variegated black and yellow, so that its inner crenate portion is black, marked with some yellow dots, while the portion opposite to this, also crenate, is wholly blackish; the intermediate part shines as if with a golden colour. This elegant variety of colours is observed only on that side of the margin which corresponds to the prone position of the animal, for on the opposite side the whole margin is painted black. The membrane that makes up the rest of the body of the lip is compacted of thick, white, almost tendinous fibres.

**[p. 55]** **§. II.** At the beginning of the head, namely where the lip just described takes its origin, two ear-shaped tentacles *c. c.* rise up, flat, broad, furnished with no sinus or cavity, four lines broad and six lines long, which in their shape not unaptly recall the ears of a hunting dog. No eyes occur visible at the root of these tentacles, nor in any other part of the head; and if there were any, it would not have been impossible to detect them, at least with a lens, in a body so very white.

**§. III.** Behind the ear-shaped tentacles the back *d.* takes its beginning, which, gradually growing slender, presents a pyramid or cone; its length comprises three inches and six lines, and the base equals one inch and two lines in diameter.

The sides of the back are adorned with a double row of fleshy white appendages *e. e. e. e.*, which have partly a conical, partly a cylindrical form. The largest of these appendages are five lines long, the smallest two. Besides these, various protuberances *f. f. f.* are observed on the back, from which commonly one or another appendage sprouts. But whether this diverse shape and size of the appendages is natural to the animal I cannot affirm, since I did not receive it until it had already been dead for 24 hours; nor afterwards did another living one come into my hands. Wherefore these could have been mutilated by some injury or other, and others wholly destroyed. On either side of the back other larger tubers *g. g.* are placed, **[p. 56]** which are portions of the abdomen, which is much wider than the back.

**§. IV.** [Figura 2.] When our animal is presented to the eyes on its supine side, the following come into view: two inches and two lines below the margin of the lip, a tubular mouth *a.*, surrounded on all sides by a thick membrane *b.* seven lines long and thirteen lines broad. This membrane, covering or rather surrounding the mouth like a lip, passes in its upper part into an acute sinus, and acquires a heart-shaped figure *c.* From the lower portion *d.* of the aforesaid membrane as far as the beginning of the abdomen *e.* runs the neck, one inch and five lines broad. On the right side of the neck, when the animal is considered placed on its prone side, but on the left if you look at the animal supine, a little below the fringed lip the external genital parts are met with: above is the opening through which the penis *f.* goes out; three lines below this the aperture of the vagina *g.* is situated.

[Figura 3.] The penis, solid, thin and conical, is furnished near the base with a testicle-like prominence *a.*, and is provided with no urethra and no half-canal such as the penis of the *lernaea* has.

From the end of the neck right to the extremity extends the oval abdomen, which is hollow only in that part which is marked by a darker colour. This colour, darker even in the animal itself, is caused by the viscera showing through the integument, which is thinner in this place. The whole integument, however, is white outside and inside, and is constructed of innumerable thicker and thinner fibres disposed in a network, **[p. 57]** just as I recalled in the preceding chapter that the coverings of the *lernaea* are made. All the aforesaid fibres are muscular, and on the abdomen various furrows run between them, by which means the abdomen of the *fimbria* is somewhat like the foot of a slug, and by its help I judge that the animal sometimes adheres to rocks and other hard bodies. On the back, the neck and the lip furnished with its fringed margin the integument is smooth and shining, except for the margin, which appears as if sprinkled with a very fine coloured powder, and within is not constructed of fibres loosely disposed in a network, but is compacted of a solid substance, which the black colour visible on the outside penetrates for the most part.

**§. V.** When the abdomen is opened, in its upper part a little below the neck, first of all a large uterus, yellowish white, comes into view; below this is placed a subglobose ovary, to which a tortuous whitish vessel, or Epididymis, lies adjacent. The oesophagus, proceeding from the mouth, ends in a stomach shaped like a liripipe, constructed of white fleshy fibres, which is situated toward the back. From the stomach the intestines proceed, twisted into various coils, which a greenish liver accompanies on all sides. Finally a testicle occupies the lowest part of the abdominal cavity, from which the above-mentioned tortuous vessel tends toward the uterus.

I did not hold it necessary to express in figures the form of the internal parts of the *Fimbria*, because they are not unlike those which I said are contained in the *lernaea*. Yet that some viscera are wanting in this animal, with which the *lernaea* has been endowed by nature, will be plain to anyone comparing the one **[p. 58]** with the other. For there is lacking in the *Fimbria* the second stomach, the shell-shaped mouth, the lungs. I say nothing of the heart, vessels and nerves, which perhaps escaped my eyes.

**§. VI.** Our *Fimbria* lives in the deep sea, where it is seen only under the most burning sun, and is caught in nets together with the fishes. Hence it must be fished when it is free and wandering through the sea waves, since the fishermen do not approach the rocks with their nets when they wish to catch fish. For the rest, however, I have no doubt that it, like the *lernaea*, is sometimes fixed to rocks or to the sandy or clayey bottom of the sea, and now and then withdraws from there either of its own accord or by the force of the waves.

Various kinds of *fuci* supply food to our animal, as far as could be understood from the contents of the stomach. That it does not feed on little snails or shells I conjecture from this: that it lacks the second stomach armed with teeth, and is moreover provided with a toothless mouth. But it can easily digest the tender fibres of *fuci*, which in a stomach, even a most tender one, macerated by the inflowing sea-water and by homogeneous humours, almost spontaneously dissolve.

It is used by no one for food, although no noxious virtue is attributed to it by the fishermen. But if I had to conjecture from its structure whether it could be taken as food without harm, I should not fear to answer in the affirmative. For the venom-bearing gland is lacking in it, and no odour so nauseous as in the *lernaea* is perceived, such as could provoke vomiting in the more delicate. Add to this that all animals, **[p. 59]** even the most venomous, may be eaten without harm if their venom-bearing parts have first been removed, as the use of vipers and other serpents abundantly shows. Indeed there are those who assert that it is useless to take such precautions, since animals teeming with venom can harm man only when alive, and then only when they are inflamed with anger. Nevertheless I readily admit that the *Fimbria* would be of more difficult digestion because of its fibrous texture.

**§. VII.** Those who are familiar with the writers on natural history will easily understand that I have only added a new name to that animal which was once described by RONDELET, FABIO COLONNA and ALDROVANDI under the third species of *lepus marinus*. Yet I think this labour undertaken by me on that account is by no means useless, both because I have tried to give a better figure of this animal, and because I wished to make plain to the curious in nature that it is an entirely different genus of animals from what the cited authors have taught us. And so that the truth of this assertion may shine out more clearly, it is fitting that I adduce some words of the aforesaid authors. RONDELET (\*) offers the following: *The third kind of lepus marinus is similar in substance, powers and faculties, for which reason we have thought it should be numbered among the lepores marini.* From this it is now plain why RONDELET called our animal a *lepus marinus*: namely because it seemed to him to agree with it in *substance, powers and faculties*. But on how feeble a foundation these generic characters rest, **[p. 60]** everyone knows who considers that in this age all known bodies are far better distributed into classes, orders, genera and species according to a definite figure and position of the external parts.

> (\*) *[p. 59]* Cap. XI. the third species of *lepus marinus*.

Substance and powers, therefore, are by no means able to reduce to the same genus two animals distinct as to the structure of their external parts. Nay more, if it pleased RONDELET to reduce several animals enjoying the same substance and the same virtues to the same genus, why, pray, did he not make the whole genus of the *sepia* a species of *lepus marinus*, which in *substance* surely agrees with the *lepus marinus* of the Ancients as much as our *Fimbria* does, and both the *Fimbria* and all the species of *sepia* are of difficult digestion? For as to *powers* and *faculties* I deny that the *Fimbria* agrees with the *lepus marinus* of the Ancients, and that for the reasons brought forward above. Nor did RONDELET himself find it to be such, as may be understood from the following: *Finally, I judge this to have the same powers, but weaker, which by the opinion of the Ancients and by our own experience we have attributed to the first kind.* From which it is at once evident that RONDELET himself acknowledged some difference as to powers between the *lepus marinus* and our *Fimbria*, *since he judges the latter to be of weaker powers*. Moreover, that RONDELET's opinion about the venomous virtues of the third species of *lepus marinus*, or our *Fimbria*, is weak, his following words teach: *By its very disagreeable and fishy odour it provokes nausea.* Is not the odour of all fishes disagreeable and nauseous? and how many distinct genera, pray, do fishes constitute, even according to RONDELET? which, although they agree among themselves in this property, are by no means venomous on that account.

**[p. 61]** ALDROVANDI (\*) also makes a third species of *lepus marinus* out of the *Fimbria*, as far as I could understand from the figure given by him. He gave such a description of it that one would conjecture he had indicated some other animal rather than the *Fimbria*: from which, lest I seem to fill the present chapter with the words of others, let it suffice to cite only the following: *It has seven appendages crowded with innumerable little proboscides, verging from indigo into blue, with which by sucking it supplies food to the body.* I found no appendages crowded with proboscides around the mouth of the *Fimbria*, nor do I know that any author has observed them, except BELON, whose history of this third species of *lepus marinus* ALDROVANDI recited. If, however, these proboscides are present in the animal whose figure ALDROVANDI gave, it is clear even from these that it differs very greatly from the genus of *lepus marinus*.

> (\*) *[p. 61]* De Mollibus L. I. c. VII.

**§. VIII.** Since, therefore, it has in this way been sufficiently demonstrated, as I think, that the *Fimbria* belongs neither according to RONDELET and ALDROVANDI, nor according to my description, such as it is, in §§ I, II, III and IV, to the genus of the *lepus marinus* of the Ancients or of the *lernaea* of LINNAEUS, I see the less need to occupy myself in adducing further arguments to confirm it, as I am held by the greater hope that there will be no one who, having even only compared the figures of the *lernaea* and the *Fimbria*, would not recognise that a great difference lies between the two. But because, notwithstanding this, someone might object that the *Fimbria* is very well reduced to one and the same genus with the *lernaea*, since both are furnished with two ear-shaped tentacles, **[p. 62]** and the number and form of the tentacles constitute, in the mind of the celebrated LINNAEUS, the generic character, I have judged that I must still strive to weaken this objection in some manner by what follows. The celebrated LINNAEUS does indeed chiefly consider the number of tentacles in establishing the genera of *Zoophyta*; yet he does not seem to take this as an absolute characteristic mark. For if one cares to examine his generic characters (\*) a little more attentively, it will appear that the celebrated man, in determining the genera of *Zoophyta*, also paid very great attention to the figure of the whole body and to the apparatus of other parts. Thus according to him *Asterias* and *medusa* are two distinct genera, because the former has a radiate body, the latter an orbicular body and moreover central folds, although each is furnished with *very many tentacles*. Whence, even if the *lernaea* and the *Fimbria* have obtained from nature two ear-shaped tentacles, yet on that account, even in the mind of the celebrated LINNAEUS, they can by no means be comprehended under the same genus: for the celebrated author, as I recalled in the preceding chapter, asserts that the *lernaea* has a *terete body and a perforated forehead*, a configuration of parts which is not observed in the *Fimbria*; I say nothing of the fact that the form of the tentacles differs very greatly in the two *Zoophyta*, so that this difference alone would be able to divide one genus from the other, just as the botanists very often separate one plant from another on account of the form of the parts of fructification.

> (\*) *[p. 62]* See Systema naturae edit. VI.

Since, therefore, the easily acknowledged Prince of the curious in nature had regard to several parts and to their varied form in establishing the genera of animals, let me be allowed to follow in the celebrated man's footsteps, and, because the body **[p. 63]** of the *Fimbria* is formed otherwise than the body of the *lernaea*, to propose each as a distinct genus of *Zoophyta*, and to assign the generic character of each. The celebrated LINNAEUS, as I recalled a little before, established the characteristic mark of the *lernaea* in its *terete body and perforated forehead* (\*): but that this does not fit the *lernaea* I demonstrated in the preceding chapter, and there in § XX I endeavoured to define it otherwise, yet after the manner of the celebrated LINNAEUS. If, however, that laconic expression of the generic character of the *lernaea* should not seem to someone complete enough, perhaps the following definition of it will please more: *Lernaea is a genus of Zoophyta provided with a subovate body, a lipped lung-bearing back, two tentacles on the forehead rivalling the ears of a mouse, and a mouth placed longitudinally. Fimbria, however, is a genus of Zoophyta provided with an oblong body, a pyramidal back furnished with pointed appendages, two tentacles on the forehead rivalling the ears of a hunting dog, a large fringed lip and a channelled mouth.*

> (\*) *[p. 63]* See Systema nat. edit. VI.

**§. IX.** Having in this way given a definition both of the *lernaea* and of the *Fimbria*, there will be no one, I hope, who will not go over to my opinion that each is a distinct genus of animals, or, to speak systematically, of *Zoophyta*. And since I undertook to demonstrate this above all in this chapter, I would now put an end to it, were it not that I must at last give the reason why I have called the *Zoophyton* here described *Fimbria*, and must remove another doubt that may easily arise in the minds of some. I shall now dispatch both very briefly: and as to the name imposed on the *Zoophyton* drawn in Tab. V. Fig. 1, anyone will perceive, even if I am silent, **[p. 64]** that it is taken from its *fringed lip*. Although this is not sufficiently apt, it nevertheless seemed the best, since some name had to be given to a new genus of animals, because it indicates the configuration of at least one part.

The doubt, however, which the following words of RONDELET, *what you see in about the middle of the supine part is the mouth; the part placed above the mouth is a trough, of the shape of an egg as in the sepia, but crenate round the edge*, could easily raise for the reader, is this: that perhaps I have taken for the mouth the trough of the *Fimbria* which RONDELET likened to the funnel of the *sepia*, and hence have wrongly put *tubular mouth* in its definition, since according to the author cited the trough is such. To guard against this doubt, therefore, I can assert with the utmost certainty that I could discover no other aperture on the supine part of the *Fimbria*, however much I tried, that could perform the office of a mouth. And in order to prove more firmly that the little tube described in § III is the mouth of the *fimbria*, it is necessary that I adduce the following experiment: when I gently introduced a probe into the little tube, and then dissected the abdomen, the probe appeared in the liripipe-shaped stomach. Whence, although I myself am not able to assert in what part of the *Fimbria* the faeces have their exit, yet I have no doubt at all about the aforesaid little tube, that it is the mouth of our *Zoophyton*.

**[p. 75]** **CHAPTER IV. ON THE HYDRA.**

**§. I.** I do not wish to bring on the stage here the apocalyptic *Hydra* described by St. John in Chapters XII and XIII, as being an animal that does not belong to natural history: but I intend to elucidate the *Zoophyton* which indeed does not possess seven necks and as many heads, as the *Hydra* is said to have, but which, being furnished with several tentacles on the head, somehow recalls it. I say elucidate, because the same *Zoophyton* has already been treated and drawn by other authors; but since a rather obscure description and a dissimilar drawing of it are found in all of them, the desire for a true knowledge of all things has led me, reckoning that a dish once cooked can be cooked again without nausea, to take upon myself the history of the *Hydra* as well.

**§. II.** All who have spoken of this animal before the celebrated LINNAEUS either called it *mentula*, or attached to it another name signifying the same: thus APULEIUS (\*) calls it *Veretillum*, VALLISNERI (\*\*) *Priapus marinus*, and the Italians *cazzo di mare*. RONDELET alone reduces it to the *Holothorium*: but the celebrated LINNAEUS (\*\*\*) imposed the name *Hydra* on our *Zoophyton*, which indeed is a generic name, **[p. 76]** comprehending the *Mentula* and the *polypus paludosus* as species. In my view the *polypus palustris* constitutes a genus of its own, and on account of its singular properties and different form is not to be reduced to the *Hydra*. For this, according to the celebrated LINNAEUS (\*), has *a cylindrical body, several tentacles on the circumference of the head*, a character which fits the *Mentula* of the Ancients very well, but by no means all the species of the *polypus palustris* of the Moderns. Whence I could wish that henceforth only the *Mentula* and the species of it yet to be discovered were understood under the name of *Hydra*. Yet I observe that the celebrated LINNAEUS has done the contrary of this wish of mine in the tenth edition of the Systema naturae (\*\*); for under the genus *Hydra* he has placed only various species of the *polypus paludosus*, and has forgotten the *mentula marina* in the whole animal system.

The celebrated JANUS PLANCUS (\*\*\*) indeed mentions *a certain Mentula marina resembling a dog's penis*: this, however, is neither the *Mentula* in the sense of the Ancients, nor, as the celebrated author thinks, does it constitute a *Thethyum*. May the celebrated man, on whose friendship, gained through his singular genius and the supreme erudition in which he excels, I congratulate myself most heartily, forgive me that I must say he has somewhat erred in this matter: the love of truth and the pursuit of a certain knowledge of things impel me to it. For on this occasion I do not wish to detract in the least from the celebrated man's fame and learning, but only to point out that which has happened to many of the curious in nature, both ancient and modern, and in which lies the sole reason why to this day we have a defective and uncertain **[p. 77]** history of very many marine animals: namely, that such marine bodies have been described and drawn either dead or quite dried up. I conjecture that the celebrated PLANCUS did one of the two, from the description of his *Mentula* and from the figure he gave of it. The words of the celebrated author are these: *For the Holothurium is a body callous indeed and leathery, free from the rocks, while the Tethyum clings to them. Wherefore, since the body expressed in Plate V and Figure V, which we found among the sweepings of the sea, shows at its extremity certain fibres by which it appeared to have adhered to the rocks, we have referred it to the Tethya. It is of a leathery nature; about it we add nothing further, since the title and the figure set forth more than sufficiently what it is and how it is.* Now if the body drawn in Plate V and Fig. V was found among the refuse of the sea, it may easily be supposed that it was at least dead, if not entirely dried up; especially because the celebrated man himself makes no mention of its life. But if it was dead, with what certainty can our excellent PLANCUS assert that it was a *Mentula* or a *Tethyum*, since such animals, as I and others have often been able to observe, change their form and outward appearance, and many parts in them when dead are obliterated which appeared pleasingly in the living? The celebrated *author* does indeed conjecture, from certain fibres which were seen at one extremity of the same body, that it had adhered to the rocks, and therefore belongs to the genus of the *Tethya*. But granted that the body drawn by the celebrated PLANCUS in Tab. V. Fig. V is a *Tethyum*, it cannot on that account at the same time be a *Mentula*. For this and the *Tethyum* constitute two distinct genera even according to RONDELET, from whom our author took the difference between the *Holothurium* and the *Tethyum*.

**[p. 78]** Moreover, it by no means follows that the body so often mentioned adhered to the rocks because some fibres hung from one of its extremities: for how many other *Zoophyta*, pray, are there not which have several fibres hanging from the body, and yet never adhere to the rocks, nor are on that account called *Tethya*? What if I were to demonstrate from the celebrated PLANCUS's own figure that the body described by him was neither a *Mentula* nor a *Tethyum* but a *Holothurium*? Does not that go by the name of *Holothurium* with the celebrated man which has a *leathery substance* and is *free from the rocks*? But the body drawn by him is *leathery*, therefore on this side a *Holothurium*; but it is also free from the rocks, therefore on the other side too it is to be referred to the *Holothurium*.

I see that the last proposition will readily be denied by all; yet if anyone wishes to have it more surely proved, let him consult the following chapter on the *Tethyum*, where I recall that there is a *Tethyum fasciculatum*, united by fibres running between its members, and free from the rocks. But a leathery body free from the rocks goes by the name of *Holothurium* with the celebrated PLANCUS; therefore his *Tethyum* is, according to him, a *Holothurium*. And that the celebrated PLANCUS's simple body was loosed from a *Tethyum* similar to my fasciculate one, everyone will agree, I hope, who has compared my figure of the *Tethyum fasciculatum* with the celebrated PLANCUS's Figure V, by which he designates his *mentula*. This at least has happened to me, that I have quite often found a simple *Tethyum* loosed from the rest, at one extremity of which also some fibres were visible. If notwithstanding this my comparison or suspicion is erroneous, the celebrated PLANCUS will grant pardon, and will perhaps willingly concede that from his very brief description of his *Mentula* I could hardly make a better one. For I doubt very much **[p. 79]** whether anyone can recognise from the *title* and *figure* alone of any thing *what it is and how it is*. This indeed is true: that from the figure of the *Tethyum* given by the celebrated man I recognised that it was not a *Mentula*, because I have very often seen the latter in its natural state. But he to whom this opportunity has been denied will by no means be able to form an idea of the *Mentula* from the figure given by the celebrated man. Although, therefore, I recognised in this way what the said body is, yet I could by no means perceive how it is: for it may have a flat, cylindrical or terete form, or be painted blue, red, yellow or some other colour, and be furnished with six hundred other affections which the figure given by no means expresses. But lest I seem to have undertaken rather to lash the celebrated PLANCUS with severe criticism than to describe the *Mentula* or *Hydra*, I now set about the work I have proposed to myself.

**§. III.** [Tab. VI. Figura 1.] The *Hydra*, when dead, commonly equals eight inches in length; while it is in full life it either extends its body beyond the length of a foot, or contracts it almost into a ball. Its figure is cylindrical, and everywhere attains an inch and some lines in diameter. The back *a.*, tinged with a dusky colour, is proud with various pyramidal fleshy papillae, whose base is also dusky, but the apex whitish. They are observed of two sizes: the larger *b. b. b. b.* run along the length of the back on either side to the number of fourteen, so that one is six lines distant from another when the *hydra* is somewhat contracted; but when it is elongated the intermediate space easily extends to eight lines. Other similar ones are placed here and there **[p. 80]** with no order kept. The smaller *c. c. c. c.* are likewise scattered without order in every direction over the surface of the back. From all of them a whitish mucus is drawn out, serving to smear the body. Whence the aforesaid papillae seem to be so many glands, furnished with an excretory duct, whose aperture is so tiny that I could not see it with the help of a common lens. Moreover, that they are provided with various muscles follows from this, that the *Hydra* can erect and obliterate them at will. [Figura 2.] When the larger papillae are fully erected, their axis attains three lines, and the diameter of the base as many.

[Figura 1.] The belly, or the part of the *Hydra* opposite to the back, *d.*, is whitish out of dusky, and is beset on all sides with cylindrical tentacles *e. e. e. e.*; these tentacles are present in such number that scarcely the head of a small pin could be placed between them. [Figura 3.] Their diameter scarcely exceeds a line, but their length comprises four lines. They shine with a white colour, except the apex, which is dusky, and formed like a sucker. By the help of these tentacles the *Hydra*, dwelling on the bottom of the sea, makes its body fast, lest it be easily torn away by storms, which would surely happen the more frequently, since this genus of *Zoophyta* lives by the shores of the sea, where the waters scarcely rise to a height of six feet. Wherefore I have very often seen it from a boat, with the greatest pleasure, adhering firmly to the bottom of the sea. But if it adheres to other bodies by the help of the ventral tentacles, the apex of these must have the form of a sucker, just as the *sepiae*, the *Echini* and the *Asteriae* have their tentacles constructed, with which they lay hold of any other body.

**[p. 81]** From this position of the *Hydra* on the bottom of the sea, which it also keeps when placed in a vessel filled with sea-water, it is open to anyone that I have not rashly determined its back and belly, which otherwise in a cylindrical body would surely have been very hard to do. But since all animals uniformly walk on the belly, or rest upon it, and the *Hydra* likewise looks toward the ground with that part of the body on which the *cylindrical tentacles* are disposed, it is altogether clear that this part is the abdomen or belly of our *Zoophyton*.

For the rest, the ventral tentacles as well as the dorsal are thrust out or obliterated at the animal's pleasure. Hence it is no light conjecture that they are provided with elevating and depressing muscles; and this most of all because all the aforesaid tentacles vanish after death; whence at the same time it becomes known that all the curious in nature have given the figure of a dead *Hydra*, since they have expressed none of its tentacles. I remain in doubt whether the celebrated LINNAEUS too did not establish the generic character upon a dead *Hydra*, since he makes no mention of these tentacles.

**§. IV.** That part of the body in animals in which the mouth is situated is called the head, as worms and caterpillars abundantly teach: in the *Hydra*, therefore, that extremity in which the orbicular mouth *f.* is seen among the branched tentacles *g. g. g. g.* is also to be saluted as its head, although no eyes and no other distinctive marks of a head occur in it. A fringe *h. h.*, membranous, thin, laciniate, dusky, embraces it, whose laciniae, equal, cuspidate and wedge-shaped, **[p. 82]** make up the greatest part of the fringe, and surround the head like a collar when the *hydra* spreads out its branched tentacles and wishes to seize food. But when it is struck with terror, the said tentacles are drawn back within the body together with the mouth, the laciniae of the fringe are raised, come together, and so cover the mouth together with the tentacles that no trace of any aperture remains, nor is anything seen of the tentacles or of the laciniae.

[Figura 4.] Next to the laciniate fringe twenty branched tentacles *g. g. g. g.*, recalling the head of a *medusa*, are disposed in a circle; these have a cylindrical body three lines long and a line thick, on which sit thin linear laciniae divided like branches, from which it has pleased me to call them *branched tentacles*, so that they might be distinguished from the ventral ones.

Among these tentacles a convex white area is seen, perforated in the middle; that hole *f.* is the mouth of the animal, into which the food is driven by the help of the branched tentacles. Diametrically opposite the mouth is another hole *i.*, two lines wide, performing the office of an anus, from which the faeces and the sea-water are eliminated.

A most delightful spectacle indeed does this *Zoophyton* offer the beholder when it is kept in a vessel full of sea-water, yet so that the water does not rise more than two inches above the body of the *Hydra*. For then, and most of all when it raises the anus a little, almost every minute of time it spurts sea-water to a height of almost two inches above the surface of the water in which it is contained; and in this way it represents an admirable fountain of an altogether new kind. But it drives the same much farther out of the body when it is **[p. 83]** freshly drawn from the bottom of the sea and handled strongly with the hand. For on this occasion it ejects the water it has drawn in even to two feet, and its body hardens like wood; which hardening, together with the spurting of water through the anus and the cylindrical form of the body, seem chiefly to have given the occasion for the Ancients to call this genus of *Zoophyta* *Mentula*, and the Italians by the obscene name *cazzo di mare*.

That it is sea-water, however, and not urine, as a suspicion might arise in the minds of some, which is excreted by the *Hydra* in this way, the following things demonstrate. First, no urinary viscera exist in its body, as will appear below. Next, the quantity of the humour excreted is so great that, even if kidneys or a urinary bladder were present, they could not contain it, nor could they secrete the liquid as quickly as it is frequently eliminated. Finally, the taste of the said humour is exactly the same as that of sea-water.

**§. V.** The order of the matter would require that I should pursue the further, that is the internal, structure of the *Hydra*; but before I begin the narration of this, I hope it will not be unpleasing if I adduce that phenomenon which I observed as altogether singular in our *Zoophyton*.

When on the first day after my arrival, which was the sixth of June, I was walking along the shore of the sea near the city itself, the first marine inhabitant that struck my eyes was a *Hydra* nestling and creeping among various marine *fuci*, which was being tossed to and fro by the storms on the shore itself. This unexpected and altogether new prize so delighted my mind that I could not refrain from carrying it home at once, **[p. 84]** most eager to discover its habits. There I was indeed astonished, and my voice stuck in my throat, when after the lapse of an hour I observed that my dear guest was gradually excreting from the anus an intestine-shaped canal full of sea sand, which at first sight I judged to be the excrement of the animal. But when a little later another canal came out, convoluted after the manner of intestines and empty, to which another body adorned with whitish globules and like a mesentery was attached, and finally very many things like small blind intestines followed, united to the same canal, I rightly suspected that this whole apparatus was something other than excrement. Yet I had no wish to divine or determine the true essence of the matter before I knew that several, indeed all, the individuals of the *Hydra* that I could obtain eject the said apparatus from the body by a fixed law of nature. Wherefore I at once ordered a certain fisherman to bring several similar *Zoophyta* the following day. The seafaring man willingly complied, and brought three *Hydrae*, which, placed in a vessel full of sea-water, cast off, more slowly indeed, yet the same parts as the first, except the small blind intestines, which in these were red and had been larger.

Although I saw this happen in four *Hydrae*, and did not doubt that it was common to all, nevertheless the greatest difficulty tormented my mind when I wished to comprehend the nature and office of the things excreted. For now, as I recalled above, the thought came into my mind that they were the excrements of the *Hydra*, but this I at once refuted when I considered their organic structure a little more attentively.

**[p. 85]** Then again it seemed more probable that the said apparatus was the intestines of another animal, swallowed by the *Hydra* and excreted again. But neither did this thought long stand on firm footing: for to what end would the *Hydra* devour such dishes, if it were compelled to eliminate them again from the body undigested? Moreover, such nourishment would be disproportionate to the body of the *Hydra*; and finally, where could our *Zoophyton* lay hold of so many intestines, unless it swallowed the animal itself at the same time, of which however I never detected the least trace? But if anyone should suppose that this had been digested, why, pray, were not the intestines themselves also changed? Having therefore dismissed this opinion in turn, I took refuge in another, which at the first instant I hoped would stand more firmly: namely, that perhaps the aforesaid parts were an ovary. But behold a new difficulty, which either overturns the opinion just conceived, or makes it very hard to retain. To what purpose, in an animal so small, indeed in a plant-animal, so great an apparatus of parts for the preservation of its species? Which part, after all, is properly the ovary? the whitish globules visible in the mesentery? or the small blind intestines? To settle on one or the other without further observations I considered rash. Whence I rejected this opinion also, and finally embraced the one that the *Hydra* excretes its own intestines. I confess this too has its difficulties and its obstacles, if we weigh the following clearly: first, that it is against the common laws of nature for any animal to discharge its own intestines not corrupted, but whole and continuous from the oesophagus to the anus, and torn away from the remaining parts. And that this should happen seems the more impossible, the less one can even conceive how it could happen while all the parts **[p. 86]** appear sound. Finally, what is more wonderful: all the individuals of the *Hydra*, after they have cast off their intestines, survive for seven hours and more, enjoying not only an elastic but a progressive motion.

Strong as these arguments against the last opinion may seem, indeed stronger than the rest against the preceding opinions, yet that the matter stands thus and not otherwise I have been taught, by several observations subsequently instituted, in the following manner.

**§. VI.** [Figura 5.] A living *Hydra* freshly drawn from the sea I dissected through the middle along the length of the whole body, and found the whole aforesaid apparatus fixed to the inner walls of the integuments, so that the thick intestine *a.*, stuffed with sea sand and with fragments of *corallina* and other *fuci*, was attached all round to the anus itself; continuous with it was another intestine *b.*, full of a greenish liquor and convoluted into a double coil, adhering moreover to no wall in the belly but free. This, advancing toward the mouth, is dilated a little, where it marks that it does the duty of a stomach *c.* From this wider part of the intestine arises a narrower canal or oesophagus *d.*, ending in the mouth and inserted on all sides into its walls.

[Figura 6.] Attached to the oesophagus are small blind intestines, cylindrical, more than a line thick, pointed toward the end, and impregnated with various red dots floating in a rather thick liquor, like little worms half a line long.

**[p. 87]** These small intestines taken together present, as to their divisions, a little tree, or better the *fucus* called *quercus marina*; they unite into a single trunk, which is inserted into the oesophagus, but whether this opens into it by some little mouth I could not discover.

[Figura 5.] Along the whole intestinal tract a greenish vessel *e. e.*, more than a line thick, runs on the outside, which a little below the wider part of the intestine is parted into two branches *f. f.* The branch *g.*, again bifurcated, passes into various twigs *h. h.*, which, clothed in a very thin double membrane, constitute the mesentery. Very many vascular twigs below the first curvature of the intestinal canal, destitute of a membrane connecting them, float freely; they are very thin, tinged with a dusky colour. Innumerable similar small vessels at the other curvature of the intestines are joined by anastomoses to another larger branch *i. i. i. i.*

When I saw these things so constituted and united to one another in the body of the *Hydra*, no doubt remained to keep me from believing that that whole apparatus which our *Zoophyton* excretes when placed out of the sea constitutes its own proper parts. For the membranous canal stuffed with sand and fragments of *fuci* constitutes the intestine with the stomach and oesophagus. But that the small blind intestines are an ovary I suspect from this: that I have found them at different times of different size and different colour. For in June they are thin, small, diaphanous and as it were glassy, adorned with very tiny white dots. In July, however, they are larger, thicker and yellowish, and in them red dots are seen, oblong and also larger than the preceding. Finally in the month of August new beginnings of them come into view. This diversity would surely not occur if they were properly **[p. 88]** blind intestines, as in fishes and in some insects, for these keep an equal dimension at every season of the year.

Having considered these things, therefore, I seemed to myself to grasp the reason why the *Hydra* excretes not only the ovary, like fishes and other animals, but also the whole intestinal tract and the parts attached to it. And anyone will easily perceive the same reason who will consider with me: that the accustomed way of nature, by which ovaries, or eggs or the embryos themselves, are discharged, is the vulva or the anus; next, that the ovary of the *Hydra* is situated near the mouth; and finally, that in its rectum no particular aperture for the exit of the ovary is observed, nor does such occur externally in the neighbourhood of the anus.

In order, therefore, that the species of the *Hydra* might be preserved, it pleased the Author of nature to establish in our *Zoophyton* a mode of bringing forth entirely different: namely, that its rectum together with the oesophagus should first be loosed from its connection and ejected from the body, and so a free way lie open for the ovary. This explanation of the singular phenomenon pleased me the more, since I saw at the same time that the *Hydra* has this in common with very many insects, that for the propagation of its kind it is deprived of its own life. For it is very well known that no animal can live without intestines: wherefore I had no doubt that the *Hydra* placed in the sea enjoys the same property, and excretes the intestines with the ovary, just as I observed it do when placed out of the sea.

But after I had recalled a little later that in the month of August I had found new beginnings of ovaries in several dissected *Hydrae*, with their intestines constituted in exactly the same manner, **[p. 89]** I rejected the opinion I had formed about the mode of bringing forth, and suspected that this takes place otherwise: namely, since the ovary goes to insert itself by a single trunk into the oesophagus, what if at the time of birth the ovary should enter this, and thence be ejected through the mouth, by an unaccustomed way though it be.

This opinion indeed seems to me to this day the more probable, both because by the common law of nature the ovary is placed in the body of animals not far from its exit; and because it could not go out through the rest of the intestinal tract on account of the multitude of faeces, and would easily be injured by their roughness.

But whether this or the preceding or finally some other mode of bringing forth in the *Hydra* be the true one, the diligent observations of others to be instituted in this matter will teach; yet this disposition of nature will always remain supremely admirable and perhaps unequalled, that a certain animal ejects from its body sound and whole viscera while its life survives for the space of several hours.

**§. VII.** [Tab. VII. Figura 1.] Having narrated the singular and very wonderful mode of the *Hydra*'s, if not bringing forth, at least dying, it remains that I set forth its further structure. This I shall perform not unaptly if I make a beginning from the skin. This is two lines thick if it is cut open in the dead animal, but does not exceed one line in thickness when it is dissected while the *Hydra* is living and the intestines have been excreted. It is compacted of innumerable white fibres of tendinous consistency, which intersect one another in various ways and form irregular cells.

**[p. 90]** On the inner face of the skin, which immediately embraces the intestines and the remaining viscera, five white muscles *a. a. a. a. a.* come into view, extended from the mouth right to the anus. Each muscle is composed of two other distinct muscles, which gape apart from each other almost a line through the whole tract of the body, and are united at the anus and near the mouth. Each simple muscle is two lines broad, and projects a little above the surface of the skin. But one composite muscle is five lines distant from another, and the intermediate space is compacted of terete fibres *b. b.* placed transversely. By the help of these muscles the *Hydra* either extends its body beyond a foot, or contracts it almost into a ball.

[Figura 2.] Within the cavity of the mouth, at the beginning of the aforesaid five muscles, a bony ring occurs, linked together out of five convexo-concave teeth, [Figura 4.] fixed in a circle to the circumference of the mouth by means of two broad ligaments *b. c.*, so that the upper ligament passes into the margin of the mouth, the lower into the outer face of the oesophagus. Each tooth equals three lines in length and two in breadth; with its convex face it looks toward the skin, with its concave face, on which the broad ligaments are placed, toward the inside of the mouth. [Figura 3.] It is composed of a body *a.* and four apophyses *b. b. c. c.*; its body is furnished with a transverse undulating furrow *d.*, from which minute striae run out toward the apophyses. The two lower apophyses *b. b.* are sharp, the two lateral *c. c.* blunt. By means of the blunt apophyses all the teeth are so firmly joined to one another that they seem to constitute a solid ring. The substance of the teeth is friable, and compacted like a floury paste.

**[p. 91]** [Figura 4.] On the convex face of the teeth other worm-shaped ligaments *a. a. a.* occur, twenty in number, and so disposed that on each apophysis there stands one such ligament; hence four are counted on each tooth. [Figura 5.] Each ligament is a conical little tube more than a line thick, filled with a limpid humour, painted on one side with a double row of dots *a.*, and marked at the sharp end or apex with a similar scarlet spot *b.*; at which end a white cloud lying hidden within also appears. Its base adheres to the apophysis of the teeth, but the apex floats free between the teeth and the skin.

The structure of the worm-shaped ligaments led me at first sight to believe that they were so many embryos of the *Hydra*: for I took that white spot visible at the apex for the beginning of the teeth, and I thought the scarlet dots to be so many beginnings of the papillae which are disposed on the back of the larger *Hydra*, § II. But when I found their base applied to the outer face of the teeth and firmly fixed, and moreover saw these vermicular bodies in all the *Hydrae* that I subsequently dissected always in equal number and disposed in a circle, I resolved to mark them with the name of ligaments.

The use of these ligaments is nevertheless very obscure: for since they are free at one end, they cannot make the teeth fast or move them; still less can they contribute anything to the comminution of food, since they are situated below the ring of teeth and outside the cavity of the mouth. Do they secrete saliva, and spit it out into the cavity of the mouth? Their structure and position indeed indicate this not slightly. **[p. 92]** But if that were so, the aforesaid worm-shaped bodies should be called not ligaments but salivary canals or ducts.

To the lower margin of the teeth the oesophagus is attached on all sides; but at its other end it is continuous with the remaining intestines, as I recalled above. Besides the parts enumerated nothing else lies hidden in the body of the *Hydra*, nor is any trace of a heart, brain, spinal marrow or other viscus to be seen. From the position of the teeth, however, everyone will now recognise, I hope, that I had the greater occasion to believe that APULEIUS, when speaking of the *lepus marinus*, had our *Hydra* or the *Mentula* of the ancients before his hands. For this, as he says of the *lepus*, is *otherwise boneless*, and has *linked bones in the belly*.

The *Hydra* therefore, as is plain from the history of it given, such as it is, is *a genus of Zoophyta provided with a cylindrical body, the belly beset with innumerable terete tentacles, the back with pyramidal papillae, an orbicular mouth, and branched tentacles twenty in number on the circumference of the head.* For thus it is distinguished both from the rest of the *Zoophyta* and also from the *medusa*, with which it could easily be confounded according to the generic character given by the celebrated LINNAEUS: since there is also a *medusa* with a cylindrical body, with tentacles on the circumference of the head, which are common to all *medusae*.

If the size of the body and the variety of colours have a place in naming the species of animals, the following species of *Hydra* can be counted: *Hydra major, ex fusco, albo, & rubescente variegata* [the larger Hydra, variegated with dusky, white and reddish]; the Epipetrum of authors. *Hydra tota fusca* [the wholly dusky Hydra]. *Hydra minor ex fusco lutea* [the smaller Hydra, yellow out of dusky]. These indeed, and no others, came into my hands in the whole time **[p. 93]** that I stayed at Naples. Whether there are more, time perhaps will teach. But I wonder very much at this, that it never befell me to see a single young *Hydra*, although only old ones linger on the shores.

**CHAPTER V. ON THE SYRINX.**

**§. I.** The *Zoophyton* depicted in Tab. VII. Fig. 6 and 7 was brought to me on the second of September, while I was occupied in packing up my treasures and preparing myself to take the road back toward Rome. The dear guest had arrived late: hence I long deliberated with myself whether, because of the shortness of time, I ought to undertake its description and add it to this little work, such as it is. But since I found it in no author even roughly drawn, or at least obscurely described, I judged that I should be doing a thing most welcome to the curious in nature if I communicated to them its figure and a description, such as it may be, of its external structure.

**§. II.** [Tab. VII. Figura 6.] The *Syrinx* exceeds eight inches in its length, and is conical from the head *a.* right to the tail *b.* Its base comprises nine lines and its apex four lines in diameter. The wider part of the body, or the head or base, **[p. 94]** is furnished with a mouth, in which is contained a proboscis *c.*, or canal or little siphon, compacted of a strong membrane and armed with fleshy three-pointed papillae equalling a grain of millet. This little siphon is united at one end on all sides to the rim of the mouth, at the other free, and the animal can at its pleasure thrust it out to the length of an inch or draw it back within the mouth. Our *Zoophyton* thrusts out its little siphon, as I gather by conjecture, at the time when it wishes to seize and swallow food. [Figura 7.] For when I received it, the little siphon was hidden within the mouth, so indeed that I believed this *Zoophyton* to be a species of *Hydra*. But after it had remained quiet and untouched for several minutes, it protruded the little siphon out of the mouth; and this protrusion and retraction of the siphon it repeated again and again, a most delightful spectacle. Whence at last I understood that my guest was a new genus of *Zoophyta*.

When the aforesaid little siphon is placed outside the mouth, its papillose face looks outward; but it occupies the inside of the mouth when it is hidden within it. Hence food seized by the free end of the siphon can no longer take flight, several papillae always standing in the way like so many little teeth, the deeper the siphon is received within the mouth. If one cares to compare this protrusion and retraction of the siphon to some familiar thing, it is no otherwise than if someone should push the tip of one or another finger of a glove into its cavity, and draw it out again. For thus, for example, the coloured part of the glove constitutes the inner face in the former case, but the outer face in the latter.

**[p. 95]** [Figura 7.] An inch and a half from the mouth there is an oblong aperture *a.*, surrounded by a slightly projecting lip *b.* as by a sphincter, and placed transversely. Whether this is on the belly or the back I cannot determine, since the whole body of the animal is uniform. Moreover, I cannot settle for certain whether the aforesaid aperture is an anus or a vulva; yet I would not deny that it is the anus and the common way for the exit of the ovary, since neither at the spherical apex nor elsewhere could I detect any trace of a hole, with all diligence applied.

The whole body of our *Zoophyton* is yellowish white, and is adorned with deep longitudinal striae and other circular ones. The longitudinal striae are half a line, the circular a line, distant from one another; between them the skin projects in an oblong-square form, and the whole body is reticulated.

The *Syrinx* has the same motion as the *Hydra*, for now it is extended to a length of almost a foot, now it is contracted into a smaller mass, widening the narrower portion of the body near the spherical apex. Yet it is never drawn together into a ball, as I recall that the *Hydra* does.

It lives in the deeper sea, whence it is never cast out on the shores, but now and then enters the nets with other fishes. It is called to food by no one, nor has it any other use, as far as the fishermen know.

**§. III.** What the structure of the internal parts of this new genus of *Zoophyta* may be I am unable to relate, both because, as I recalled before, of the shortness of time, **[p. 96]** and because I was loath to tear it apart with the anatomical knife. For only a single one was brought to me in the whole time that I stayed at Naples, and this I was anxious to preserve in spirit of wine. [Figura 7.] As far as I could gather from handling it, the viscera extend only from the base as far as the aperture *a.* and a little beyond it. For the remaining body, together with the spherical apex, seemed to me impervious and solid.

Nevertheless, from the rough description and drawing of our *Zoophyton* as to its external structure, everyone, I hope, will perceive that it cannot be referred to the *Hydra*, with which however it seems to have a certain affinity, still less to any other genus of *Zoophyta*. Not to the *Hydra*, since this is provided with *branched tentacles on the circumference of the head*, with *cylindrical tentacles* on the belly and with *pyramidal papillae* on the back, of all of which the *Syrinx* is destitute. Moreover, the *Hydra* has its anus situated at the extremity opposite the mouth, from which it pours out water like a fountain; the *Syrinx* keeps its anus not far from the mouth, and does not eject water.

That the *Syrinx* does not belong to any other genus of *Zoophyta* or of other animals, the external configuration of its body teaches sufficiently and abundantly: so much so that it would be superfluous to confirm it with further words. If, therefore, the *Syrinx* can be reduced neither to the *Hydra* nor to any other genus of animals, no one, I hope, will accuse me of error for having created a new genus out of it. Perhaps it will not please everyone that I call it *Syrinx*, and this most of all because it does not eject water, a property which it ought to enjoy in order to deserve the name of *Syrinx*. I confess that the *Hydra*, for example, could better have been marked with this name, **[p. 97]** since it pours out sea-water like a syringe. But because the celebrated LINNAEUS imposed the name *Hydra* on the *Zoophyton* described by me in the preceding chapter, I preferred to call the new *Zoophyton* *Syrinx* rather than to breed confusion by a transmutation of names. To avoid which I have also chosen to call it *syrinx* and not *syringa*, which however is the same thing, so that it might the more easily be distinguished from the hydraulic instrument and the shrub of the same name.

The reason, however, that led me to seize on this name is this: the motion of the little siphon placed in the mouth of our *Zoophyton* in some measure imitates the motion of the piston in a syringe, that is to say the hydraulic instrument. If this is not sufficient, I think I have made a small error. For how many names, pray, have been given to bodies with reason? and what likeness has the *Mentula* of the Ancients to the apocalyptic *Hydra*? nevertheless it seemed to the celebrated LINNAEUS worthy to be called *Hydra*.

But whether this nomenclature of mine be received or rejected by the curious in nature, the *syrinx* will always remain a new genus of *Zoophyta*, and that one which is provided *with a reticulated, pyramidal body, adorned with a globose impervious apex, and with a mouth placed in the middle of the base, and in it a movable little siphon.* But if anyone shall think that it would be better reduced to the order of worms, he will not have me against him. For since it is destitute of tentacles or limbs, it deserves a place among the worms. Yet it belongs to the order of *Zoophyta* by the same right by which *Tethys*, also destitute of tentacles, has been placed in it by the celebrated LINNAEUS.

**[p. 128]** **CHAPTER VII. ON THE TETHYUM.**

**§. I.** I long deliberated with myself under what name to propose the genus of animals now to be described, whether, namely, I ought to call it *Holothurium* or rather *Tethyum*. For if we consult the writings of authors on this matter, we draw from them great occasion for hesitation as to what properly is a *Holothurium* and what a *Tethyum*.

**[p. 129]** About the *Holothurium* there is a great silence among the Moderns, if you except the celebrated JANUS PLANCUS and LINNAEUS (\*): of whom the former, after the mind of the Ancients, calls *Holothurium* a *callous and leathery body free from the rocks*, while the latter simply subjoins it to the *Tethyum* as a species. The older writers indeed, such as RONDELET and ALDROVANDI, have committed some things to memory about this *Zoophyton*, but these are so obscure that you cannot understand what they mean.

The history of the *Tethyum* which we read in ARISTOTLE (\*\*) and in RONDELET, ALDROVANDI and also JONSTON and others who have followed him does indeed fit the genus of *Zoophyta* to be described, as does also the generic character attributed to the same by the celebrated LINNAEUS after the mind of ARISTOTLE. But the description of two *Tethya* committed to print by the celebrated DONATI (\*\*\*) was long an obstacle to my calling our *Zoophyton* by the name assigned to it by ARISTOTLE himself. The matter at last weighed more maturely, I judged it more advisable to retain the name *Tethyum* according to ARISTOTLE and the celebrated LINNAEUS, and at the same time to point out that the *Tetiae* of the celebrated DONATI belong to the *Alcyonium* and not to the *Tethyum*; by which assertion I hope the less to offend the celebrated man, since he himself confesses that a great similarity lies between the *Alcyonium* and his *Tetiae*.

To avoid further confusion, therefore, *the Tethyum is a genus of Zoophyta provided with a more or less oblong body, with two extended organs of which one is shorter, and lacking tentacles.* From this definition it is plain that the celebrated LINNAEUS has very rightly placed the *Tethyum* and the *Holothurium* of the Ancients under one and the same genus, since in both **[p. 130]** the same characteristic marks occur. For that the one adheres to the rocks and the other is free from them cannot distinguish one genus from the other, though the celebrated JANUS PLANCUS is pleased to maintain this with the Ancients. From the same definition it is at the same time clear that the *Tetiae* of the celebrated DONATI do not belong to our genus of *Zoophyta*, since the generic character given is not observed in them, as may be understood from their description and figure.

>
> (\*\*) *[p. 129]* Hist. An. l. 4. c. 2.
>
> (\*\*\*) *[p. 129]* Saggio della storia naturale del mare adriatico.

Besides the common *Tethyum*, which adheres chiefly to oysters and is eaten among the inhabitants of the coast, three other species of it came into my hands. Namely *Tethyum coriaceum, asperum, coccineum, organorum orificiis setis exiguis munitis* [the leathery, rough, scarlet Tethyum, with the orifices of its organs armed with tiny bristles]. *Tethyum gelatinosum, coccineum, leve, organorum orificiis setis destitutis* [the gelatinous, scarlet, smooth Tethyum, with the orifices of its organs destitute of bristles]. *Tethyum membranaceum, subalbidum, rugosum, organorum orificiis setis destitutis* [the membranous, whitish, wrinkled Tethyum, with the orifices of its organs destitute of bristles]. These species, therefore, as not to be found in the writings of other authors, or only obscurely designated, I intend to describe briefly in this chapter.

**§. II.** [Tab. X. Figura 1.] The *Tethyum coriaceum* &c. is for the most part three inches long, one inch and seven lines broad, of oval figure. Above it is provided with two mammillary protuberances, or two extended organs *a. b.*, of which one, *a.*, placed at the summit of the body, has a cruciform orifice. The other, *b.*, is placed a little lower, and its orifice is placed transversely and is triangular, as Fig. 2 shows more clearly. Several bristle-like yellow hairs a line long surround the lips of each orifice, keeping no regular **[p. 131]** order. The outer face of the whole body is rough, crowded with tiny oblong scarlet granules or papillae. [Figura 1.] The end opposite the organs, or the base, is furnished with peduncles *c.* of various form, by means of which this *Zoophyton* sits firmly on rocks and other bodies, so that it cannot be torn from them without injury to the peduncles.

The skin is thick and hard as leather, making up the greatest mass of the animal. The entrails can scarcely be distinguished, except a certain intestine-shaped part which, taking its beginning a little below the orifice of the upper organ, descends almost to the base, and returning thence to the right side is inserted into the lower orifice or organ. Whence it may not unreasonably be conjectured that the upper organ performs the office of a mouth, the lower that of an anus.

The fishermen call this species of *Tethyum* limone di mare. It seems to be indicated by JONSTON (\*) under the name of *mentula marina*, Fig. II. It has no use as food.

> (\*) *[p. 131]* See L. IV. de Exanguibus aquat. Tab. XX.

**§. III.** [Figura 3.] The second species of *Tethyum* attains one inch and ten lines in length, and sixteen lines in breadth. It has a compressed form. It is wholly smooth, tinged with an elegant scarlet colour, and translucent like a jelly, of the same substance as the *Pulmo marinus* of Mattioli and others, that is, of a consistency midway between jelly and cartilage. Its organs are oblong, furnished with a slit or longitudinal orifice *a. a.* The wrinkled lips of the orifices are armed with no hairs. **[p. 132]** At the base, just as in the preceding species, various peduncles *b. b.* occur, by whose help it sits on other bodies. The *Tethyum* of this species which I here exhibit drawn I found on the 22nd of August adhering to a piece of wood on the shore near the Castel Nuovo, and it did not befall me to see another before or after.

**§. IV.** [Figura 4.] The species of *Tethyum* that now follows seems to me to be that which the celebrated JANUS PLANCUS (\*) described under the name of *Mentula marina penem caninum referens*. This *Tethyum* is found either loose or entangled with other individuals of its species. Whence it might not unaptly be called *Tethyum fasciculatum*.

Its whole body is compacted of a thick whitish membrane like the intestines of quadrupeds, so that when on the 19th of August I came upon a single such *Tethyum*, already deprived of life, on the shore of Naples, I took it for a portion of the large intestine of some animal. Wherefore, not caring much about it, I threw it away on that occasion. But after I had found, on the 26th of the same month, while walking on the same shore, the cluster depicted in Tab. X. Fig. 4, I at last learned that the body I had seen before, on the 19th of August, was not a piece of intestine but a species of *Tethyum*. For besides the fact that I saw several similar bodies united to one another, I observed at the same time a manifest motion in them. Whence I at once placed the said cluster in a vessel filled with sea-water, and carried it home, so that I might better contemplate that kind of animal and inquire into its structure.

> (\*) *[p. 132]* See Lib. de Conchis minus notis part. tert. C. III.

**[p. 133]** Seven *Tethya*, then, as Fig. 4 shows, were united to one another by means of peduncles of almost tendinous consistency, and had formed an elegant bundle. Each such *Tethyum* has, on the part opposite the peduncles *b.*, two organs *a.* somewhat extended and furnished with orbicular wrinkled orifices destitute of hairs. Its body is compressed and smooth, and at the pleasure of the animal is now curved, now extended in a straight line. A strong skin, constructed, as I recalled above, on the pattern of intestines, not only clothes the whole body but constitutes it. If this is dissected longitudinally, another membranous canal comes into view, full of a blackish matter, which, descending from the upper organ almost to the base, is there bent back and goes to insert itself into the lower organ.

These organs are sometimes strongly constricted, sometimes relaxed, yet so that no intermediate aperture ever appears. Whether I handled this bundle of *Tethya* out of the water or considered it placed in the water, I never observed the organs to breathe out water, as the *Tethyum coriaceum* is wont to do.

But if the body of such a *Tethyum* is irritated with a needle or any stylus, the whole of it contracts and wrinkles toward its own base, that is toward the centre of the bundle, the rest remaining motionless. Whence this bundle, although it seems to be one body, is nevertheless not one simple animal, but several individuals of *Tethyum* constitute it. Here is another argument, clearer than light itself, which plainly proves that the *Pennae*, the lithophytes and the corals are simple animals.

**[p. 134]** But whether it is proper to this species of *Tethyum* that several individuals always live together, or whether such a single *Tethyum* sometimes adheres to rocks and other bodies, it is hard to determine either way. I never found a single one sitting on other bodies, but sometimes found one loose on the shore, and I saw several such bundles of *Tethya* both on that day and afterwards. Whence the former property seems to me the more probable, and it is now plain that the body described by the celebrated JANUS PLANCUS under the name of *Mentula marina penem caninum referens* belongs, according to his own mind, not to the *Tethyum* but to the *Holothurium*, since it is free from the rocks.

The following mode of generation of our *Tethyum* confirms this property of it. When on the 27th of August I visited the sea-shore again, there came into my hands such a *Tethyum*, loose, larger than all those I had been able to see before, [Figura 5.] on whose trunk eight younger *Tethya* *a. a.* are to be seen; among these very many tiny young *b. b.* appear, and on the opposite side of the same trunk eight young *a. a.* a little larger than these tiny ones are scattered. [Figura 6.] Both in the larger and in the middle-sized and also in the smallest of these *Tethya* the beginnings of the organs are visible, but the peduncles cannot be distinguished.

This singular mode of generation I suppose to be accomplished thus: the little eggs of our *Zoophyton* seem to be so constituted that, when driven out of the body of the older *Tethyum*, they are easily applied by the sea waves to its outer surface, and the young that come out of them remain there applied and adhering until they have attained a certain size. When this is done, several unite themselves by the help of the peduncles, and wander through the sea in bundles. That young of different sizes **[p. 135]** are placed on the same trunk depends on the difference of the time at which the eggs of these or of those were excreted. We observe an almost similar mode of generation in certain species of *ostreum*, several of which are accustomed to unite with one another.

**CHAPTER VIII. ON THE MEDUSA PALLIATA.**

**§. I.** The *Zoophyton* depicted in Tab. XI. Fig. I I call a *Medusa* after the mind of the celebrated LINNAEUS, but according to others it is a species of *urtica*. Its singular structure seemed to deserve that I should annex its history to this little work, such as it is.

Its figure, just as in all species of *Medusa*, is cylindrical, or, as it pleases the celebrated LINNAEUS, orbicular. The internal fabric is the same: namely innumerable tiny cylindrical tentacles *a. a.* are placed in a circle on the outside; an oblong mouth *b.*, furnished with rather thick lips, from which very long and very white filaments *c. c.* hang, opens almost in the middle of the body; a little above the mouth the anus *d.* projects in the form of a cylindrical little tube. The skin on the outside is white, painted with most elegant scarlet dots *e. e.* But this species of *Medusa* has this singular beyond the rest, that on the outside it is clothed with another skin, separated from the orbicular body, like a little cloak, whence I have called it *Medusa palliata*.

**[p. 136]** It comes into the fishermen's net chiefly in the month of August, at which time I also received several individuals of it. All the *Medusae* of this species sit upon the empty shells of a *cochlea umbilicata subalbida punctis coccineis notata* [an umbilicate whitish snail marked with scarlet dots] *f.*, and in respect of colour seem to form almost one body with the snail. The position of each *Medusa* on the snail is this: its orbicular body adheres to the inner lip of the snail's mouth, while the little cloak *g.* is firmly applied all round to the margin of the outer lip, so that between the orbicular body of the *Medusa* and the inner face of the cloak an empty space remains, and the cloak is spread like a sail while this *Zoophyton* stays in the water; but the cloak collapses as soon as it is drawn out of the water.

The spreading of this cloak is the reason why our *Medusa* swims in mid-water and wanders through the sea, where it falls into the fishermen's net. That it adheres only to the aforesaid snail, the reason seems to be partly the form of the cloak proportioned to the mouth of the snail, partly the matching colour of the two, lest perhaps any enemies among its fellow inhabitants of the sea should distinguish this soft animal from the shell of the snail. I confess that this species of *Medusa* could better be called *velifera*, from the fact that it spreads its little cloak like a sail; yet lest it be confounded with the *urtica velifera* of FABIO COLONNA, I preferred to propose it under the species *Medusa palliata*.

Let me be allowed to note in this place that the celebrated MARCUS CARBURI (\*) calls the *urtica velifera* of FABIO COLONNA *Armenistari*, a name in use in Cephalonia, and separates it from the *urticae*. Why, I do not see: for if according to him it seems to deserve the name of *urtica* because, placed on the mouth, it burns after the manner of the *urticae*, **[p. 137]** much more does it deserve that name on account of the structure of the body, which it has in common with the *urticae*, except for the bony sail to which it firmly adheres and which constitutes a part of it. Both FABIO COLONNA and the celebrated CARBURI have described this *Zoophyton* at length, and the latter has added an elegant figure of it: hence it would be useless to repeat its history in this place, although the same was brought to me in the month of August in such numbers that I could have drawn out its entire structure from them with the slightest trouble. Yet I cannot pass over that which the celebrated CARBURI himself was unable to settle. For when he describes the sail-shaped membrane, he says that he cannot affirm whether the membrane of the *wing* is continuous with the membrane of the *base*. How, then, this membrane and the whole sail are constituted, and in what way I discovered its structure, I shall now relate.

> (\*) *[p. 136]* See Allgemeines Magazin, 10ter Theil.

Of the several individuals of this most elegant *Zoophyton* which I had received in the month of August, I sent some, laid up in spirit of wine, and some dried in the sun, to Prague, where on my return, examining my dried *Medusae*, or better their sails, I found that the membrane of the base is compacted of a double lamina, one lower, on which, namely, the animal sits, the other upper, which, raised upward, forms the wing of the sail; hence the upper lamina of the base and the wing are one and the same membrane, or, what the celebrated CARBURI left undetermined, *the membrane of the wing is continuous with the membrane of the base*. The lower membrane of the base is smooth, except for circular furrows which are thinly engraved on it. The inner face of the upper lamina of the base, that is the one by which it is applied to the lower, is furnished with other concentric little membranes raised to the height of a line, and these concentric little membranes **[p. 138]** form the aforesaid furrows in the lower lamina of the base. But although the upper lamina is firmly united to the lower lamina by means of these little membranes in the living animal, yet when it is dead and dried it is very easily separated from it. In the membrane of the wing various striae drawn at an obtuse angle are observed. The substance of these membranes constituting the sail of the *Medusa* is the same as in the scales of fishes and in the bone of the *loligo*, yet a little more tender. Whence only the sail is preserved after the death of the *Zoophyton*, for the rest of the body of the *Medusa* is so soft that it either flies away into the air or dissolves in spirit of wine.
