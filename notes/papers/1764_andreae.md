## Original publication and dating

The book itself gives the publication history. The preface (pp. III–V)
says the letters were first printed in the *Hannoverisches Magazin*
("begun 1763, and still continued", footnote on p. III), with a short
preface dated "Hannover im März 1764". After the letters had appeared
there, Andreae published a set of *Nacherinnerungen* (afterthoughts),
quoted in the preface as "the Nacherinnerungen of 1765", correcting
and supplementing them; the 1776 edition folds these into the text as
footnotes marked "N.E." or "Nacherinnerung von 1765". Further notes
are dated 1766–1775 (d'Annone 1766 and 1771, Gessner 1765 and 1769,
"N.Z." 1768 and 1773, J. S. W. 1775), so the footnotes must be read
date by date. The letters are dated at the end of each; those with
echinoderm content are dated Basel, 14 August 1763 (Letter 1), 17
August (Letter 3), 24 August (Letter 5), Schaffhausen, 1 September
(Letter 11), Zürich (Letter 14), and Geneva, 5 October 1763 (Letter
38). The scan does not say in which Stücke of the Magazin each letter
appeared, so the 1764 and 1765 pagination cannot be recovered from
it; what can be said is that the main text below is the 1764 printing
(letters of 1763), and that anything in a footnote without a date is
also original, while dated footnotes belong to 1765 or the 1776
edition as marked.

## Where Encrinus appears, and why the two searches disagree

A text search of the scan finds *Encrinus* on pp. 4, 6, 56, 185 and
339; the book's own Register (p. 343) gives "Encrinus. 27. 40. 41.
56. 339" and, as separate entries, "Encrini Calyx, L. Lap.
pentagonus. 32", "— coralloidaei. 3–5", "— verstümmeltes Original. 6.
7" and "Entrochi. 5. 8. 64". So the index does cover pp. 3–7 under
its sub-entries, and the only page the index omits is 185, a one-line
remark that Bertrand's cabinet contains no Encrinus. Pages 40–41 are
in the index but not in the text search because the text there calls
the fossils "Medusen" and "See-medusen-Haupt" after Hiemer, with
"Encriniten" only in footnote (h); the plate explanation names Tab. 6
"Ein Encrinus oder stieligte Meduse". The Fraktur text layer of the
scan is also very poor, so the search also misses p. 27 (Encrino) and
p. 64 (Encrino, in the dative).

The passages with taxonomic content are:

- **pp. 3–5** (Letter 1, Bawier's cabinet, Basel): the entrochal
  corals of the Baselische Merkwürdigkeiten are a kind of Lilienstein
  or Encrinus; Andreae proposes the name **Encrinus Coralloides**, and
  the varieties **Encrinos coralloidaeos divergentes** and
  **convergentes**; discussion of Rosinus's *stellae polyactinobolae*
  and the distinction from Rumphius's *Capita Medusae* (all Encrini
  have a stalk, Capita Medusae do not).
- **pp. 6–7** (Letter 2, Frey's cabinet): a living specimen of the
  animal-plant "of which the petrifaction is called Encrinus" (Tab. 1
  Fig. A), the only piece of the original then known.
- **p. 8**: Entrochi ramificantes coralloidaei; an *Entrochus
  pentagonus* or *Astroita columnaris* with five apophyses (Tab. 1
  Fig. i).
- **p. 11**: Hofer's Entrochites or "joint-stones belonging to the
  Encrinus", described by Hofer as *Anthoporiten* in the Acta
  Helvetica.
- **p. 27** (Letter 5, d'Annone's cabinet): a small Encrinus on
  sandstone (Tab. 2 Fig. g).
- **p. 32** (Letter 7, Gessner's cabinet): a *Calyx Encrini* or
  *Lapis pentagonus* that is concave (Tab. 3 Fig. c, d, with
  d'Annone's 1766 note); the Solnhofen star-fish for which Andreae
  proposes **Asterias geniculata, radiis pinnatis geniculatis, pinnis
  quam plurimum alternis**.
- **pp. 40–41** (Letter 9, Amman's cabinet, Schaffhausen): the two
  Württemberg slates with Hiemer's "Medusae", one of them a stalked
  form with pentagonal star-shaped trochites (Tab. 6).
- **p. 56** (Letter 12): a longitudinally broken Encrinus from Lukeln
  near Braunschweig showing the stalk entering the body.
- **p. 64** (Letter 14): an Entrochus of the same Encrinus as Amman's,
  from Württemberg (Tab. 2 Fig. h); an eleven-jointed one from St.
  Gall.
- **p. 339** (Letter 48, closing survey): no Swiss cabinet has a
  complete long-stalked Encrinus; the Encrini come from a sea-plant
  animal of unequal substance; Gruner's **Encrinus Lilium lapideum**
  from Schinznach.
- Echinoids: **pp. 10–11, 14–16** (Hofer's cabinet and his own Latin
  list, with the remark on the ambulacra of *Echinus Diadema* and
  *Echinus Cidaris* Linn.), **p. 40** (Amman's spines and echinites),
  **p. 53** (Echinoconus, Echinanthus), **p. 56**, **pp. 266–267**
  (de Luc's echinite with its Lapides Judaici still attached, and
  Amman's; Linné not yet accepting the Judenstein as echinoid spines).

Skipped as having no taxonomic bearing: p. 185 ("and no Encrinus is
there"), and the Alcyonium and Corallium passages on p. 33.

Names stay as Andreae prints them (Encrinus, Lilienstein,
Räderstein, Trochit, Entrochus, Echinit, Meer-Igel). "Räderstein"
(wheel-stone) and "Trochit" are columnals; "Lilienstein" (lily-stone)
is the Encrinus. Footnote letters are the book's own.

## Letter 1 (Basel, 14 August 1763), pp. 3–5: Bawier's cabinet

**[p. 3]** … The petrifactions Herr *Bawier*, a learned citizen and
artist of Basel, has bought up, and a part of them has come through
him to Sweden. But since Herr B. himself also studies nature in
petrifactions, and has collected in them for many years, one can
easily think that he will have kept the most remarkable pieces of the
Platerian collection for himself. I have had the pleasure of making
the acquaintance of this skilful man and of seeing his cabinet, and I
believe it will be enough, said to the praise of the latter, that it
contains all the singular petrifactions which are presented in copper
in the first eight parts of the *Baselische Merkwürdigkeiten* (d) and
are to be read described by him. A special consideration among them
is deserved by the coral-sponges described in the seventh part of the
said Merkwürdigkeiten **[p. 4]** and figured on the seventh plate,
which, although their base consists of concentric lamellae, yet form
star-like lateral outgrowths, which presupposes a singular internal
structure. The six pieces g. h. i. k. l. m., which indeed hold the
preference among the rest, present, as one may now wish to call them,
coralline Entrochi, or entrochal corals. They seem to me to be a
hitherto hardly yet remarked kind of the Lilienstein, or Encrinus,
whose stalk consists of a series of very broad trochi, and which
ramify into a head in a not so orderly and regular way as the common
Encrini: for the number of the branches or radii of the head is in
these of no determinate certainty, nor do they lie in any determinate
figure against and beside one another, but often twine unequally
through one another, so that at first sight one should take this
petrifaction for a mere Madrepora Corallium album officinarum. But the
entrochal composition of each radius shows otherwise. I should
therefore be inclined to give it the name **Encrinus Coralloides**.

*Rosinus* (e) has meanwhile already laid a good foundation for the
knowledge of these Encrinorum coralloidaeorum (f). He calls them
stellas polyactinobolas, and finds that there must be an exceedingly
great number of them in nature, since from the speciminibus
trochitarum described by himself he brings out already more than 80
species of them different from one another (g). Between them and the
Capitibus Medusae of Rumphius he fixes the difference that the latter
are furnished with branches which, like the branches of trees, are
manifoldly divided and into smaller ones etc., but the former only
single rays, be they now 2, 3, 4, 6 and more, joined against and
beside one another, stretching out etc., and dividing into no forked
rays or branches, since R. assures that among more than a thousand of
the kind he has seen not a single one whose branches should have been
split again into smaller branches. But, as certain as a difference
between them and the Capitibus Medusae is, yet it seems to me that
one has to seek it not so much in the manner of division of their
respective branches, as rather and chiefly to fix it in this, that
the Stellae polyactinobolae and all Encrini are furnished with a tail
or stalk, but the Capita Medusae are not. At least the Swiss specimina
praised above show here and there divaricationes ramorum, which make
Rosinus's proposition waver.

The figures o. p. found in the said seventh part of the Baselische
Merkwürdigkeiten are likewise trochitic corals. But these do not
merely, like those first described and like all Encrini, come forth
from one point, but they also close **[p. 5]** together again into a
point, as the ordinary ones also do, but not the former. I should
therefore, for distinction, if it is constant, call those g. h. i. k.
l. m. **Encrinos coralloidaeos divergentes**, and o. p. **Encrinos
coralloidaeos convergentes**.

If you, Sir, as I doubt however, already possess all the *Baseler
Merkwürdigkeiten*, then look up also the incomparable Encrinos
coralloidaeos which stand in the 8th part on the 8th plate at a. b.
c. d., indeed the whole 8th plate like the 7th, together with the
description, p. 185 ff.

Forgive my prolixity on this; the beauty and rarity of these
petrifactions has extorted it from me.

In closing I will only mention a singular Trochi Petref., which Herr
*Bawier* also possesses. It is of four equal sides and angles, and is
divided into four equal parts by the figure of a cross which it bears
in the middle. This is a most rare piece, of which perhaps no other
cabinet has a second to show. See *Baselische Merkwürdigkeiten*,
plate 8 at Lit. p.

In the 20th part and the figures of the 20th plate of these
*Merkwürdigkeiten*, as in the 4th volume of the Act. Helv. p. 201,
Tab. 6, Fig. 77 (h), you will see and read with pleasure much that
belongs here. …

> Merkwürdigkeiten der Landschaft Basel. First part, Basel 1748. Up to
> now 22 parts are out, of which the last appeared at Basel 1762. —
> I add now that this fine work was closed with the 23rd part, still
> in 1763. 1774.
>
> (e) *[p. 4]* M. R. Rosini Tentamen de Lithozois — Hamburgi 1719.

## Letter 2, pp. 6–7: Frey's cabinet and the living original of the Encrinus

**[p. 6]** Sir, The famous botanist *Stähelin*, whose name is so
often mentioned by *Haller* in his Enumeratione Stirpium Helvetic.,
has also left behind a natural history collection; and this has come
into the hands of Herr *Frey*, a citizen of Basel, who holds the post
of a captain in the French Swiss regiment of Boccard, but has a
dwelling in Basel and often stays there. … Since he stayed long with
the regiment on the island of Corsica, and used the opportunity there
to collect kinds of corals and other sea-creatures, his cabinet has
thereby gained a considerable increase, which has uncommonly
beautified it. Dried foreign fishes, Pennae marinae of every size, a
quantity of valuable shells, together with a white Madrepore with red
coral sitting on it, and a large Tubo vermiculari which still shows
its inhabiting polyp, etc. are among them, as also a foot-long lump
of Tubulis vermicularibus which is very well preserved. Of the shells
I will only remark that among them are also Terebratulae or boring
shells, of which, petrified, the copies have hitherto been so very
common, but the originals have only lately become known. Of these
Herr F. possesses 4, 5 or 6 species; (a) he also presented me with a
pair, in one of which the dried animal still sits … There is also a
considerable stock here of half-sponge-like sea-plants: but the
rarest and most excellent among the sea-creatures of this cabinet is
undoubtedly the piece of the animal-plant which I at once recognised
as belonging to those which *Mylius* and *Ellis* (b) have described,
and which has so much likeness to the Encrinus, hitherto not yet
known in the original. Only a pity that this is but one piece of the
whole. (c) It is, moreover, white in colour, has wedge-shaped joints,
as the Encrinus has, and a substance like tender leather, or **[p.
7]** like the half-woody half-spongy sea-plants. Herr F. does not know
whence this piece is; his father left it to him. …

## Letter 3 (Basel, 17 August 1763), pp. 10–11: Hofer's cabinet at Mülhausen

**[p. 10]** … I visited the latter [Doctor Hofer] first, and found
with him a numerous cabinet, in which are a fairly strong Herbarium
ordered after the Linnaean system, various Marina, and very many
estimable petrifactions. … Of a quantity of Echinites I therefore note

## Letter 3, pp. 14–16: Hofer's own list of the best pieces of his cabinet (echinoderm entries)

**[p. 14]** *Catalogue of the best pieces found in the collection of
natural rarities of Johannes Hofer, M. D. in Mülhausen. Together with
a short description of the shell bed at Courtagnon near Rheims in
Champagne.*

… Helmintholithus Echini esculenti Linn. S. N. superficie pluribus
radiis adpressis obtecta ex marga coerulea eademque marga lapidefacta
farctus. The rarity of this petrifaction consists in about 30 spines
which, also petrified, cover one side of this body; they lie close
against one another pressed to the shell of the urchin, are
cylindrical like those which cover this animal in the sea, and one
and a half to two, also two and a half lines long. This petrifaction
is perfectly whole and its diameter one and two-thirds inches; it
comes from the county of Neuchâtel.

Helmintholithus Echini mamillati Linn. S. N. minera ferri farctus
luteus. The petrifaction of this kind of sea-urchin is rarer than that
which Linnaeus names Echinus Cidaris, whose fine petrifactions have
made the Randen-Berg so famous; in Switzerland at least these are not
common. The piece in question here comes from Basoeille in Lorraine,
from a rich iron ore with which it is filled; the shell itself also
seems, from its yellow colour, to be ferruginous, although its
substance is selenitic and of the kind which Luidius calls
Pergamenum. The diameter of our petrifaction is one and a half
inches.

Helmintholithus Echini cidaris Linn. S. N. compressus & difformis, ore
ossicula ejus maxillae duo falciformia fovens, marga lapidifica grysea
farctus. This petrifaction has the same shape as a remarkably fine one
in Captain *Frey*'s collection at Basel has, with the difference that
the Hofer one has kept only two jaw-bones lying out of order, whereas
the **[p. 15]** Frey piece shows all 5 teeth in their natural
position. This petrifaction is two and a third inches across, and is
from the canton of Basel. Beside this petrifaction lies one like it, in
whose open mouth lie two of the jaw-bones which the clear describer of
sea-urchins Theodor *Klein* calls trabecula (Echinoderm. Tab. 31. h.
pag. 42). All kinds of jaw-bones of sea-urchins, coming from the
Randen-Berg, are also to be seen here singly.

In passing one may here remark that the Echinus Diadema Linn., like
other sea-urchins which have the mouth and vent directly opposite one
another, is divided by ten ambulacra, and the Echinus Cidaris Linn. is
the only one which has but five of them; without doubt the five far
too narrow areae of the Echinus diadema have led Herr von *Linné* into
this error.

Helmintholithus Echini lacunosi Linn. S. N. altero latere crebris
radiis tecto, marga dura albissima farctus. This body is no perfect
petrifaction, but the shell has still kept a good deal of its
substance, which is altered only in this, that it has become more
brittle and calcined through the white sand-mixed marl in which it
lay; this fine undamaged piece is three and a half inches long and two
and a half inches wide, and comes from Malta; the spines are like
those on the first noted here, but somewhat thinner.

Helmintholithus Echini rosacei Linn. S. N. lapide gypseo albissimo
farctus. This petrifaction is so large that its diameter is five and a
half inches long; the shell is very thick and hard, fine white and, at
a place where it is a little damaged, quite shining in the fracture;
it comes from the Sevennes mountains in Languedoc.

Helmintholithus radii Echini magnus integerrimus cucumerinus. This
petrifaction is perfectly whole with its little knob serving for
articulation with the wart, as if it had just been broken off the
living sea-urchin; it is two-thirds of an inch long and four lines
thick, rough-granulated all round, comes from the canton of Basel.
Beside it lies one equally whole, half as large, whose lower
circumference is somewhat thicker, which has brought about its
comparison with an acorn among naturalists; it is ferruginous and
comes from Basoeille in Lorraine.

Helmintholithus radii echini cornu Damae referens. This petrifaction
comes from Verona; its extraordinary figure, like no known sea-urchin
spine, would hardly let it be recognised, if the knob serving for
articulation and the substance of the broken pieces did not make a
proof whither they must be brought. In general it is remarkable that
most petrifactions of sea-urchin spines are of kinds whose originals
from the sea are still unknown, since they are all (the Bastoncini di
Santo Paolo from Malta excepted) much thicker and more bellied than
the spines coming from the sea.

**[p. 16]** *Tab. 2. Fig. c.* Helmintholitus echini spatagi Linn. S. N.
ambulacro secundario quasi sagittali bina anteriora binis
posterioribus jungente, cote grysea farctus. This petrifaction departs
from all hitherto known Spatagi; the mouth is as usual below, but the
vent behind at two-thirds of its height; in front is a notch without
marks of the two dotted lines which Linnaeus calls ambulacrum; the two
front ones of these doubled lines arise, as usual, on the front height
and run fairly straight to the mouth; the two hind ambulacra come from
the hind height of the back and go curved to the mouth; between the
front and hind ambulacra run, on the height of the back, two dotted
lines, which distinguish this petrifaction from that which Herr Joh.
Jakob d'Anone J. U. D. has made known in the fourth volume of the Act.
Helvet. pag. 275, Tab. XIV, Fig. 1, 2, 3. This petrifaction comes from
the Birs near St. Jakob, above Basel, and has suffered so much by the
motion in this strongly running water that in many places the shell is
worn off; it is 1⅓ inches long and one inch wide.

… One also finds not a few petrifactions, of various kinds, on which
the basis of this kind of Lepas still lies, such as the Entrochus Act.
Helv. Tom. IV. Tab. VII. Fig. 9, Belemnites, boring shells etc., which
the owner of this collection has made recognisable by the destruction
of some Lepas from the sea. …

## Letter 7, pp. 32–33: Gessner's cabinet at Zürich

**[p. 32]** … a Calyx Encrini or Lapis pentagonus, which, from the side
where the stalk was seated, is not, as one commonly finds, convex from
the centre toward the tips of the five segments, but concave (d).
Further, an excellent piece is a Monoculus, Cancer Moluccanus dictus,
in a white slate, from Solenhofen in the Pappenheim district; this has
6 inches of length (e). A singular kind of the star-fishes I also find
here, in white slate, also from Solenhofen. The body or centre of it is
not to be seen in the stone, but one sees distinctly the rays, of which
2 or 3 are stretched out, the others lie hidden and in disorder, 10 in
number altogether. One could name it **Asterias geniculata, radiis
pinnatis geniculatis, pinnis quam plurimum alternis**; it has much
likeness to the one drawn by *Baier* (f). Of the Corallio
equisetiformi geniculato there were here two single genicula, one of
which is whole and 2 inches long, **[p. 33]** but pointed at both
ends; white in colour, from Messina in Sicily (g). …

## Letter 9, pp. 40–41: Doctor Amman's cabinet at Schaffhausen

**[p. 40]** And now I will tell you something of a cabinet. This is
possessed by a great connoisseur of such things, the physician here,
Herr Doctor *Amman*. … That the Randberg has supplied much that is
fine here one can easily think, and they are almost all choice and
complete pieces which Herr Amman has deemed worthy of a place in his
collection, from other places as well as from there. Those which have
seemed to me the finest among them are the following:

Sea-urchin spines furnished with pointed prominences, or thorny, from
Giengen in Swabia (a); saw-shaped three-sided ones, from there (b), and
smaller of the kind from the Randen; also two kinds, differing in size
and otherwise, of small ones still sitting on their little knobs,
which are called needles, from there (c); Echinites themselves, with
such needles still lying on them (d), and spines (e); and others burst
open, which are filled with oolites; a flat Echinite with a raised
star on the back, from Spain; another still flatter with a flat star,
from Malta (f); a warted round one, with uncommon, and others with
common warts, 3 inches across, from the Randen; various small crabs of
brownish stone, outside their matrix, from Coromandel; leaf
impressions in sandstone, from Winterthur; the like in whitish slate,
from Oeningen; and from the same place a very fine Ichthyolite 1¼ feet
long, together with others also fairly large, in black slate, from
Glarus; two black flinty slates from Württemberg, with the Medusae
described by *Hiemer* (g). One of these slates is almost entirely
covered with the bent Medusa-rays which rise out of the stone; the
other shows on its body, which is otherwise similar enough to the
known sea-Medusa's head, but begins to divide right from the centre,
**[p. 41]** a stalk which is stretched out straight and unbent, whose
half thickness projects out of the slate, and which nothing but
five-sided trochites, flower- or star-shaped on their flat face, have
composed (h). Both slabs, which come from the Gmelin collection, are
perfectly fine, and, if I remember rightly, about 1 foot wide and 1½
feet long. Pieces which are worthy of one of the foremost places in
the cabinet of a great prince!

## Letter 38 (Geneva, 5 October 1763), pp. 266–267: the de Luc cabinet

**[p. 266]** … [I must] mention an almost chalky sea-urchin, not to
admire which would be called being no connoisseur of petrifactions at
all. This is an Echinita mammillaris, which sits on flint and is filled
with flint, from England, where one of the Messrs de L. bought it at
an auction. What I had long doubtingly wished, namely to be convinced
by eyesight that the Lapides Judaici, melones dicti, also
cucumeriformes, were true spines of an Echinus, that has at last been
granted me here. On this Echinite, namely, these Judensteine still sit
fast, and indeed 8 of them. They are of unequal thickness and length,
in consideration of their round head and more or less long stalk at
once, which latter's foot fits exactly on one of the breast-shaped
warts, which in the sea have presumably all borne such singular
spines. The largest of them has a head of ½ inch length and an almost
inch-long stalk, which seems to be jointed in the middle; it was bent
down toward the chalky-flinty base on which the Echinite rests.
However, a more detailed description and exact figure of the whole
Echinite as well as of the spines will shortly appear publicly in the
Mémoires étrangers, since the Messrs de Luc have sent it in to the
French Académie roy. des Sciences (q). I add only that some of these
spines can still be moved to and fro.

What do you say, Sir, to this instructive and incomparable piece? Is a
similar one already known to you? Echinites with remains of short
spines, which are called needles, Aciculas, one meets indeed here and
there, and I can show you such from my own collection. But still
really furnished with spines of that kind, that is something
extraordinarily rare. … And so I can tell you from the account of a
traveller that in the cabinet of an amateur in London, who is an
actor, there are still a pair of Echinites which are equally
remarkable with the one described above, and one of which still has
spines lying on it, of the kind called cucumber-shaped. …

## Letter 48, p. 339: closing survey of the Swiss cabinets

**[p. 339]** Petrifactions too I bring back, some found by myself,
some received as gifts; but they are not of the rarest, nor of the
finest. Here a twofold deficiency occurs to me, which shows itself in
the cabinets of Switzerland. None of them, namely, contains a complete
Encrinum with a long stalk, such as are at home at Lukeln in the
Braunschweig country; and yet the joints of their stalks, the common
round Rädersteine, are found often enough. But where does one not find
these?

One is truly forced by this difference to conclude that the body of
the sea-plant animal from which the Encrini derive must be of very
unequal substance. Without doubt the head or body is a soft slimy
being, more subject to decay, but the tail or stalk harder,
cartilaginous. However, *Gruner* (d) assures that once an **Encrinus
Lilium lapideum** was found in a quarry near Schinznach, which was
quite excellent, but did not itself remain in Switzerland.

The second piece which I miss in the Swiss cabinets, and indeed
entirely, is the singular petrifaction which, to my knowledge, is at
home only in England near Dudley, and has the name, most commonly, of
Dudley-Fossil (e). …
