During the summer of 1878, the family of Major General S. A. Plautin was staying in Reval for the spa season. The entire household busied itself enthusiastically with collecting local Silurian fossils; the General's wife, however, showed particular zeal and interest, and it is to her kindness that I owe—along with other valuable specimens—the two examples marked Figs. 1 and 2 in the accompanying woodcut (as well as the pieces illustrated in Figs. II 2 and 3). They have grown upon *Calopora heterosolen* Dyb. (*Chaetetes heterosole* Keys.) and belong to what I now term the Echinosphaerites Bed (C₁); this forms the uppermost layer of the Estonian Klint and overlies the true *Vaginatus* or *Orthoceras* Limestone (B). My Echinosphaerites Bed corresponds largely to the Swedish *Chasmops* Limestone.

Among the aforementioned finds, I soon recognized a new form of cystoid related to *Agelacrinus* Van. (in the broad sense), or a new genus belonging to the group of Edrioasteroidea established by Billings¹)—a group that includes *Edrioaster* Bill., *Hemicystis* Hall, and *Cystaster* Hall, in addition to *Agelacrinus* Van., and which, according to him, is more closely related to the asteroids than to the cystoids.

*Cyathocystis plautinae* consists of a cup-shaped, bluntly pentagonal calyx (sometimes with two calyces fused together, as in Fig. 2) which is usually found in isolation and only rarely bears a flatly arched lid—the very feature that makes it possible to interpret the fossil in question. This lid, which has a regular, bluntly pentagonal shape, is fitted into the upper rim of the calyx and consists of five ambulacral rays (each composed of two alternating rows of small plates, Fig. 3a) that alternate with an equal number of triangular, slightly concave interambulacral plates (Fig. 3b), each formed from a single piece. In the center of the entire lid plate, one sees five small, irregularly five-sided plates (Fig. 3c) resting upon the inner tips of the interambulacral plates; by analogy with asteroids, we designate these as oral plates¹). These oral plates are connected laterally to the adjacent ambulacral plates; in the center, however, they do not close together regularly, and in this respect, they are reminiscent of the oral closure (according to Volborth) seen in *Glyptosphaerites leuchtenbergi* Volb.

The entire "lid" of our *Cyathocystis* is surrounded on all sides by a row of marginal plates (Fig. 3d)—a feature reminiscent of asteroids²)—whereas the characteristic valvular pyramid of the cystoids (Fig. 3e), composed of five triangular plates fitting together regularly, appears on one of the triangular interambulacral plates (situated between the latter and the marginal plates). No regular pores, such as those found in most cystoids, are visible on *Cyathocystis*. The surface of the individual plates is finely granular (Fig. II, 1). The ambulacra taper towards the margin and exhibit a fine longitudinal groove along their crest, running from the margin to the mouth.

On a damaged, partially destroyed ambulacrum (Fig. II, 1a), I believe I can discern traces of an inner double row of ambulacral plates, which would correspond to Billings's "ambulacral ossicles" (loc. cit., at *a*). On this same specimen (Fig. II, 1), the considerable thickness (approx. 1 mm) of the large interambulacral plates is evident; their lateral faces appear to be shallowly grooved, such that the outer ambulacral platelets attach at the upper edge of the groove, while the inner ones appear to attach at the lower edge.

In the case of Hall's specimens (Report of the New York State Museum, Pl. VI, Figs. 1–3), too, it is possible to distinguish between outer and inner ambulacral plates. The figures cited belong to *Hemicystites* (subgen. *Cystaster*) *granulatus* Hall, a species that bears a certain resemblance to our *Cyathocystis*. The ambulacral rays, oral plates, and anal(?) pyramid are present in a similar fashion, whereas the marginal plates and large triangular interambulacral plates are absent. I have recently come to doubt the interpretation of the valvular pyramid (here and in other cystoids) as the mouth—a view I had previously adopted, following Lovén and opposing Volborth, in my last paper (*Miscellanea Silurica II*). All the original specimens are being sent to Prof. Lovén in Stockholm, who will use them for his major work on cystoids.

The most distinctive feature of our *Cyathocystis*—setting it apart from all its relatives—is its solid, single-piece calyx, which sits parasitically upon other fossils (mostly chaetetids) and is attached to them by short anchoring roots (Fig. II. 3). The calyx wall is 1 to 2 mm thick; it consists of crystalline calcite lamellae arranged much like those in crinoid stems—a structural characteristic that, when the calyces were first discovered in isolation, allowed researchers to infer a close relationship to crinoids. The outer surface of the calyces displays a fine, transversely wrinkled epitheca and, in some places (Fig. II, 2 and 3 at *a*; also on the original specimen of Fig. I, 1), vertical lines or sutures—apparently one on each side—indicating interruptions in the continuity of the shell, similar to the vertical divisions observed in the stem ossicles of *Echinoencrinus angulosus*. As previously mentioned, "twin" forms—calyces fused in pairs (Fig. I, 2)—also occur; these appear to result from lateral budding of the calyces rather than from simple fusion.

The view regarding budding just expressed is further reinforced by an examination of the other species of *Cyathocystis*—originating from the Hemicosmites Limestone of Wassalem (in the Jewe Beds of Estonia)—which we name *C. rhizophora* (Figs. III, 1–4 and I, 4) due to the more robust development of its root system, by means of which it attaches to various corals and bryozoans. In the case of *C. rhizophora*—which has never yet been found with its lid—the clustering of two or three calyces is quite common (Figs. III, 1, 4); indeed, even in the specimen illustrated in Fig. 4b, one can see that the three calyces are not only interconnected at the base but that the fused calyx base is traversed by a common nutrient canal, which in the aforementioned specimen is surrounded by densely packed crystalline calcareous lamellae. The calyx walls in *C. rhizophora* are far more massive than in *C. plautinae*, and the calyx cavity does not extend as deeply, precisely because the calyx base—along with its root-like extensions—is much more massive in *C. rhizophora*.

Vertical lines—or rather, sutures—are also clearly visible (in Fig. III 3b, a divergent arrangement of the calcareous plates composing the calyx wall can be seen at this suture); furthermore, distinct recessed pits are discernible at certain points (Figs. III 2a, 3a) on the outer wall of the calyx—features that, incidentally, also occur in traces in *C. plautinae*. On the curved calyx shown in Fig. III 3, individual blunt protuberances (similar to those sometimes seen in *C. plautinae*) can be observed along the outer edges; these are to be regarded as points of growth. The rugose epitheca is present, just as in the typical species (most clearly visible in the original specimen shown in Fig. III 1). As previously mentioned, I have long been familiar (since 1863) with the calyces of *Cyathocystis rhizophora* from the Hemicosmites Limestone of Wassalem (belonging to the uppermost stage of the Jewe Beds); at that time, I collected several specimens, most of which—along with my entire collection from that period—were turned over to the Volborth Collection (now the property of the Academy of Sciences). I could not account for these enigmatic calyces and—based solely on the nature of the calyx wall—could only infer a probable relationship to the crinoids, which were very common there. Similar specimens were later found near Reval but received little attention until I finally obtained the two complete specimens illustrated in Figs. I, 1 and 2 from General’s wife Plautin, in whose honor we named our *Cyathocystis plautina*.
