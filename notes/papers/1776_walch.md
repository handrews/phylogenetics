I. A Fossilized Ostracion.

This rare specimen, which I received from His Serene Highness the Hereditary Prince of Schwarzburg-Rudolstadt from his excellent cabinet, I have had depicted on Plate V, Nos. 1 and 2. It appears here in its natural size. It is almost perfectly spherical and covered with its still naturally hard, fossilized shell. The matrix, as can be seen through a small round opening, is a sandy limestone. On one side, a round opening is visible, which has a somewhat raised edge, and above it, on both sides, two smaller ones (Fig. b), one of which, however, is almost completely covered with a blackish, stony deposit. Above these two small openings, a very small protrusion or elevation is noticeable on both sides (Fig. c), one of which, however, where the blackish deposit is, is no longer clearly visible and appears to be somewhat worn away. The most remarkable features are the regular lines and stripes with which the shell of this body is marked. They are all raised, approximately the thickness of a strong thread. They form hexagons, each of which encloses a six-pointed, and here and there a multi-pointed, star, composed of these raised stripes, which all extend to the aforementioned hexagonal border, meet it, and are of the same height. On the other side, opposite the aforementioned opening, the raised stripes converge closely together in a central point, in which a very small opening is observed. As far as I can see, there are only four kinds of natural bodies that can be compared to this peculiar petrifaction in order to perhaps discover its original form.

The first body is a type of sea urchin, which appears on Knorr's plates of petrifactions, Suppl. X. a, Nos. 3 and 4, and which I described in the natural history of fossils in the third part, p. 229. At that time, I only had the specimen from Genzmer before me, and it was the very first one that I made known to naturalists. Afterwards, I was fortunate enough to receive this rare petrifaction from a friend. Even now, only the bare nucleus remains, on which, however, not much can be discerned, and the impression of the former natural shell, which, however, clearly shows the raised stripes of the former echinoid shell. This type of echinoid is round, the shell is covered with raised stripes, which also form hexagons, which, like in our specimen, enclose six-rayed star figures. The raised stripes are not simple, as in our specimen, but double, sometimes triple or quadruple. In the indentations on the stone, which formerly appeared as raised features on the natural body, one notices very small raised warts and elongated holes. The actual original of this echinoid has not yet been discovered, but it seems to be perhaps a special species of the echinocidaris afterizante of the late Klein's echinoderms, Tab. VIII. F. For it has small star-shaped rays around its tubercles.

The second specimen could be either Donati's Tethya sphaerion (a), or another genus of Alcyonium aurantium, of which there are several genera that have not yet been sufficiently studied. For it is clear that some must possess a vascular tissue or a peculiar arrangement of certain muscles and fibers that, in the fossilized state, presents a body covered with hexagonal plates, as can be plainly seen in the fossilized specimen that I described in the third part of my Natural History of Fossils, in Chapter 4, page 165, from the Genzmer cabinet, and which is illustrated in Knorr's Petrifaction Plates, Supplement IV, D, Number 8. If one compares this specimen with our fossilized body, one will find some similarity between the two with regard to the round shape of the hexagons with which the body is, as it were, covered.

The third body is the Astroites fauites, which I described in the fifth part of the Naturalist, p. 43, and noted that the stars on it have a five- or six-sided figure.

The fourth body I find among Linnaeus's Amphibia natantia. There is, namely, a certain living marine product that was formerly a fish, but is now an amphibian. It bears the name Ostracion and is covered with a bony shell that is divided into numerous hexagonal plates or fields.

Now the question arises, among which of these four bodies is the original of this very rare fossil to be sought? I will briefly share my opinion on this.

It is certainly not an Echinus asterizans, because it has no mouth opening on the base, which all and every echinus must have there. Furthermore, it has neither a trace of former pores, nor of any warts or elevations on which its spines once sat. Both are necessary to characterize an echinoid with complete certainty. Since both are lacking here, our fossil cannot have been an echinoid. In addition, the Echinus asterizans has a completely different pattern on its surface than our fossilized body, which is clearly revealed by a comparison of both the Klein genus in his Echinodermata and the Genzmer fossil in my Natural History of Fossils.

I can just as little consider it an Alcyonium aurantium. The crust in our fossil is not spatulate, and it lacks the osculum characteristic of Alcyonium. If one were to consider the aforementioned opening as such, one would be mistaken, because the oscula of Alcyonium are deeply concave and usually have irregular, often branched incisions, both of which are completely absent in our fossil. Moreover, although the Alcyonium that I described in my natural history of fossils seems to exhibit five- and six-sided fields, they are far from as regular as those on our specimen; they have neither a raised border nor a star-shaped formation within their periphery, as our fossil does.

The fourth body mentioned, namely the Ostracion, bears the greatest resemblance to our fossil. Such an Ostracion is covered with a bony armor that appears to be composed of numerous hexagonal plates or fields. The plates themselves are marked in different ways depending on the species. Some are covered with small warts, others are dotted, in still others the angles are intersected by pointed short lines, and finally there are some in which, as in our fossil, each plate has a star-shaped figure formed by raised lines. Such Ostracions can be found in detailed drawings in the third part of Seba's Thesaurus on the 24th copperplate, especially in Willughby's Historia Piscium, Tab. J. 9. et seq. These drawings show more than clearly that the star-shaped bony covering of these Ostracions corresponds very closely to that of our fossil, especially to those which, as Artedi says (Ichthyol. gen. 56. syn. 85.), have prominent edges of hexagonal figures, even though Artedi's genus is different from the one from which our fossil originates. These Ostracions, like our fossil, have a round, slightly protruding mouth opening, above which are the two eyes, of which equally clear traces can be found in our fossil. In some, two conical spines or horns are located at a short distance from the eyes, which our fossil also possessed in its natural state. These Ostracions also have a tail that emerges from the bony covering, specifically from a round opening at the end, of which some trace can also be detected. These Ostracions are generally angular, and are therefore usually divided into triangular and quadrangular types. However, this angular shape should not be taken too literally. Willughby's certainly accurate drawings convince us that among them there are also many genera whose imperceptible corners would give them a far greater right to be classified among the round rather than the angular fish shapes. This much is certain: among the spiny or globular fish there are small varieties that, in size and round shape, correspond as closely to our fossil as one egg does to another. Such examples can be found in the Deliciae Naturae Selectae, Vol. III, No. 5, in Willughby, Tab. J. 8, No. 1, Jonston Pisc. Tab. XXIV, XXV, and in Professor Müller's German edition of Linnaeus's Systema Naturae, Tab. X, Fig. 1.

The only difference is that these pufferfish have spines, while ours has the bony covering typical of the angular Ostracion, divided into hexagonal plates. From this, in my opinion, it cannot be concluded that our fossil does not represent an Ostracion. The pufferfish with spines are, even according to the Linnaean system, far too closely related to the Ostracions for nature not to have created, in this short step from one genus to another, certain intermediate forms that combine similarities of both genera, uniting the round shape of the pufferfish with the bony, star-shaped armor of the Ostracions. This is the law of nature, and it is a real pleasure for the naturalist, who, based on the ever-consistent plan that nature has established, can reasonably assume the existence of certain forms, when the fossil record unexpectedly reveals them, whereas the natural body often only presents itself to the eyes of posterity after many years and through a chance occurrence. However, in this case, our descendants don't even need to see this. The Ostracions described by Willughby already show such a round body shape that we don't need to wait for new discoveries to confidently identify our fossil as a fossilized Ostracion, since all other essential characteristics of an Ostracion are perfectly combined in this fossilized body. Since it depicts the position of the mouth, eyes, and horns in exactly the same way as in natural Ostracion specimens, and since an Ostracion is particularly well-suited for fossilization compared to many other organisms, and since shields of such Ostracion specimens have already been found, it is, if not entirely certain, then at least highly probable that a round, spherical Ostracion was the original of our fossil.
