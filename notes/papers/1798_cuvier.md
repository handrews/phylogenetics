# Introduction

**[p. 1]** **Chapter I. Of natural history in general, and of its relations with the other sciences.**

§ 1. Natural science, or physics, has for its objects the mobile and extended beings which we call bodies. This science divides into general and particular. The first considers in isolation the properties common to all bodies or to the greater number of them. Thus the part named dynamics treats of the general laws of motion and of its communication, of the force which carries bodies **[p. 2]** toward one another and which holds their molecules in the state of adherence or cohesion, etc. Chemistry sets out the laws according to which the elementary molecules of bodies act on one another at near distances, etc.

§ 2. There is almost no natural body which does not present applications of all the general natural sciences, whether in its relations with other bodies or in those which its various parts have among themselves. This is the object of particular physics or natural history, which is the knowledge of the various natural bodies.

All knowledge is double: 1°. that of all that the senses can teach us: size, form, internal and external structure, movements, actions, in a word, of all the sensible properties or events; 2°. that of the explanation of these events, of these properties, and of the effects they occasion, that is to say, of the demonstration of their **[p. 3]** conformity with the general laws of the physical and mathematical sciences, if it is a question of purely physical effects, or with the general laws of the moral and psychological sciences, if it is a question of moral effects.

§ 3. The particular natural history of any body whatever must therefore, to be perfect, comprise: 1°. the description of all the sensible properties of this body, and of all its parts; 2°. the relations of these parts among themselves, the movements which take place there, and the changes they undergo so long as they remain united; 3°. the active and passive relations of this body with all the other bodies of the universe; 4°. finally the explanation of all these phenomena. One may say that we do not yet possess completely the natural history of any body.

§ 4. General natural history considers from a single point of view all natural bodies and the common result of all their actions **[p. 4]** in the great whole of nature. It determines the laws of coexistence of their properties; for such and such of these properties necessarily supposes or excludes a certain number of others. It establishes the degrees of resemblance which exist between the different bodies, and groups them according to these degrees. It can be brought to its perfection only when the particular histories of all natural bodies have been completed.

**Chapter II. Of the organisation and the properties of organised bodies.**

§ 1. An inorganic or brute body, such as a stone, etc., is formed of molecules which have among themselves no other relations than those of coherence and adhesion, which do not form a common whole. It can be separated into fragments which will all be of the same nature as the whole body. Brute bodies are formed only by the unions of molecules conformable to the laws of **[p. 5]** chemistry, increase only by new molecules which come to set themselves against the first, and are destroyed only when the molecules which compose them are separated and dispersed.

§ 2. An organised body, such as a plant, an animal, is composed of a tissue of solids which contain fluids in motion. All its parts have a reciprocal action on one another, and concur toward a common end, which is the maintenance of life. Organised bodies are born of bodies similar to them, of which they at first form part, to separate from them at determined epochs and in determined circumstances. They grow by attracting without cease, by a force proper to them, new molecules which come to interpose themselves in the intervals of those which already existed. They die when, the action of their solids and the movement of their fluids being interrupted, the molecules which compose them are abandoned to their own forces, and act on one another to combine and form brute bodies.

**[p. 6]** § 3. With regard to structure, organised bodies vary to infinity by the number of their fluids, the forms, the nature, the relations of their solids. We shall see in man an example of the most perfect and most complicated organisation, and we shall follow in the other animals the different degrees by which they approach more or less to simplicity.

§ 4. The property of nourishing itself or growing by intus-susception is a force particular to organised bodies, which they exercise during their whole life; it holds their molecules together, and attracts others, overcoming the physical and chemical laws which would govern them in their free state: so these molecules do not delay, soon after death, to separate to form new compounds. Organised bodies exercise this attractive force by their whole surface. Plants exercise it only by the exterior surface, and above all by their roots. Animals have in addition an interior cavity which they fill with food, and whose walls are the principal source of their nutrition.

**[p. 7]** § 5. Generation is the sole way by which new organised bodies are formed. All those which exist formerly made part of bodies similar to them, from which they have separated. This separation is made in several ways. In plants and in the simplest animals, a fragment separated from the total individual becomes again with time an individual similar to that of which it formed an integral part: this is generation by cutting. The same plants and the same simplest animals also produce, in certain places of their bodies, buds which contain small organised bodies similar, size apart, to those which produce them; they separate from them and form beings apart: this is generation by gemmae, bulbils or buds. But the most common mode is generation by eggs or seeds. The small germ is enclosed, with the portion of nourishment which will be necessary to it during the first period, in an envelope more or less compact, of which it rids itself when it has taken a certain growth. **[p. 8]** Eggs or seeds do not develop spontaneously like buds; there must beforehand be a fecundation, that is to say, an action by which these eggs and these seeds are, so to speak, awakened and set in play. The organs which contain them are named female organs; those which fecundate them, male organs. They are sometimes united in a single individual, sometimes separated in different individuals: this is what one names the sexes. The nature of this property of engendering is quite as unknown as that of the property of nourishing oneself.

§ 6. Independently of the accidents which cause death to organised beings by destroying some one of their essential parts, it arrives spontaneously to all at certain epochs, by the very effect of their life, and, as it appears, by the engorgement which nutrition produces in their vessels.

§ 7. All that we have just said suits all organised bodies, animals as well as vegetables; but the former have besides, above **[p. 9]** the latter, an essential property, that of moving themselves at will in whole or in part. It appears likely enough that this faculty is always linked to that of perceiving what passes within them and around them. Animals possess the one and the other in very different degrees, according to their different degrees of perfection.

**Chapter III. Of species and varieties in natural history.**

§ 1. Although organised bodies produce only bodies similar to themselves, there are circumstances which alter up to a certain point their primitive form in the succession of their generations: thus a less abundant nourishment makes the young acquire less size and strength. A more or less cold climate, a more or less humid air, a more or less continuous exposure to light, produce analogous effects; but it is above all the care given by **[p. 10]** man to the animal or vegetable productions which he raises for his use, the sustained attention with which he limits them to a nourishment, an exercise, or an exposure different from those nature had destined for them, which change their properties more promptly and more perceptibly.

§ 2. When the offspring of an organised body have thus departed more or less from the form of their stock, one says that they have varied. One has determined up to a certain point, by experience, which are the variable properties in organised bodies, which are the causes that produce each variation, what is the degree of variability of the former, and the degree of influence of the latter: but this work is still very imperfect.

§ 3. It has been remarked that the most variable properties in organised bodies are size and colour. The first depends above all on the abundance of nourishment; the second, on the influence of light, and on several other causes so hidden that it often appears to vary by pure chance. However, the **[p. 11]** variations of the one and the other of these qualities are enclosed within certain limits which one can determine by observation.

§ 4. The length and thickness of hairs are very variable. A hairy plant, transported into humid ground, becomes there almost smooth. Animals lose their hair in hot countries, increase it in cold countries, etc. The number of certain exterior parts is sometimes found increased or diminished (the stamens, the petals, the teeth, etc.); parts of little importance change proportion, lengthen or shorten (the beards of ears of grain, etc.); parts of analogous nature change into one another (the stamens into petals in double flowers, etc.).

§ 5. The collection of all the organised bodies born of one another, or of common parents, and of all those which resemble them as much as they resemble each other, is named a species. The organised bodies which differ or appear to differ from a species only by **[p. 12]** accidental causes, similar to those stated above, pass for varieties of this species.

§ 6. The notion of species thus resting on the supposition that all the beings which compose it could be reciprocally ancestors and descendants, it is only by conjecture that one can refer to it as a variety such another being which differs from it more or less. One had, in truth, proposed as a general rule for recognising oneself in this regard, that individuals of different species could not, by their mixture, produce fertile individuals. This assertion rests on no proof; but at least it is constant that individuals of the same species, however different they may be, can always produce together.

§ 7. To believe that two more or less different beings are only varieties of one and the same species, it is necessary: 1°. that the properties by which they differ be of the class of those which have been recognised as variable; 2°. that there be causes of variation; **[p. 13]** 3°. that they can by mixing produce fertile individuals. Thus, two wild races which inhabit the same places, the same climate, without mixing and while always preserving their differences, must be regarded as distinct species, however small these differences may be: with all the more reason when they are a little considerable, and concern even the internal framework and the organisation of the parts. But one cannot conclude reciprocally that when two different races mix and produce intermediate and fertile individuals, they are of the same species, and were not originally different.

§ 8. It appears that in the beginning each species of animal, and even of plant, existed only in a determined country, from which it spread according to the means its conformation gave it. Even today several of them seem to have been bounded around similar original centres, either by the seas when they could neither swim nor fly, or by **[p. 14]** temperatures they could not bear, or by mountains they could not cross, etc. The varieties of each must have been the stronger and more numerous, the farther the circumstances of the places or of its nature have permitted it to extend: this is what may make one believe that the great differences found among men, dogs and the other beings spread over the whole world, are only effects of accidental causes, in a word, varieties. It must however be remarked that certain species have been found the same in very distant climates, separated by great spaces of sea, without these species being in the intermediate climates.

**Chapter IV. Of the natural relations of organised beings.**

§ 1. The differences and the resemblances of brute bodies result from the elements of which they are composed. Organised bodies, on the contrary, are almost all composed of the same **[p. 15]** elements. Their differences and their resemblances consist above all in their form and their structure, or, in a single word, in their organisation. This organisation passes from fathers to children. It is therefore the result of a force which is transmitted by generation, whose origin goes back to that of organised bodies themselves, and whose nature is unknown.

§ 2. Any two species whatever of organised beings have necessarily some points of organisation by which they resemble each other. These points of resemblance are what one names their natural relations. The more numerous they are, the greater these relations are.

§ 3. Experience teaches us that relations are not distributed at random among species, but that some are more constant than others. Thus, suppose one examines, for example, all the species which resemble each other by three quarters of their properties, and which differ only by a quarter; this quarter of differences will not bear indiscriminately on all the points of organisation; **[p. 16]** but there will be a certain number of invariable ones, which will be found the same in all those species.

§ 4. These relations more constant than the others are those which hold to the most important parts of the organic economy. As all the parts of this economy form only a single whole, there are some which exercise a more general influence, which impress their action on all the others; there are on the contrary some which exercise only a limited and local action, and which influence the general system only very little. The differences which these unimportant parts can undergo from species to species therefore do not necessarily entail differences in the other parts, and they can vary although all the others resemble each other. On the contrary, the important parts cannot undergo considerable difference without all the others feeling it; and the more these important parts differ from one species to another, the more also these species differ in **[p. 17]** their whole organisation, the fewer relations they have.

§ 5. The most constant relations are therefore at the same time the most important relations, the superior relations; and those which are more variable are the subordinate relations. Thus the constancy of a relation once determined by experience, one can conclude from it the importance of the part from which this relation is taken; and, vice versa, when reasoning shows us the importance of a part, one can conclude from it that the relations drawn from it will be very constant.

**Chapter V. Of methods and of nomenclature in natural history.**

§ 1. The number of the productions of nature being immense, it has been necessary to find means of distinguishing them and of recognising each of them surely. These means are the **[p. 18]** particularities or the assemblages of particularities exclusively proper to each. Now there is almost no being which has a unique character, that is to say, which can be distinguished from all the others by a single one of its properties. There is hardly anything but the combination of several of these properties which can distinguish a being from the neighbouring beings, which indeed also have some of these properties of the first being, but do not have them all, or have them combined with others which it lacks; and the more numerous the species of beings one compares, the more of their properties one must unite to assign to each a character which distinguishes it surely from every other. Thus, to distinguish a species considered in isolation from all those which exist in nature, one would have to express in its character the near totality of its properties, that is to say, to give an almost complete description of it.

§ 2. One avoids this inconvenience by the use of graduated characters; one compares together only a certain number of the nearest species. Their characters need **[p. 19]** express only their differences, which, by the supposition that these species are the nearest, make only the least part of their properties. Such a union of species is called a genus.

§ 3. The rest of these properties, those which are common to all the species of the genus, form together the character, or rather the description of the genus, which distinguishes it from all those one could form by uniting other species; but the number of these common properties being still very considerable, one employs anew the same means to reduce the characters of the genera to fewer terms. One compares together only the nearest genera, and the generic characters have only to express their differences, which again make the least part of their properties. Those which are common to all form the character which distinguishes their entire union from the other unions of genera. Such a union of genera is named an order.

§ 4. By repeating the same operation one unites the neighbouring orders to form a class; **[p. 20]** the neighbouring classes to form a kingdom. One can likewise establish intermediate degrees between the kingdoms and the classes, between the classes and the orders, between the orders and the genera, between the genera and the species. This scaffolding of divisions, of which the superior comprise the inferior, is called a method.

§ 5. One sees that the higher one rises, the more constant also are the properties which remain common; and as the most constant relations are those which belong to the most important parts, the characters of the superior divisions will be found drawn from the most important parts, and in proportion as one descends to the less general divisions, one will see that their characters will be drawn from less essential parts.

§ 6. This subordination of characters furnishes a means of forming a method of natural beings, without being obliged to begin by comparing them all with one another. If one takes care to make one's first cuts according to the differences found in the most **[p. 21]** important parts, and follows a similar march in the inferior cuts, the species will be found grouped according to their natural relations; those of a genus will be more similar among themselves than they will resemble those of any other genus; the genera of an order will be more similar among themselves than to those of any other order, etc. The method will be what is called a natural method.

§ 7. But for that one would have to have determined in advance, with exactness, the degree of importance of each organ; and this work not being yet executed, one cannot limit oneself rigorously to the subordination of characters: one must employ also the detailed comparison of species; and as one does not know them all, one is often reduced, in the formation of natural methods, to a simple groping.

§ 8. One could also divide beings by beginning with the primitive cuts, without regard to the importance of the parts from which one would draw the characters: provided these characters were well marked, one would succeed **[p. 22]** equally in distinguishing the species; but such a method, which is called an arbitrary or artificial method, would have only this single advantage; it would not make known the relations which the species have among themselves, and it would be impossible to sum up their properties in order to form general propositions from them.

§ 9. If each species had a proper name, the number of these names would overload the memory too much. It has been agreed to give to all the species of a genus the same substantive name, and to distinguish them from one another by a single adjective taken from some one of their properties, and which is called the trivial name; but this convenient usage is still adopted only in the Latin language: it would be desirable that one could introduce it also into the vulgar tongues.

# Book VI. Des Mollusques

**[p. 372]** **Chapter I. Of the white-blooded animals in general, and in particular of the Mollusques.**

§ 1. The white-blooded animals do not have as many common characters as those with red blood; they even appear to have only negative ones, such as the absence of a vertebral **[p. 373]** column, and of an articulated interior skeleton, etc. We must therefore limit ourselves to considering them successively, and to indicating the various degradations which their organisation undergoes, and the principal divisions which result from them.

§ 2. Those of the white-blooded animals which are best provided with organs have a muscular heart, into which the nutritive liquor arrives by the veins, and from which it leaves by the arteries; organs rather similar to the gills of fishes, in which this liquor is exposed to the influence of the ambient element; glands which pour into the alimentary canal different digestive liquors. One observes in them a brain, nerves, and some organs of the senses; but there is, in this regard, more variation than in the rest. Their body or at least their limbs have no bones in the interior; but several of them are enveloped in very solid cases, or even stony ones, which are named shells (testa), and bear in particular the name of testaceous: we shall comprise them, with those which are entirely naked, under the common name of *Mollusques*.

**[p. 374]** § 3. The animals to which the name of insects has been given, because their body is divided by kinds of constrictions, also have white blood, and lack hard parts in the interior; but they have no muscular heart. One supposes, without proof, that its functions are in part supplied by a vessel which runs all along their back, and in which one perceives a liquor in motion. Insects have no brain properly so called, but only a spinal marrow swollen from space to space into knots or tubercles, from which the nerves depart. The air necessary to their life penetrates into their body by vessels named tracheae, open at their sides, and ramifying in their interior. Each of their articulations is enclosed in a case of horny substance, which forms a ginglymus with those of the neighbouring articulations. What insects have most particular is that those among them which are to have wings do not take them at first; they are obliged to pass beforehand through two forms often very different from their perfect state, **[p. 375]** of which the first is named larva, and the second nymph. They engender only when they have arrived at their perfect state; the crayfishes and some neighbouring genera are the only perfect insects which have a muscular heart, and breathe by gills like the mollusques. There are animals with articulated body, with knotted spinal marrow, in a word very similar to larvae of insects, and which nevertheless never change figure, never obtain articulated limbs, and engender from this first state. We reserve for them the name of *vers*.

§ 4. Finally we shall give the name of *zoophytes* to the animals which have neither heart nor brain, in which each point of the body appears to be provided with the faculty of feeling, and to receive immediately its nutrition by the organs of digestion, or by its contact with the nutritive matters. There are again several different degrees of perfection among these zoophytes; the last of them even appear to be only an animated pulp or mobile atoms.

**[p. 376]** § 5. Thus the white-blooded animals divide into: I. *Mollusques*, which have a muscular heart, and no knotted spinal marrow; II. *Insectes et vers*, which have a longitudinal dorsal vessel, and a knotted spinal marrow, or at least one of the two; III. *Zoophytes*, which have neither heart, nor brain, nor nerves.

*Les Mollusques.*

§ 6. Have white muscles, very irritable, and life very tenacious. They preserve movement even after having been cut into pieces, and they reproduce very considerable parts of their body when these have been removed. Their skin is always humid; there most often oozes from it a viscous humour; it is very sensitive, and provided with organs capable of lengthening more or less to palpate better, named *tentacules*. One knows in these animals no organs of smell; but many have eyes, and some even ears. **[p. 377]** Their body is ordinarily enveloped, or at least covered in part, by a membranous mantle; several have in addition a stony envelope, named shell, of one or several pieces or valves, which is produced by a calcareous juice that transudes from the mantle. Its growth is made by new layers which glue themselves to the inner face of the first, and which always exceed them. The body of the animal is attached to it by muscles which serve to withdraw it inside or to bring the valves together. These muscles really change place, by obliterating themselves on one side and growing on the other, so as to keep always the same position relative to the parts of the shell, in spite of its unequal growth. The greater number of the mollusques inhabit the waters of the sea. There are also some in fresh waters, and some absolutely terrestrial.

§ 7. One can divide the mollusques according to their form, whatever the envelopes which clothe them. Some have the mantle in the form **[p. 378]** of a sac, from which comes out a head crowned with large tentacles on which they crawl; we shall name them *céphalopodes*. Others crawl on the belly, which is made in the form of a flat and sticky disc, and have a free and prominent head; we shall name them *gastéropodes*. Finally there are some whose head consists only in a mouth hidden under the mantle; these will be our *acéphales*. Each of these three orders contains naked genera, and others clothed in shells.

**Chapter II. Of the Céphalopode mollusques, or with body in the form of a sac, with free head, crowned by the feet.**

I. *Les Seiches.* (Sepia.) Their sac has a fin on each side, and contains toward the back a body of friable or cartilaginous substance, placed among the flesh without adherence, **[p. 379]** and going from one end to the other without articulation. The head is round, and provided with two large mobile eyes, whose organisation is almost as perfect as that of the eyes of red-blooded animals. The mouth is on the top of the head, and furnished with two jaws similar, in form and substance, to a parrot's beak; eight conical tentacles, furnished with suckers or kinds of cupping-glasses, by means of which the animal fixes itself where it wishes, surround this mouth, and form on the head a kind of crown. There are in addition two very long ones, which have suckers only at their extremity, and which serve the seiche to hold itself at anchor. Before the neck is a funnel which closes the entrance of the sac, and through which the excrements flow out; for the seiche always holds itself head downward. There is in the interior a liver, a muscular gizzard, usually followed by a caecum, and a short intestinal canal. The circulation is effected by very singular organs. A heart placed toward the bottom of the sac pushes the blood into the whole body by the arteries: the veins bring it back into the vena cava, which divides to carry it into two other hearts placed on the sides, which push it into the gills, from which it then returns into the first heart. **[p. 380]** The female lays soft eggs, united in a kind of cluster, which the male sprinkles with his milt. The seiches spread, when they perceive some danger, a black liquor which hides them by darkening the sea water far around, and which a particular gland prepares in their body. Collected and dried, this liquor furnishes China ink.

1. La seiche commune (Sepia officinalis. L.). The bone of its back is oval, very thick, of a friable substance, composed of very thin plates, in the intervals of which are a multitude of small hollow columns perpendicular to these plates. The mantle is furnished, all along its two sides, with a narrow fin. 2. Le calmar (Sepia loligo. L.). Its bone is thin, cartilaginous, transparent, and in the form of a sword blade. The body is oblong, pointed, and has only toward the end two broad triangular fins. These two species are common in the Ocean and in the Mediterranean; they live on crabs and other marine animals.

II. *Les Poulpes.* (Octopus.) Have the intestines, the hearts, the mouth, the eight tentacles and the black liquor like the seiches; but they have no solid part in the back, and they lack those two long arms which have suckers **[p. 381]** only at their extremity. In return, their tentacles are much more elongated than those of the seiches. 1. Le poulpe commun (Octopus vulgaris) Sepia octopus. L. With smooth skin, with close-set suckers. Is common in our seas, and destroys on our coasts many shellfish, crabs, and other useful animals. It becomes very large; it is even claimed that it can be dangerous to swimmers by applying its suckers to their body, and thus entwining itself around them. 2. Le poulpe ridé (Octopus rugosus) Sepia rugosa. Bosc. With wrinkled skin, with spaced suckers. From the seas of China. Spreads an odour of musk. It appears that it is from it in particular that the Chinese draw their ink.

III. *Les Argonautes.* (Argonauta.) This name has been given to shells claimed to be inhabited by an animal entirely similar to the poulpes; they are easy to distinguish from the other univalves or shells of a single piece by their form of a launch or boat with deep keel, which results from the turns of the spiral being all in the same plane, and the last, always much larger than the others together, enveloping them all. Their substance is white, and so thin that it has been compared to paper, and some call them paper nautiluses. It is said that the animal sails at will on the surface of the waters, or sinks beneath; that, in the first state, it raises two of its arms, between which is stretched **[p. 382]** a thin membrane which serves it as a sail, and that it lowers the six others to employ them in place of oars.

IV. *Les Nautiles.* (Nautilus.) Are another genus of univalve shells, which also pass for belonging to an animal similar to the poulpes. The turns of the spiral are all in the same plane, and, in the common species, the last turn envelops all the others. Their whole cavity is divided by transverse partitions into a multitude of chambers, of which the animal fills only the last: they are all traversed at their middle by a tube, in which is lodged the ligament which retains the animal; but this tube does not communicate into the chambers. The common species (nautilus pompilius) is a large swollen shell, which, stripped of its bark, presents a fine nacreous lustre; drinking vessels are made of it, which are engraved, and mounted in gold or silver.

One finds under ground, in certain beds of calcareous and other stones, a great number of fossil or petrified shells, which have some relation with the nautiles, and whose living analogues are not known; these are the *Ammonites*, or horns of Ammon, all of whose spires are in the same plane, without enveloping one another, and divide into a multitude of chambers by transverse **[p. 383]** partitions, with deeply cut edges, and of which one of the sides is pierced by a tube. There are many species of them of very different sizes. The *Orthocératites* have the same internal structure as the ammonites; but a great part of their shell is in a straight line. They are likewise known only in the fossil state. The *Camérines* are a third genus of fossil, which presents on the exterior the form of a lentil or of a coin, and which contains in the interior a canal, wound in numerous spirals, and divided into an infinity of small chambers, without a tube traversing them. It is only by conjecture that these different fossils have been placed in this order.

**Chapter III. Of the Gastéropode mollusques, or crawling on the belly, and having a free and mobile head; and in particular of the naked gastéropodes.**

The gastéropodes have under the belly a muscular plane which serves them to crawl by its contractions, as anyone can see in **[p. 384]** the slug. They have only one heart. Their gills are sometimes in the interior of the body, sometimes around the body, sometimes on the back; naked in some, and covered by some operculum in others; they take the form of plates, of leaves, of plumes or of simple networks. The common trunk of the veins subdivides to distribute to the gills the blood which returns from the body, and which from the gills returns to the heart, which sends it everywhere. Most of the gastéropodes are hermaphrodites, and need for their fecundation a reciprocal coupling. There is almost always in the neighbourhood of the womb a pouch containing a liquor whose use in the economy of these animals is unknown, but which, in several, is the colouring liquor of purple. The intestines consist in a stomach more or less thick, an intestinal canal and a voluminous liver. There is a brain and nerves. A great number of gastéropodes is clothed in shells. It would be advantageous to be able to bring each of them near the naked genera **[p. 385]** which resemble them most: but as we do not have all the knowledge necessary for that, we shall be obliged to treat them apart, and to class them principally according to the form of their shells, which are much better known, because they have been collected at all times in cabinets on account of their beauty and the ease of their preservation.

The principal genera of the naked gastéropodes are:

I. *Les Limaces.* (Limax.) Which have the body oblong, expressing at the least contraction much glutinous humour. Their back is furnished with a kind of coriaceous and wrinkled shield. The head and the four tentacles with which it is surmounted re-enter the body, and come out of it again like the fingers of a glove: the two upper tentacles each bear a small black point at their extremity; it is believed to be an eye. The mouth has a strong jaw; the gills are in the interior, and receive the air by an opening placed on the right side of the trunk; the anus is at the posterior edge of this opening; the orifice of generation is under the right tentacle. The limaces inhabit our country, are numerous in species and in **[p. 386]** individuals, feed on herbs, and do much damage in our crops, above all when the weather is humid. They are distinguished by size and colours. 1. La limace rouge (Limax rufus). 2. La limace noire (Limax ater). 3. La grande limace brune (Limax cinereus). 4. La petite limace grise (Limax agrestis), etc.

II. *Les Téthys.* (Tethys.) Have the body made nearly like the limace; but the mantle has free and floating edges, and it extends before the head into a broad veil, rounded and fringed. The mouth is under this veil, and lengthens in the form of a proboscis; the orifices of generation and of respiration are on the right side of the neck. These animals inhabit the Mediterranean sea. There is a larger species in which the edge of the veil is scalloped (tethys fimbria), and another smaller in which it is fringed (tethys leporina).

III. *Les Aplysies.* (Aplysia.) Have nearly the form of the limaces. Their eyes are on the head itself, at the foot of the upper tentacles; the lower ones are only a fold of the lip. The sides of the body are bordered by a membrane which curves back over the back. The gills are numerous vascular leaflets placed on the middle **[p. 387]** of the back, and covered by an operculum which contains between two membranes a piece nearly analogous to the bone of the calmar: the anus is on the hinder part of the back; the orifice of the male parts, under the right horn; that of the female, on the right side of the trunk. The bladder of the purple contains, according to some authors, a fetid liquor so acrid that it makes the hair fall out: others deny this particularity. The aplysies are common in the Mediterranean. The most widespread species (aplysia depilans) is of blackish colour.

IV. *Les Doris.* (Doris.) The body has the form of that of the limace; but it is flat, and bordered all around by a broad membrane, which extends even above the head. The mouth and the lower tentacles are beneath, but the upper ones are above: the gills are around the anus, on the hinder part of the back, free and without cover, and represent fringes and ramifications. 1. L'argus (Doris argo). Its upper tentacles appear each to bear a great number of eyes. The back is flat and smooth: this animal is found in the Mediterranean.

One could separate from the genus of the doris: a) *Les Tritonies* (Tritonia), which have, like the limaces, the body crawling and convex **[p. 388]** on the back. The number of the tentacles which surround the mouth varies from two to eight: the organs of respiration are kinds of plumes borne on pedicels, or bundles of fibres running all along the back. b) *Les Éolides* (Æolidia), which have the body like the tritonies; but their organs of respiration are kinds of leaves or membranous scales, arranged like tiles on the two sides of the back.

V. *Les Phyllidies.* (Phyllidia.) Have a coriaceous shield like the limaces, but which exceeds the body on all sides. The gills are small membranous leaflets, placed in file one after another like those of a book, and forming a cord, under the rim of the mantle or shield, all around the body.

VI. *Les Scyllées.* (Scyllæa.) Have the body compressed, of almost gelatinous substance. The foot is in the form of a furrow, and not of a disc, as in the preceding genera: so these animals use it to embrace the stems of fucus. At one extremity is the mouth, on which are two tentacles in the form of leaves. There are one or two similar ones on the other end. The gills are other leaflets, arranged by pairs on the back, and having, on the face by which they look at each other, tufts of vessels.

**[p. 389]** VII. *Les Thalides.* (Thalis.) (Thalia. Brug.) (Holothuria. Lin.) Have a compressed, gelatinous body. The foot is in the form of a keel; or rather they have none at all, and always swim. The organ of respiration is a straight membranous crest, placed on the back. One species (thalis physalus) (holothuria physalus, Lin.) has long and numerous tentacles; the others (holothuria thalia, etc. Lin.) are without them.

VIII. *Les Lernées.* (Lernæa.) Are parasitic mollusques, which attach themselves to fishes to suck them; they have a round, flexuous body, prolonged in front into a slender neck, at the end of which is the mouth, surrounded by tentacles or horns in small number, often branched at their extremity. This mouth can prolong itself in the manner of a proboscis: one sees above it small tubercles which could be eyes. From the rear of the body hang two very long intestines, striated across and much entangled, which have been taken for ovaries. 1. La lernée des morues (L. branchialis). Two inches long, yellowish; the neck red, brown, hard, terminated by three branched horns. It attaches itself to the gills of cods and of the other gadi of the northern sea. One finds, on the gills of several fishes, parasitic animals **[p. 390]** which naturalists have taken for lernées, but which appear to come nearer the insects, and in particular the family of the monocles.

**Chapter IV. Of the testaceous gastéropode mollusques, which make the greater part of the univalve shellfish.**

The animals which inhabit the univalve shells present fewer differences in their organisation than the naked gastéropodes. Their gills are, either, as in the phyllidies, under the rim of the mantle, or, as in the limaces, in a particular cavity, which itself has for orifice either a hole, or a fleshy tube more or less long. All those which have their gills in the interior appear to need to breathe air in kind, and often come for that to the surface of the water. The tentacles are four in number in the terrestrial species, and two in almost all the aquatic. The eyes are sometimes on the point of these **[p. 391]** tentacles, sometimes on their base, sometimes on the head itself. The mouth is slit lengthwise or across, or pierced round, or prolonged in the form of a proboscis; it is armed, or not, with hard teeth. As to the shells, they vary to infinity by the forms and by the colours; their most important differences hold to the form of the aperture, to the elevation of the turns of the spire above the last, to the tubercles, to the varices, to the furrows found on their surface, etc. Several genera are provided with a testaceous or horny operculum, attached to the feet of the animal, which closes the shell in whole or in part when it has withdrawn into it.

The genera of these testaceous gastéropodes are:

A. Those with shell of several pieces.

I. *Les Oscabrions.* (Chiton.) The animal resembles the phyllidies; but its mantle has on its middle a longitudinal series of testaceous pieces: its two edges are coriaceous, sometimes smooth and sometimes wrinkled, shagreened, hairy, or even spiny, according to the species. **[p. 392]** 1. L'oscabrion ponctué (Chiton punctatus). Is common on our coasts, in places full of rocks. It has eight valves, longitudinally keeled. The body is marked with sunken points.

B. Those with shell of a single non-spiral piece.

II. *Les Patelles.* (Patella.) The animal resembles the phyllidies, that is to say that it breathes by gills placed under the rim of the mantle all around. It has on the head two tentacles, behind which are the eyes, and the edge of its mantle is furnished besides with other smaller tentacles which it extends at will. Its shell is in a very widely opened cone. There are species of which the cone is simple, such as: 1°. La patelle commune (Patella vulgata). Greyish, with fourteen ill-marked furrows. Very common on our coasts. 2°. La patelle applatie (Patella saccharina). With seven large obtuse ribs, with cone almost flat. Others have the point of the cone curved backward, such as: 3°. La patelle cabochon (Pat. hungarica). Round, white, finely striated, with point curled backward. Some have, with this last conformation, a horizontal plate, holding to the posterior edge of the cone, and advancing a little into the interior. Such are: **[p. 393]** 4°. La patelle porcelaine (Patella porcellana). Flat, white, spotted with fawn. 5°. La patelle polie (Patella polita). Vulg. la nacelle. Smooth, marked with a violet network on a white ground. Others have inside, toward the point, a vertical plate twisted into a half-tube, such as 6°. La patelle bonnet de Neptune (Pat. equestris). White, with radiating striae, fine and well marked. One observes finally some which have an oblique plate descending spirally, and forming so marked a passage to the spiral shells that it is difficult to distinguish them from certain turbo. Such is 7°. La patelle bonnet chinois (P. chinensis). With shell in an obtuse cone, round, furrowed in rays, where one sees on the exterior a line which indicates the plate beneath. Bruguières has made a genus apart of certain patelles with conical shell, with oblong base, whose summit is pierced by a hole which probably serves as passage for the anus; he has named them *fissurelles*. Such is: 8°. La patelle grecque (Pat. græca). Which one distinguishes, independently of the hole, by numerous radiating ribs which other circular ribs cross.

C. Shell of a single piece in a spiral, with entire mouth, without notch or canal.

III. *Les Ormiers* or *Oreilles de mer.* (Haliotis.) Shell with very large aperture, similar to **[p. 394]** an oval basin, and with extremely small spire, of which one sees almost all the turns from inside, pierced by several holes on a line parallel to the outer lip, that is to say, to the edge of the aperture which is opposite to the columella or to the axis around which the spire turns or is supposed to turn. The animal has four tentacles, two upper short ones, bearing eyes at the end, and two lower long and pointed. The whole edge of its mantle is furnished with numerous filaments. 1. L'oreille de mer ordinaire (Hal. tuberculata). Oval, marked outside with crossed striae; its interior is of the finest nacre: it is the same with almost all the other species. This one lives on our coasts. 2. L'oreille d'âne (Hal. asinina). Oblong, narrow, smooth, and spotted outside with green on a grey ground. It is from Amboina.

IV. *Les Nérites.* (Nerita.) The spire rises little above the last turn; the mouth is semicircular, with straight columella, and closed exactly by an operculum of the same form, which, when the animal comes out, folds back like a shutter on the flat part of the columella. The animal has on the head two very fine tentacles; the eyes are at their base on the outside. The nérites have been subdivided into *natices*, whose shell is umbilicate, that is to say that the turns of the spire, not touching **[p. 395]** entirely on the inner side, leave a hollow which penetrates the axis of the columella. Such are: 1. La nérite jaune d'œuf (N. vitellus). Smooth, yellow, clouded with white; umbilicus in the form of a heart. From the East Indies. 2. La nérite bouche noire (N. melanostoma). Oblong, grey, with brown columella. And into *nérites* properly so called, which have no umbilicus; their columella is flattened, and most often toothed. Such are: 3. La dent saignante (N. peloronta). Varied in colour, with toothed columella, white, marked with a red spot. 4. La nérite polie (N. polita). Smooth, prettily varied in colour; with toothed columella, white, smooth; interior yellowish. 5. La grive (N. exuvia). Varied with white and black, and furrowed by large ribs which follow the direction of the turns; inner lip tuberculate. 6. La nérite d'eau douce (N. fluviatilis). From our rivers, smooth, small; with entire columella, white and grey, with brown scales.

V. *Les Planorbes.* (Planorbis. Br.) Their shell is discoid, that is to say that all the turns of the spiral are in the same plane: they touch **[p. 396]** without enveloping one another, and one sees them all from both sides. The aperture is round or half-round, wider than high. The animal has two tentacles, and the eyes placed at their anterior base. The planorbes inhabit fresh waters. Linnaeus left them in the genus of the hélices. 1. Le planorbe corné (Planorbis cornea). An inch in diameter at most, greyish, almost smooth; aperture round. From our rivers. 2. Le planorbe corne de bélier (Pl. cornu arietis). Similar to the preceding, but turning in the contrary direction; which is seen because, in both, there is a side where the spire is more concave. 3. Le planorbe tourbillon (Pl. vortex). With turns flattened on one side; which produces an edge which follows their length, and comes to terminate at a salient angle of the mouth. From our rivers.

VI. *Les Hélices.* (Helix.) Vulg. colimaçons. The turns of the spire rise so as to form a shell sometimes globular, sometimes orbicular: the aperture is wider than high, and in a half-moon, because the penultimate turn notches the last. These are terrestrial shellfish, whose animal resembles the limace, has like it its gills in the interior, breathes by a lateral hole, and bears four tentacles, of which the upper are terminated by eyes. **[p. 397]** 1. Le grand colimaçon des vignes (Helix pomatia. L.). Large, greyish yellow, lightly striated across the turns. The animal is edible. The ancients formerly raised them for that; it is very voracious, and does harm to crops. 2. La livrée (Hel. nemoralis). Almost smooth; with edge of the mouth black; with shell yellow, fawn or white, with brown lines more or less broad, more or less numerous, which follow the length of the turns. This hélice is very common in gardens, and harms trees. 3. La lampe antique (Hel. ringens). Its shell is little convex: its mouth is turned upward; that is to say, directed to the same side as the spire. One sees two teeth on each of its edges. From Saint-Domingue.

VII. *Les Bulimes.* (Bulimus.) Had also been mixed with the hélices by Linnaeus. They differ from them in that the aperture is higher than wide: the penultimate turn notches the last as in the hélices. The spire rises diversely. There are bulimes: a) With smooth columella; with entire mouth. 1. Le bulime des étangs (Bul. stagnalis) Helix stagn. Lin. Thin, grey, with long and pointed spire. From our still waters. The animal has two short, compressed tentacles; the eye is at their base on the inside. 2. Le bulime épineux (Bul. amarula) Helix amar. L. Blackish brown; the top of each turn crowned with spines. From the rivers of India. **[p. 398]** b) With entire mouth; with plaited columella, that is to say, presenting some salient lines which wind around it. 3. Le bulime oreille (Bul. auris Midæ) Bulla auris Midæ. L. Shell oval, brown, striated crosswise, and as if granulated; two strong plaits on the columella. c) With columella truncated at the bottom; with oval and pointed shell. 4. Le bulime zèbre (Bul. zebra). Very large, smooth, streaked, across the turns, with white and fawn. This species is terrestrial, and lives in Senegal on the leaves and young shoots of trees. The animal has four tentacles, of which the two upper bear the eyes at their extremity.

VIII. *Les Bulles.* (Bulla.) Have a shell with very swollen turns, of which the last is vaster than those which precede it, and exceeds them above and below. 1. La bulle œuf (Bulla ovum). Large, ovoid, smooth, white, yellow inside; the mouth is narrow, and the edge folds, at each of its extremities, into a small canal. 2. La bulle muscade (Bulla physis). Smooth, ovoid, clouded with white and brown. The aperture is narrower on the side of the spire than on the opposite side. **[p. 399]** 3. La bulle oublie (B. lignaria). Oblong, striated, and streaked with yellow and white, along the direction of the turns; aperture ample, and very wide at the bottom. 4. La bulle à ceinture (B. gibbosa). Oblong; an obtuse projection prolongs itself along the middle of the turns; the mouth is narrow and linear; shell small, smooth, white.

IX. *Les Sabots.* (Turbo.) Have their spire more or less elevated: the aperture and the section of the turns are perfectly round, whether each turn be a cylinder curved in a spiral, or whether it be completed by the preceding turn, against which it glues itself as in the hélices. The shell is closed by an operculum. The animal has most often, besides the two ordinary tentacles, some extensible filaments on the two sides of the mantle. The species with complete turns are either, a) With spire elevated to a point (turriculate), such as: 1. La scalata (Turbo scalaris). Small shell famous for its dearness. Its turns not touching, it represents in some sort a corkscrew. Sharp edges surround each turn from space to space like rings. It is perhaps only a variety of La fausse scalata, which differs from it only because its turns touch as **[p. 400]** in all shells. Both are from the Mediterranean. One also names fausse scalata an American shell more elongated, and with more obtuse varices than the preceding. It is the turbo clathrus. L. Or b) With spire more or less flattened, such as: 2. Le dauphin (Turbo delphinus). With umbilicate shell, with flattened spire, with turns armed with large obtuse and cut-out spines, turned toward the mouth. The species with incomplete turns also have different forms of shell. There are: a) With turriculate spire, such as: 3. Le sabot à vis (Turbo duplicatus). With very long and very pointed shell, where two salient lines follow the direction of all the turns. b) With spire in the form of an obtuse cone, without furrows between the turns, such as: 4. La veuve (Turbo pica). Smooth, umbilicate, spotted with black and white. c) With turns very distinct by furrows. 5. La bouche d'argent (T. argyrostomus). The interior is of a fine nacre. The turns are furrowed along their length, and armed with a double series of spines. d) With flattened spire. 6. L'éperon (Turbo calcar). With shell entirely flat, with turns compressed into a sharp keel, **[p. 401]** armed with spines; so that the whole represents the rowel of a spur.

X. *Les Toupies.* (Trochus.) Their spire forms a cone little acute: the aperture of the shell is almost quadrangular, and cuts obliquely the direction of the last turn. One must remark: a) The species with flat base, whose umbilicus is in the form of a widely opened funnel. Such is 1. Le cadran (Trochus perspectivus). White, with turns bordered by a brown and white line. The spire is very obtuse, and the umbilicus so open that one sees the inner face of all the turns. b) Those with convex base, with an umbilicus: 2. Le bouton de camisole (Tr. pharaonius). Small, with toothed mouth and umbilicus. Close-set series of tubercles, some all red, the others white and black, follow the direction of the turns, and make this shell resemble a tissue of glass beads. c) Those with convex base, without umbilicus: 3. Le moule de bouton (Tr. vestiarius). Very smooth, equally convex on both sides, prettily varied in colours. d) Those with concave base: 4. La fripière (Trochus agglutinans). Gathers fragments of shells, or small whole shells, **[p. 402]** or small pebbles, and incorporates them coarsely into its own.

D. Shell of a single piece in a spiral, with mouth terminated by a canal.

Beginning with the haliotides and the nérites, the solid part which serves as retreat to the animal twists more and more into a spiral; but its aperture had remained, in all the preceding genera, entire or without notch, although it lengthened a little in the bulimes, and still more in the bulles. In the following genera we shall see it prolong itself into a canal more or less long, which serves to lodge a fleshy tube which the animal lengthens at its pleasure, and which establishes a communication from its gills to the outer air.

XI. *Les Murex.* (Murex.) Linné united under this name all the shellfish which have their aperture terminated by a straight canal. As they present many different forms, they have been subdivided. a) Bruguières has given the name of *Cérithes* to those which have the shell turriculate and the canal short. There are of these cérithes: α) With straight canal, such as: 1. Le cérithe noduleux (Murex nodulosus. L.). Wrinkled, with turns armed with a row of large tubercles. **[p. 403]** β) With canal curved backward; such as: 2. Le cérithe demi-strié (Murex vertagus). Whose columella has a plait. Its turns are smooth at their lower part, and furrowed across at the upper. γ) With canal twisted outward, such as: 3. Le cérithe télescope (Trochus telescopium. L.). With perfectly conical spire, with turns striated along their length, with flat base, with canal turning outward in a spiral. b) Bruguières has given the name of *Fuseaux* to those which have the shell turriculate and the canal long. Such are: 4. Le fuseau ordinaire (Murex fusus). With rounded, smooth turns; with very long canal, as also the spire; with toothed outer lip. 5. L'entonnoir (Murex colus). Turns keeled, with a series of tubercles following their direction. The spire and the canal are very long. c) The third family of the murex is with ovoid or flattened spire, and with canal more or less long. It subdivides according as the shell is, α) Furnished with varices, that is to say, with ridges which cross the direction of the turns (1). It is to these that Bruguières has reserved the name of *Murex*. Such are: **[p. 404]** 6. La tête de bécasse épineuse (Murex tribulus). Shell oval, canal extremely long; three series of varices, armed with many long, slender and pointed spines. 7. La cuiller (Murex haustellum). Shell oval; canal very long; varices close-set, wrinkled, without spines. 8. La massue (Murex brandaris). Spire a little flat, canal long; varices each provided with three large spines. β) Or with shell without varices. 9. La figue (Murex ficus). Shell rounded, narrowing insensibly into a canal, and representing a fig. 10. Le nègre (Murex morio). Shell with spire little pointed, with turns flattened toward the top. The longitudinal angle which this flattening forms is scalloped. Colour brown. A white cord on the junction of the turns.

> (1) *[p. 403]* These ridges are formed by the animal around its mouth, when it has taken a certain growth; when afterward it enlarges its shell, the varix remains at the place where the mouth was before. The number of varices thus increases successively.

XII. *Les Strombes.* (Strombus.) Have an oblong aperture, terminated by a canal more or less long, straight or curved, either to the right or to the left, independently of which their lip has toward the bottom a deep notch. This lip, simple in the young, widens with age, and divides in some species into very varied digitations. **[p. 405]** There are strombes: a) With turriculate shell. 1. Le strombe fuseau (Str. fusus). With very pointed spire, with long canal, and with lip toothed above its notch. b) With short spire, with lip not digitate. 2. Le strombe pesant (Str. pugilis). Short spire; a row of spines on each turn; lip widened, but not rising above the last turn; shell fawn and smooth. 3. Le strombe large (Str. latissimus). Shell smooth, without spines; with very thick lip, rising into a broad rounded appendage, higher than the spire. 4. Le strombe crête de coq (Str. gallus). A row of short and thick spines; lip broad, rising into a long channelled strap, much higher than the spire. c) With short spire, with digitate lip. 5. Le millepied (Strombus millepeda). The lip has from ten to twelve short digitations; the shell has three rows of tubercles on the last turn, and one on each of the others. 6. Le scorpion (Strombus scorpius). With seven long digitations, counting the ordinary canal, which curves toward the outer lip. The mouth is furrowed across.

**[p. 406]** XIII. *Les Casques.* (Cassidea.) Have the spire very little prominent, the last turn very high; the aperture elongated and toothed, terminating by a short canal, curved toward the back; its edges fold back into broad lips. (Linnaeus comprises the casques among the buccins.) There are species: a) With spire a little pointed. 1. Le casque dentelé (Buccinum vibex. Lin.). With smooth shell; with inner lip hardly marked; the outer has outside toward the bottom some small teeth. b) With rounded spire. 2. Le casque rayé (Bucc. testiculus. Lin.). With broad furrows, hardly sunken, following the direction of the turns; fine and close-set striae cross them. The mouth is furrowed on both lips. c) With flat spire. 3. Le casque roux (Bucc. rufum. Lin.). Shell tuberculate; lips broad, swollen, smooth, furrowed toward the mouth, and of a fine very lively russet colour. 4. Le casque tricoté (Bucc. cornutum. Lin.). Shell engraved with a multitude of sunken points which are there like meshes; two smooth longitudinal bands; a series of tubercles at the top of each turn; mouth white. With age, the inner lip becomes a great discoid flattening, and the tubercles change into strong spines. **[p. 407]** 5. Le casque triangulaire (Bucc. tuberosum. Lin.). The inner lip forms, at certain epochs, a great triangular flattening, whose mark remains when the shell enlarges beyond. These successive marks give the spire a triangular form. There is a large brown spot on the lip. The turns are tuberculate toward the top.

E. Shell of a single piece in a spiral, with aperture notched at the bottom.

The notch of the genera of this section serves, like the canal of the genera of the preceding section, to transmit to the outside the tube by which the animal breathes.

XIV. *Les Buccins.* (Buccinum. L.) Have a spire more or less prominent, a shell oval or elongated, and an ample aperture, terminated at the bottom by a broad notch. There are species: a) With bellied shell; with last turn very convex, striated along its length. (They are vulgarly named *Tonnes*.) 1. La perdrix (Buccinum perdix). With little-marked furrows, varied in waves of grey and white. 2. La licorne (Bucc. monodon). With scaly longitudinal ribs; a long salient spine, implanted on the outer edge of the mouth near the notch. **[p. 408]** b) With bellied shell, furnished with ribs salient across the turns. (Vulg. *Harpes*.) 3. La harpe ordinaire (Bucc. harpa). With red and white ribs; with white intervals, waved with brown, transversely striated. c) With bellied shell, whose notch has above it a small hollow canal in the mouth. Linné had placed them among the murex. These are the *pourpres* of Bruguières. 4. Le buccin persique (Bucc. persicum). With thick, brown shell, with black and white striae and cordlets following the direction of the turns. 5. La mûre (Bucc. morus). With round, thick shell, with large short spines; the mouth toothed, of lilac colour. d) With bellied shell; with pointed spire. 6. La double spire (Bucc. spiratum). Smooth, with spiral umbilicus; a canal on the line of junction of the turns; colour white, spotted with fawn. e) With turriculate shell. (These are the *vis* of Bruguières.) 7. La double vis lisse (Bucc. dimidiatum). Shell smooth, with very long and very pointed spire; a single furrow following the direction of the turns. 8. La double vis rayée (Bucc. duplicatum). Similar to the preceding. A longitudinal furrow; several across.

**[p. 409]** XV. *Les Volutes.* (Voluta.) Their aperture, more or less elongated, terminates at the bottom by a broad notch. The columella is as if plaited, that is to say that some strong flutings wind around it in a spiral. The animal of the volutes is a gastéropode with two horns or tentacles; its mouth and its canal of respiration both prolong themselves in the manner of a proboscis. There are volutes: a) With short and rounded spire. 1. La tasse de Neptune (Voluta cymbium). Very large, smooth, fawn. The junction of the turns forms a deep canal, or a kind of ditch, which winds round the whole spire. The columella has two large plaits; the aperture is very ample. b) With spire a little raised to a point. 2. Le plain-chant (Voluta musica). The top of the turns has a series of tubercles. Their face is marked with yellow longitudinal lines and square black spots, which give it some resemblance to plainchant. There are eight plaits on the columella. c) With spire entirely turriculate. 3. La mitre (Voluta mitra). Very pointed, smooth, white, with large red spots.

XVI. *Les Olives.* (Oliva.) Their shell is almost cylindrical, with flat **[p. 410]** channelled spire; their mouth longitudinal and narrow; their columella furrowed with a multitude of plaits. The olives are remarkable for their pretty colours. (Linné ranged them among the volutes.) 1. L'olive de Panama (Ol. porphyria). Becomes very large, and of a very pale fawn, with a great number of distinct lines, in the form of zigzags, of brown colour.

XVII. *Les Porcelaines.* (Cypræa.) The spire is almost nil, because the last turn, rising to the level of the preceding, envelops them almost entirely. The total form of the shell is ovoid; and the aperture, narrow, long and toothed, appears to divide it into two equal parts, because the outer lip is swollen so as to equal the rest in width. The animal of the porcelaines is a gastéropode with two horns, whose canal of respiration opens above the head. Its mantle is so ample that it can, by turning itself up around the shell, envelop it entirely; and, at certain epochs, it clothes it outside with a calcareous layer, otherwise coloured than that which it had first produced in the ordinary manner; which makes the shell much thicker. It is also then that the swollen and toothed lips form; for the **[p. 411]** shells of porcelaines have, in their thin state, simple lips and a rather wide aperture. There are porcelaines: a) With oblong shell, of equal diameter. 1. L'argus (Cypræa argus). Of grey-brown colour, with spots in the form of rings, of a blackish brown. The lips have each two large black spots. This shell, in its first state, is fawn, with two whitish ribbons which surround it. b) With oval shell, wider toward the top, with flattened lips. 2. La carte de géographie (Cypræa mappa). The back is all covered with small brown traces: there is a broad whitish, irregular line, giving branches on both sides, and resembling a river such as they are represented on maps. One sees besides some round whitish spots scattered in the brown. 3. Le tigre (Cypræa tigris). Shell white, very smooth, agreeably strewn with round, violet and brown spots. c) With oval shell, with lips swollen and forming a rim on both sides. 4. Le cauris (Cypræa moneta). Small white shell, whose lips are swollen unequally, and form on each side one or two tuberosities. This species serves as small money in some countries of India.

**[p. 412]** XVIII. *Les Cornets.* (Conus. L.) The spire is flattened; the aperture entirely in the form of a line, narrow and without teeth. The turns narrow at the bottom; so that in all the shell has the figure of a cone, of which the spire would make the base. The animal has two tentacles which bear the eyes near their point; its trachea is in the form of a tube, and its mantle very narrow: its foot bears under its posterior extremity a very small rounded operculum. The species of cornets are sought after on account of the brilliance and variety of their colours. They are very numerous. They are divided: a) Into cornets with conoid and crowned shell; that is to say whose turns are tuberculate toward the spire. 1. Le damier (Conus marmoratus). Large, smooth, with brown ground, strewn with large triangular white spots, close-set. From the Moluccas. 2. La piqûre de mouches (Conus stercus-muscarum). Middling, smooth, white, with small brown points, close-set. 3. Le cedo-nulli (Conus ammiralis-summus). Large fawn spots on a white ground; dotted cordlets following the direction of the turns. It is to this species that belong those so precious varieties known under the name of amiraux. **[p. 413]** 4. La couronne impériale (Conus imperialis). Spire entirely flat; interrupted ribbons, yellow, spotted with brown. b) Cornets with conoid and not crowned shell. 5. Le minime (Conus figulinus). Fawn, streaked with brown, in close-set lines, along the direction of the turns. 6. L'amiral ordinaire (Conus ammiralis). Was confused by Linné with the cedo-nulli, but has no tubercles. Is marked alternately with fawn ribbons, strewn with large triangular white spots, and other ribbons with white ground finely reticulated with yellow. 7. Le mille-points (Conus literatus). With spire perfectly flattened; with turns channelled, white, with a multitude of square brown points. c) Cylindraceous cornets: with shell almost equal and bellied. 8. La brunette (Conus aulicus). With brown ground; with triangular white spots, unequally dispersed; with conical and convex spire. 9. Le drap d'or (Conus textile). Of the same form as the preceding; with similar spots, but with ground of a fine yellow streaked with brown.

**[p. 414]** **Chapter V. Of the mollusques without distinct head, or Acéphales.**

These animals, most of which are clothed in shells of two valves, have the whole body enveloped in a membranous mantle, open in front, or at both ends, or at one only. It is to this mantle, and above all to the part by which the water is introduced, that hold small tentacles, the only sensitive organs which these animals show on the exterior. Their gills are large vascular leaflets, placed on both sides, immediately under the mantle. The heart is toward the back. They have a liver in whose interior is the stomach, and the greater part of the alimentary canal, which receives the bile by pores and not by a single canal. The mouth, which leads straight to the stomach, is placed between the gills, at the end opposite **[p. 415]** to that by which the water arrives to them. This water alone furnishes nourishment to the mouth; for there is no mobile head. Around the mouth are four triangular leaflets, which are no doubt kinds of tentacles. The brain is placed between the gills and the intestinal canal, at least in the solens and the pholades; for it has not yet been seen in the other genera. Several genera have a foot placed before the body between the gills, and more or less well organised. They appear to be all hermaphrodites, and to produce without coupling.

A. Naked acéphales or without shell.

I. *Les Ascidies.* (Ascidia.) Have a mantle in the form of a sac, of substance half cartilaginous, half gelatinous; fixing themselves to some body, often several individuals together. There are two openings toward the free extremity. One leads the water between the gills to the mouth, which is in the bottom of the sac; the anus ends at the other: there is no foot. The sac is vaster than needed to contain the body of the animal, and the rest of the interval is filled with water. The species of ascidies are numerous, and vary **[p. 416]** in size and colours. They are found in all seas.

II. *Les Biphores.* (Salpa. Lin.) Their mantle is open at both ends: one of the openings, which is very large, serves for the introduction of the water between the gills and for its exit; the other is the anus. For the rest, their organisation resembles that of all the acéphales. They have no feet. These animals are found in all seas. Several of their species are remarkable for the order they observe in their manner of swimming: there are some whose individuals are always in file one after another on two lines, one over the other, and touching, but in such a way that each individual of the upper line bears on two of the lower; others are placed side by side, in lines of which the upper advances more than the lower, etc. Each arrangement is observed with constancy by all the troops of the same species.

B. Testaceous acéphales, without foot, and with inequivalve shell.

They all have the mantle open in front, and its edge furnished with tentacles; gills four in number; four triangular leaflets **[p. 417]** around the mouth, and a single muscle in the middle, which goes straight from one valve to the other, and closes the shell by contracting. It opens only by the elasticity of a ligament placed behind the hinge. It is claimed that it is the less swollen valve (named cover) which stays beneath, and which even often attaches itself to the ground. The genera of this section are:

III. *Les Huîtres.* (Ostrea.) Their shell is irregularly oval, thick, composed of ill-united plates. Their hinge presents no tooth, but only a hollow on each valve, for the attachment of the ligament. 1. L'huître commune (Ostrea edulis). Whose form is known to everyone, ordinarily attaches itself to rocks, stones, or other immobile bodies of the sea bottom. One goes to tear it off, and keeps it in parks under water for need. Those of certain marshes are particularly esteemed, where the salt water, renewing itself only at the great tides, takes a green colour which communicates itself to the oysters, and makes them be called green oysters. This shellfish serves rather to sharpen the appetite than to satisfy it. It is claimed that milk facilitates its digestion. The oysters, though lacking a foot, are not totally without progressive movement. It is said at least that those which are not attached know how to open and shut their shell suddenly, so as to make the water they expel give them an impulse toward the side where the hinge is. **[p. 418]** It is also said that those which have lived in places often uncovered at the ebb keep better in transport, because the want of water they have often suffered has taught them to keep their shell closed to preserve the water it contains.

IV. *Les Spondyles.* (Spondylus.) Have a thick, irregular and spiny shell. The most convex valve is very massive. The cover is flat, and has two strong curved teeth which enter two pits of the opposite valve; in the middle is, on each side, a pit for the ligament. 1. Le pied d'âne (Spondylus gæderopus). Its shell, bristling with large spines, is sometimes white, sometimes of different reds. The heel of its most convex valve has a flattening which seems to have been made with a saw. This species is found in the Mediterranean and the warm regions of the Ocean.

V. *Les Placunes.* (Placuna.) Have an irregular shell composed of two very thin and very flat valves. There is no hinge; but two small plates form a salient chevron inside each valve, and give attachment to the ligament. 1. La selle polonaise (Pl. sella) Anomia sella. L. With rounded valves, one convex, the other concave, intercepting **[p. 419]** only an extremely thin space, so that the animal must be very flat. Their colour is violet. 2. La vitre chinoise (Pl. placenta). With round valves; one lightly convex, the other flat. Colour silver white, semi-transparent.

VI. *Les Anomies.* (Anomia.) Have two irregular, thin valves, one convex, the other flat or concave: the latter has an opening or rather a notch toward its base, through which passes a muscle which inserts into a third much smaller valve, which adheres to rocks or to other bodies. 1. La pelure d'oignon (Anomia ephippium). With leafy, shining valves, with a reddish tint like that of onion peelings. 2. L'anomie sillonnée (An. patella). The leaves are less distinct; the perforated valve has five broad shallow furrows, going from the hinge to the edge.

VII. *Les Pélerines.* (Pecten.) Have a hinge similar to that of the oysters, that is to say without teeth, with a pit on each valve for the ligament. The valves are thin, and without detached plates, rather regularly oval, and augmented, on each side of the hinge, by a triangular auricle. **[p. 420]** The species of pélerines are numerous. There are some which have one valve convex, and the other flat or even concave, such as 1. La grande pélerine (Pecten maximus) Ostrea maxima. Lin. Large, reddish, with fourteen broad and flat ribs, striated lengthwise; with intervals striated across. This shell is from our seas. It is served on our tables. 2. Le bénitier (Pecten zigzag) Ostrea zigzag. Lin. One of the valves is very swollen, and the other entirely flat. Both are very thin, and have forty-eight striae in rays, approached by pairs. Others have both valves convex, such as 3. La sole (Pecten pleuronectes) Ostrea pleuronectes. Lin. With extremely thin valves, very flat, perfectly smooth outside, marked inside with twenty-four fine, raised ribs, approached by pairs. 4. Le corail (Pecten nodosus) Ostrea nodosa. Lin. Of a fine red colour, with very thick valves, with nine large very salient ribs, striated along their length, and furnished with large rounded knots. There are, among these pélerines with two convex valves, species which have them almost equal, and which must perhaps form a genus apart, which will be ranged in the following section if one observes some day that they have a foot.

**[p. 421]** C. Testaceous acéphales, provided with a foot, with equal valves, with mantle open in front.

Their foot is a fleshy mass, which serves some only to crawl, but which, in others, is made in the form of a tongue, and can, by folding itself into a gutter, draw threads with a glutinous matter which a particular gland furnishes. The animal ordinarily uses these threads to suspend itself from rocks. For the rest, the body of these acéphales does not differ from those of the preceding section; only the posterior edge of the mantle sometimes lengthens into a double pipe.

VIII. *Les Limes.* (Lima.) Are confused with the pélerines by many authors, because they likewise have a triangular auricle on both sides of the hinge: but their valves are always equal and obliquely oval; their ligament is placed on the back of the valves, and not in pits. Finally the animal has a foot, and sends out a bundle of threads by a notch on the posterior side. 1. La lime ordinaire (Lima alba) Ostrea lima. Lin. White; with twenty raised ribs, furnished with half-tubular scales.

IX. *Les Pernes.* (Perna.) Were formerly confused with the oysters **[p. 422]** because their shell is also formed of leaves which separate easily. Their valves are equal, of irregular outline; their hinge is composed of several transverse ribs, which do not cross with those of the opposite valve, but are placed opposite; their intervals serving to lodge the ligaments. The animal attaches itself to rocks by a bundle of threads or byssus, which comes out as in the limes. 1. La selle (Perna ephippium). With round shells, lightly convex, with a notch on one of the sides quite near the hinge. 2. La cuisse (Perna isognomum) Ostrea isognomum. Lin. The shell lengthens irregularly in the form of a narrow tongue. The hinge is wider than the rest.

X. *Les Arondes.* (Avicula.) The hinge has no tooth; its side is always straight: the rest of the shell is sometimes rounded, sometimes elongated; but there is always near the hinge a notch for the passage of the byssus. Linnaeus had mixed the arondes partly with the mussels, partly with the oysters. 1. L'aronde aux perles (Avicula margaritifera) Mytilus margaritiferus. Lin. Its shell is rounded, lightly plaited in rays, striated **[p. 423]** parallel to the edge, and of an obscure green on the exterior; the interior furnishes mother-of-pearl, which its brilliance, and the changing colours mixed there with a silvery lustre, cause to be employed in the arts; the pearls themselves are produced by an extravasation of the liquor destined for the periodic augmentations of the shell. They form in all testaceous animals, and have in each species the colour of its nacre. Those of the aronde aux perles, which are so famous under the name of oriental pearls, are preferred only because its nacre is the finest of all. It is known that the finest pearls are fished near Cape Comorin. 2. L'hirondelle (Avicula hirundo) Mytilus hirundo. Lin. The shell is rounded; the side of the hinge is wider, and projects by two triangular auricles. 3. Le marteau (Avicula malleus) Ostrea malleus. Lin. The shell is irregular, elongated, and very narrow; the side of the hinge forms two long auricles, from which results in all the figure of a hammer. This shell is rare and dear.

XI. *Les Moules.* (Mytilus.) Their shell is closed everywhere, and longitudinal, that is to say longer, taking from the projection which each valve makes near the hinge (a projection named nates) to the edge, than in the opposite direction. The hinge has only one or **[p. 424]** two small teeth, or even lacks them altogether. The ligament is on one of the sides outside. The animal has a foot in the form of a slender tongue, susceptible of very varied contractions and extensions. It spins. 1. La moule commune (Mytilus edulis). Its shells are of a blackish violet, and almost smooth. It is eaten, but it is an indigestible and unwholesome meat. It is said that brandy relieves when one is incommoded by mussels. 2. La feuille-de-tulipe (Mytilus modiolus). Has its shells streaked or flamed with violet or purple on a white or yellowish ground.

XII. *Les Jambonneaux.* (Pinna.) Have two valves in the form of a fan, or of sectors of a circle, soldered together by the angle, and consequently unable to move or to separate. There remains between them an interval for the passage of the foot and the byssus. The latter is very fine in the jambonneaux. It is employed for knitting, and is even mixed into cloths and other stuffs, to which it gives a particular lustre. The species are little distinct.

XIII. *Les Anodontites.* (Anodontites.) Were formerly confused in the genus of the mussels; but their shell is transverse, that is to **[p. 425]** say less wide from the nates to the edge than in the other direction, and there is no tooth on the hinge. The animal does not spin. All the anodontites are fluviatile. 1. L'anodontite des oies (An. anatinus) Mytilus anatinus. Lin. It is the pond mussel; it is very common there, as in all streams with muddy bottom. Its valves are very thin, whitish inside, greenish outside.

XIV. *Les Unio.* (Unio.) Are fluviatile shells which resemble the anodontites on the exterior, and which enclose an animal quite like theirs. But the hinge consists, on one side, in a tooth which enters a similar pit of the opposite valve, and, on the other, in a long plate which places itself between two similar plates. 1. La moule des peintres (Unio pictorum) Mya pictorum. Lin. Is found in all streams. Its shell is thin and oblong. 2. L'unio épaisse (Unio littoralis). Is equally very common. Its shell is thick, and almost square. 3. La moule du Rhin (Unio margaritifera) Mya margaritifera. Lin. Is similar to the preceding, but a little more oval, and becoming much larger. Its nacre is rather fine, and **[p. 426]** its pearls can be employed. It is claimed that by feeding it in a certain way, one can make it produce them more abundantly.

XV. *Les Tellines.* (Tellina.) The shell is flat, sometimes oblong, sometimes round. There is toward one of the extremities an angle which seems to have arisen from one's having folded both valves at once, along a line going from the hinge to the edge. The hinge has two teeth in the middle, and two distant plates on the sides, entering no pit. The animal has a foot, but does not spin. Its mantle forms behind two tubes, one for the anus, the other for the introduction of the water. One may remark among the oblong tellines, 1. Le soleil levant (Tellina virgata). Rose colour, with broad white ribbons, which extend in rays from the hinge toward the edges. 2. La langue de chat (Tellina lingua felis). White, with rose-coloured rays; all strewn with rough tubercles, placed in quincunx. And among those which are almost round, 3. La lime rude (Tellina scobinata). White, very flat, bristling with small half-tubular scales, placed in quincunx.

XVI. *Les Bucardes.* (Cardium.) The nates or protuberances of the valves are so **[p. 427]** strong that they give the shell the form of a heart. The hinge has two teeth in its middle, and, at some distance, on each side, a plate entering a pit of the opposite valve. The animal spins in some species. Its mantle forms behind two short tubes, one for the anus, the other for the entry of the water. Their orifices are furnished with tentacles. 1. Le cœur de Vénus (Card. cardissa). The valves are so compressed from the sides that their middle forms an acute keel, sometimes toothed. The shell is thin and white. 2. La coque (Cardium edule). The shell is swollen, thick, and has on each valve twenty-five salient ribs, disposed in rays, with little-marked transverse striae. This species is very common on our coasts, and is eaten. 3. Le cœur à côtes (Card. costatum). With very swollen shell; with raised ribs, compressed and sharp.

XVII. *Les Mactres.* (Mactra.) Their shell is oval, wider than long, flat. The hinge has lateral plates as in the bucardes, and in the middle a pit for the ligament. The animal does not spin. Its mantle has two tubes. 1. La mactre papyracée (M. stultorum). With shell almost thin as paper.

**[p. 428]** XVIII. *Les Vénus.* (Venus.) The valves are more or less convex, ordinarily wider than long. On their junction there is, in front and behind, an impression which varies in form and extent. The name of vulva or corselet has been given to that which is on the side of the ligament, and that of anus or heart to that of the opposite side. The hinge has in the middle two teeth as in the bucardes; but there is only a single lateral plate on the side of the ligament. The animal is similar to that of the mactres and the bucardes. It does not spin. 1. La conque de Vénus (Venus dione). Has the vulva very large, flattened, bordered by two rows of salient spines; the anus small, in the form of a heart, and sharp ribs parallel to the edge. 2. La vieille ridée (Venus paphia). Vulva oblong, concave, smooth; anus in the form of a heart; very large rounded ribs, parallel to the edge, becoming sharp on the side of the vulva. 3. La lisse (Venus chione). Large, thick, smooth, russet outside; vulva narrow; anus broad, in the form of a heart. One has distinguished from the Vénus the *Donaces*, which have the side of the anus so flattened that it seems truncated; the *Lucines*, which have the anus compressed and as if sharp; and the *Capses*, which have, on the side of the anus, neither impression nor compression.

**[p. 429]** XIX. *Les Cames.* (Chama.) Have large shells, often irregular, whose hinge has only a single thick and oblique tooth, entering a similar pit of the other valve, and on the anterior side a plate which enters a furrow. Bruguières has reserved the name of *Cames* for the irregular and fixed species, which must be placed near the oysters and the spondyles; such as 1. Le gâteau feuilleté (Chama lazarus). With shells little convex, formed of ill-united plates, plaited, projecting over one another, of yellow or reddish colour. He has given the name of *Tridacnes* to those which have a regular shell and little prominent nates, such as 2. La tuilée (Chama gigas). With shell as if folded into deep and rounded furrows, and into ribs raised with scales similar to tiles. One of the sides of the shell has a toothed opening. It is the largest of all known shellfish. He calls *Cardites* the species with regular shell, with salient nates twisted as in a spiral. Such is 3. Le cœur de bœuf (Chama cor). Large russet shell, extremely swollen, whose nates curve to one side.

XX. *Les Arches.* (Arca.) Have shells sometimes round, sometimes oblong, and more or less convex, or even humped. **[p. 430]** Their hinge consists in numerous teeth, which insert between similar teeth of the opposite valve. One may remark among the arches with round shell, 1. L'arche ondée (Arca undata). With valves little convex, thick, white, spotted with fawn, with edge lightly crenellated. Among those with transversely oblong shell, 2. L'arche de Noé (Arca Noæ). Its hinge is a long straight line, divided into small teeth like a saw. The back of the shell is flattened, and its nates salient and curved toward the hinge. The faces of the valves are furrowed in rays.

D. Testaceous acéphales, provided with a foot; with equal valves; with shell open at both ends; with mantle closed in front.

XXI. *Les Solens.* (Solen.) Vulg. manches de couteau. Their shell is cylindrical, or at least of an equal width, open at both ends. The hinge has only one or two teeth. The animal extends by one extremity a cylindrical foot, which serves to raise it or to sink it into the sand, where it ordinarily remains buried; by the other extremity comes out a short tube which contains the two pipes of the anus and of respiration. There are solens which have their hinge in the middle of the length of the valves. These are wider: **[p. 431]** 1. Le solen rayé (Solen strigilatus). With flat valves, obliquely striated, flesh colour, with two white rays. Others have their hinge entirely at one extremity. Their valves are narrower and longer. 2. Le fourreau (Solen vagina). With cylindrical shell, quite straight. 3. L'épée (Solen ensis). With cylindrical shell, arched along its length. Both are found on our coasts.

XXII. *Les Myes.* (Mya.) Have a shell open at both ends like the solens: by one extremity passes the foot; by the other the mantle lengthens into a tube, whose interior contains the two ordinary pipes. The hinge consists in a large salient plate of one of the valves, which enters no hollow of the opposite valve.

XXIII. *Les Pholades* or *Dails.* (Pholas.) Have a shell composed of two large equal valves, open at both ends, and of some other smaller ones attached under the ligament on the outside. The number of these last varies according to the species; but there is always at least one unpaired. The animal sends out by one extremity a long tube formed by the mantle, and containing the two **[p. 432]** ordinary pipes; by the other extremity, which is always wider, comes out a cylindrical or compressed foot, short, with flat base. The pholades live in the interior of calcareous rocks, which they know how to dissolve and gnaw. They hollow out there from their infancy holes whose opening would no longer let them pass when they have grown.

XXIV. *Les Tarets.* (Teredo.) The mantle of the animal is in the form of an elongated tube, and enveloped in a similar tube, calcareous, very thin, lodged in the thickness of woods sunk under water. There are at the bottom two valves similar in small to those of the pholades, between which comes out a cylindrical foot. The taret uses them to pierce the wood. Its other extremity comes out by a hole of the surface of the wood, and emits two short pipes, analogous to those of the preceding genera, furnished on each side with a small testaceous piece in the form of a paddle. 1. Le taret ordinaire (Teredo navalis). Is native of the torrid zone, from which it has been brought to Europe in the wood of ships. It destroys them, as well as the piles and all the woods sunk under sea water, and it has often alarmed Holland by threatening to destroy its dikes. *La Fistulane.* Brug. (Teredo clava. Lin.) Has its tube prolonged over the two valves, and entirely closed over them. One must presume that the animal opens the bottom of it when **[p. 433]** its growth forces it to sink further into the wood.

E. Testaceous acéphales, without foot, provided with two fleshy tentacles, ciliated, rolled in a spiral.

From the oyster to the taret, one observes no well-marked difference between the acéphales but a foot, and some prolongations or some connexions in the edges of the mantle, more or less. The two following sections have more important characters. The first has a mantle with two lobes, to each of which adhere the gills, which are nothing other than cords of small membranous leaflets; between these lobes are the viscera and the muscles, and, on the side of the edge, two very long arms or tentacles, bordered with soft cilia, and which the animal rolls in a spiral when it withdraws them.

XXV. *Les Térébratules.* (Terebratula.) These shells, which Linnaeus had united in a single genus with the anomies and the placunes, have two regular valves, of which one has its tuberosity or its nates much more advanced than the other, and forming thereby a kind of beak, pierced by a hole at its extremity. Through this hole passes a ligament or a tube, which attaches itself to some fixed body. The hinge is formed of two teeth which enter pits. **[p. 434]** To the unperforated valve hold two bony branches, slender, forked, which serve as support to the animal. Living térébratules are known in the sea; but there is a much greater number of species which are found only fossil or petrified. Among the living species there are a) With smooth shell. 1. La poulette (Terebratula vitrea) Anomia terebratula. Lin. Oval, smooth, convex; with thin valves, white and semi-transparent. From the Mediterranean. b) With shell striated in rays. 2. La térébratule bossue (Terebratula dorsata). With shell as wide as long, furrowed in rays; a longitudinal depression on the flat valve; a projection on that which has its nates perforated. From the strait of Magellan. 3. La térébratule tronquée (Terebratula truncata). Wider than long; a depression on the flat valve; a projection on the other; the nates of the latter truncated at the level of the hinge. From the Mediterranean sea. One also ranges among the térébratules a small shell of horny and transparent substance, whose two valves are soldered by the back, which has three denticulations, of which the middle one is perforated. One of these valves is oblong; the other, much shorter and more convex, is almost hemispherical, so that there remains in front a space always open. It is the anomia tridentata. Lin. It is found in the Mediterranean toward the Levant. **[p. 435]** The fossil térébratules present forms analogous to those of the living térébratules; but one has made, under the name of *Cranie*, a proper genus of a fossil species with two flat valves, soldered together, of which the lower, lightly concave, is pierced by three holes, which probably were closed by three opercula adhering to the rocks. This genus is no doubt nearer the anomies than any other.

XXVI. *Les Lingules.* (Lingula.) Have a shell composed of two longitudinally oblong valves, little convex, with pointed nates, equal to each other, without teeth on the hinge. The ligament prolongs itself into a fleshy tube, which fixes itself to rocks or to some other body. The animal has no bony skeleton like that of the térébratules. Its mantle is bordered with fine hairs. 1. Le bec de cane (Lingula unguis). The shell is thin and greenish. Linné long knew only one valve of it, of which he made a patelle (Pat. unguis). Afterward, not knowing their common pedicle, Gmelin made this shellfish pass into the genus of the anomies (An. unguis). It is Bruguières who established the genus lingule, but he did not know the animal which inhabits it.

XXVII. *Les Orbicules.* (Orbicula.) The animal appears to resemble much that of the lingules; but it has only a single shell, similar to that of the patelles. Its other face adheres so to stones or other bodies that one cannot separate it without tearing it. **[p. 436]** Only a single species of this genus is known, discovered by Müller in Denmark, and ranged hitherto among the patelles (Patella anomala).

F. Testaceous acéphales, provided with a multitude of articulated and ciliated tentacles, arranged by pairs.

These animals depart still more than those of the preceding section from the common model of the acéphales; and their tentacles or feet, of horny substance, and divided into articulations, seem to make of them a shade between the class of the mollusques and that of the insectes and vers.

XXVIII. *Les Anatifes.* (Anatifa.) Vulg. pousse-pieds. Are recognised by a fleshy tube more or less long, adhering by one extremity, and bearing at the other an assemblage of testaceous pieces, five, seven, or more in number, but opening like a bivalve shell, and lined with the mantle of the animal. Its body adheres to the tube by its base, where is the mouth, directed forward; the other extremity terminates in a long and pointed proboscis. Between it and the mouth are on each side five tubercles, which bear each five tentacles, long, horny, compressed, ciliated, divided into a multitude of articulations, and which withdraw by rolling in a spiral. The ovaries are between the base of the body and the mantle. **[p. 437]** One sees in the interior an intestinal canal, and two serpentine tubes which take the place of testicles. The proboscis serves as anus. No gills are known, unless the tentacles take their place. It is the anatifes which gave rise to the fable of certain ducks claimed to be born on trees or from the rotten wood of ships. Hence their name of conchæ anatiferæ. 1. L'anatife lisse (Anatifa lævis) Lepas anatifera. Lin. With five compressed, smooth valves, with wrinkled tube. Is met in all seas; attaches itself to ships, etc. 2. L'anatife pousse-pied (An. pollicipes). Is ordinarily found in groups of several individuals. Besides the five ordinary valves, there are a great number of smaller ones at the base, and even on the tube, where they represent small scales.

XXIX. *Les Balanites.* (Balanus.) Their animal is similar to that of the anatifes: but it has no tube, and is clothed in a shell in the form of a truncated cone, whose base is fixed on some body, and whose other extremity has an opening furnished with four testaceous leaves which open and close it at the will of the animal. **[p. 438]** 1. Le gland de mer (Balanus vulgaris) Lepas balanus. (Lin.) Small, whitish, so common that it covers the rocks, the pebbles, and often the other shellfish, and even the crayfishes and crabs. 2. Le balanite tulipe (Bal. tintinnabulum) (Lepas tintinnabulum. Lin.). Is much larger and has a shell more elevated than that of the preceding, streaked with white and purple. N. B. Linnaeus had united the anatifes and the balanites under the common name of Lepas.

# Book VII, Chapter XI. Des Vers

**[p. 624]** Perfect insects are distinguished, among all the white-blooded animals, by the perfection of their organs of movement, which consists above all in this, that their limbs have distinct articulations, and that the parts of them are solid. A part of their larvae also enjoys this advantage: those of the orthoptera, of the hemiptera, have legs as perfect as their perfect insects; the larvae of the lepidoptera, of the coleoptera, have these limbs generally very short, and little capable of producing a prompt movement. They disappear entirely in the larvae of the diptera and of many hymenoptera, in which they are replaced by hairs, by bristles, or only by the rings and the transverse wrinkles of the trunk. The animals of which we are going to speak are **[p. 625]** in the case of these larvae; but they never change form, and propagate themselves in this state.

The largest have the body divided into quite distinct rings: one finds in their interior a knotted medullary cord. Those which live in water often breathe by membranous or plumed gills, like many aquatic larvae. Others have, on the sides of the body, stigmata entirely similar to the orifices of the tracheae of insects. Several have, for organs of movement, stiff bristles, or even spines. The others crawl by wrinkling or by contracting successively the various parts of the body. One observes in some even antennae. In a word, it is impossible to assign a general character taken from the external form or the internal organisation by which one could, in all cases, distinguish the vers from the larvae of insects.

The greater part of the vers inhabit the interior of other animals, as some larvae of insects do; others live in the earth or in the waters. There are among the latter **[p. 626]** some which build themselves solid houses, either by agglutinating foreign bodies, or by transuding a calcareous juice, as the testaceous mollusques do; but one will always distinguish the shells of the vers from those of the mollusques, because those of the vers are in the form of tubes more or less straight, more or less tortuous, but never in an entirely regular spiral nor in a widely opened cone, and above all because the animal is not attached to its shell, whereas the mollusques always are.

A. *Vers* provided with spines or bristles to aid themselves in their movements.

I. *Les Aphrodites.* (Aphrodita.) Their body is oblong, flattened horizontally, and divided into rings, each of which bears bundles of stiff spines, which the animal can move in various directions, or even withdraw. These bundles form two rows on each side; an upper, and a lower. The mouth is at one extremity, without teeth; the anus is at the other. On the side of the mouth are small fleshy and articulated antennae. The organs of respiration are small toothed crests, placed in the intervals of **[p. 627]** membranous leaflets, disposed on the back like two rows of scales. 1. L'aphrodite commune (A. aculeata). Oval, four to six inches long. Among the spines spring bundles of fine hairs, which shine with the finest metallic colours, and a kind of grey tow, mixed in, which covers the whole back like a mantle. This ver is spread in all our seas, above all toward the north. Its stomach is muscular and very robust; the intestinal canal produces on each side a quantity of canals which appear to distribute themselves to the gills; the nervous system is organised as in the insects. There are about forty rings and fourteen pairs of dorsal scales. The latter have a void in the interior like sacs.

II. *Les Amphinomes.* (Terebella. Lin.) Their body is more elongated than that of the aphrodites, and bears likewise two rows of tufts of bristles on each side: but their organs of respiration are kinds of plumes or ramifications, and are not covered by membranous leaflets; they run all along the back. One sees around the mouth several fleshy filaments.

III. *Les Amphitrites.* (Amphitrite.) Have the body elongated, furnished on each side with a row of tufts of bristles: but the plumes, often very large and very compound, which serve them as gills, are situated at the anterior **[p. 628]** extremity of the body only. The mouth is furnished with many fleshy filaments, and there are often under it kinds of combs, of hard substance and metallic colour, whose use is unknown. These animals keep in tubes which they compose of grains of sand, of fragments of shells, or of other substances. 1. L'amphitrite des huîtres (A. ostrearia). Is very common on oyster shells, which it covers with tortuous tubes, built of fine sand, and rather solid. It has a long tubular tail, which curves toward the entrance of the canal. Linnaeus united, under the name of *Sabella*, all the tubes similar to those of the amphitrites, whose animals he did not know. There are several in the number which appear to have been built by larvae of friganes or of other insects.

IV. *Les Serpules.* (Serpula.) Are animals similar to the amphitrites, which inhabit tubes of a calcareous, uniform substance, which they appear to form as the testaceous mollusques form their shell. These serpule tubes are attached to rocks or to other bodies, and are sometimes straight, sometimes tortuous, or diversely twisted, according to the species. 1. La serpule en masse (S. conglomerata). With tortuous tubes, lightly angular, glued to one another in considerable masses on shells, stones, etc. **[p. 629]** There has been separated from the serpules *L'Arrosoir* (Penicillus. Br.) Serpula penicillata. Lin., which is a conical calcareous tube, whose broad end is closed by a plate pierced with holes or with very short tubes, which a circle of somewhat longer tubes surrounds. The animal to which it belongs is not known. One must also separate from this genus pretended serpules in a regular spiral, whose turns are spaced from one another, which are inhabited by gastéropodes, such as the serpula lumbricalis Lin. and the *Siliquaires*, Brug., whose shell is a tortuous, spiral tube, with a slit which follows its whole length. The animal is a ver without bristles or tentacles. The serpula anguina Lin. is of this genus.

V. *Les Dentales.* (Dentalium.) Are tubes in the form of very elongated cones, of calcareous, solid substance, open at both ends, and free from all adherence, which are formed and inhabited by animals similar to the serpules. 1. Le dentale éléphantin (D. elephantinum). In a very long cone, a little arched, with nine longitudinal ribs; ordinarily of greenish colour.

VI. *Les Néréides.* (Nereis.) Are naked vers, inhabitants of the sea, whose body is elongated, articulated, and provided with tufts of bristles on each side, like the preceding, **[p. 630]** but which have no external organs of respiration. Their mouth is furnished with fleshy filaments or tentacles. 1. La néréide phosphorique (N. noctiluca). It is a small ver almost invisible to the naked eye, which is one of the most frequent causes of the glow often observed in sea water.

VII. *Les Naïades.* (Nais.) Are naked vers, inhabitants of fresh waters, whose body is long, slender, a little flattened, articulated, and whose lateral bristles are simple, but long and perceptible. Their mouth has no tentacle. 1. La naïade à trompe (Nais proboscidea). Reddish; the mouth lengthened in the manner of a thread. It keeps itself half sunk in the mud; the free part floats in the water, and sinks at the least appearance of danger. This ver multiplies in a very particular manner: its last articulation takes by degrees the form of an entire animal, which often separates from its mother only after having itself produced another in the same way. For the rest, it also multiplies by eggs; and its force of reproduction is such that one can cut it into several pieces, which all become perfect animals again.

VIII. *Les Lombrics.* (Lumbricus.) Are naked vers, with body long, cylindrical, articulated, provided with several rows of very small **[p. 631]** spines hardly perceptible, and whose mouth has no tentacle. They live under ground, or in the silt under water. 1. Le lombric terrestre (L. terrestris). Vulg. ver de terre. Is the most common and best known ver of all. It is known that millions of them show themselves on the surface of the earth after rain. It appears that they feed only on mould; they serve to render it more porous, and to facilitate the growth of vegetables. They have both sexes, and need for producing a reciprocal coupling. Their force of reproduction is rather great; and when one cuts them in two, each part becomes an entire animal.

IX. *La Furie.* (Furia.) Is a small ver of Lapland, with cylindrical body, provided, on each side, with a row of small spines directed backward. It is claimed that when the wind throws it on a man or on an animal, it penetrates in less than no time into the flesh, and causes a prompt death, preceded by atrocious pains: hence its name of furia infernalis.

B. *Vers* without spines and bristles.

Some genera live in water or in the earth, but the greater number exists only in the interior of animals.

X. *Les Sangsues.* (Hirudo.) Have the body elongated, more or less flattened **[p. 632]** horizontally, divided by many annular wrinkles, without bristles or tentacles, but provided, at each extremity, with a fleshy disc which exercises a considerable force of suction; they walk by holding themselves alternately on one of these discs, and by lengthening and shortening their body, according to need. Their mouth is under the anterior extremity: it has three small teeth, which cut into the skin of animals, from which the sangsue draws blood in long draughts. The anus is at the opposite extremity. Each individual is provided with both sexes, and needs, like the gastéropodes, a reciprocal coupling to produce. The sangsues keep only in fresh water. 1. La sangsue des chirurgiens (H. medicinalis). Narrow, blackish; six yellow stripes, varied with black above; spotted with yellowish beneath. It is used with advantage to draw blood in certain illnesses, above all in haemorrhoids.

XI. *Les Planaires.* (Planaria.) Have the body flattened horizontally, almost gelatinous, the mouth at the anterior extremity, and two openings under the belly, one for generation, the other for the anus. They live in fresh water like the sangsues. Their species are very multiplied; and as they differ by the form, the number of the eyes, the absence or presence of **[p. 633]** tentacles, one could perhaps divide them into several genera.

*Les Vers intestins*, properly so called, that is to say those which are found in the interior of living animals, are extraordinarily numerous. There is almost no animal which does not nourish several species of them, and rarely are those observed in one species of animal found again in another. There are some not only in the alimentary canal, but even in the cellular tissue and in the parenchyma of the best-clothed viscera. The difficulty of conceiving how they get there, joined to the observation that they are not found outside living bodies, has made several naturalists think that they are engendered there spontaneously.

XII. *Les Douves.* (Fasciola.) Have the body extremely flattened, the mouth at the anterior extremity, and a little further back an opening which serves as anus, and through which the organs of generation come out; both sexes are united in each individual, and they need a reciprocal coupling. They are oviparous; one sees in their interior tortuous intestines and coloured ramifications. They are found in all sorts of animals. 1. La douve du foie (Fasciola hepatica). In the form of a leaf, nearly an inch long. It is **[p. 634]** found in the biliary ducts and the gall bladder of many domestic animals, and above all of sheep, to which its too great abundance causes dropsy and death. *Les Ligules* (Ligula) appear to differ from the douves only because their body is excessively elongated and narrow like a ribbon. One species is found in birds, and one in fishes. *La Linguatule* (Linguatula) is a small flat ver like the douves, which has four small holes at the anterior extremity, and which is found in the lungs of the hare.

XIII. *Les Tænia.* (Tænia.) Are a very numerous genus, and very baneful to the animals in which these species dwell. Their body is entirely flat, composed of very distinct articulations, which ordinarily have a small hole on each of their edges; the head bears four openings proper for sucking, and often, between these openings, a circle of small hooks directed outward. Several species are found in man, and they are vulgarly known under the improper name of solitary worms. a) *Tænia* whose head has no hook. 1. Le tænia large (Tænia lata). White; with very short articles, knotted in their middle; the **[p. 635]** lateral openings isolated. It is found in the intestines of man. There have been some of more than a hundred feet long, and nearly six lines wide. This species is more common in Russia than elsewhere. It is driven out by castor oil. b) *Tænia* whose head is armed with hooks. 2. Le cucurbitain (Tænia solium). White, almost cartilaginous; with square or oblong articles, partly sheathing one another; with thin edges, with isolated lateral openings. It is found in the intestines of man, often in great number, and causes great ills and even death. It is the species most difficult to drive out. There have been some of sixty feet in length. It is said to be more common in Saxony and Holland than elsewhere. 3. Le tænia commun (Tænia vulgaris). Thin, transparent, and as if membranous; with double lateral openings. It is the most ordinary species in Sweden; it is also very difficult to drive out: but only some of from ten to sixteen feet in length have been found. c) *Les Hydatides*, whose head is armed with hooks, and whose body terminates in a bladder. They live in the very parenchyma of the viscera, or in the thickness of the membranes, and present there kinds of bladders full of a limpid liquor, which form part of their body. Their too great abundance often causes grave illnesses. 4. L'hydatide du cerveau (Tænia cerebralis). Has only a single bladder, common to several bodies; it is found on the brain and the cerebellum of sheep attacked by the staggers, an illness which makes them leap or turn, and which is regarded as incurable.

**[p. 636]** XIV. *Les Ascarides.* (Ascaris.) Are, after the tænia, the most common and most dangerous vers. They have the body long, round, pointed at both ends, and three small tubercles on the head by which they attach themselves to the intestines. They live there in troops, have the sexes separate, and are most often oviparous. Man has two species of them. 1. L'ascaride lombrical (A. lumbricalis). A span long; rather similar to the earthworm, but easy to distinguish by the absence of the small spines and of the annular swelling. Its tail is obtuse, and its anus in the form of a transverse slit. 2. L'ascaride vermiculaire (A. vermicularis). Half an inch long, with very pointed tail: it attacks above all children, and keeps in the rectum, where it causes insupportable ticklings. It is driven out by the infusion of Corsican moss.

Modern observers have further discovered in the intestines of man and of animals a multitude of other vers of which they have made several genera, such as *Les Trichures* (Trichocephalus). With body round, thick and obtuse behind, twisting in a spiral, terminated in front by a long, slender proboscis, without stings. **[p. 637]** One species is found in man, which is very frequent in certain illnesses, keeps above all in the caecum, and reaches up to two inches in length. *Les Échinorhynques* (Echinorhynchus). With body long, round, armed in front with a short proboscis furnished with small stings curved backward, by means of which they attach themselves fixedly to the intestines. They are numerous in species: but none has yet been found in man. *Les Crampons* (Hæruca). With body long, round; with mouth at the anterior end, surrounded by a circle of hooks curved outward. *Les Strongles* (Strongylus). With body long, round; with ciliated mouth; with tail terminated in the male by a spine and three small membranous leaflets. They are found in horses, in sheep, etc. *Les Uncinaires* (Uncinaria). With body long, round; with soft, angular mouth; with tail terminated in the male by a vesicle, in the female by a hook. They are found in badgers, foxes, etc. *Les Proboscides* (Proboscidea). With body long, round; with mouth in a proboscis, **[p. 638]** situated under a prominent muzzle, and without sting. They are found in several sorts of animals. *Les Cucullans* (Cucullanus). With body long, round, pointed behind, obtuse in front; with mouth situated under a kind of striated hood. They are found above all in fishes. *Les Massètes* (Scolex). With body excessively small, contractile; with large head, capable of taking various figures, and provided with four holes or suckers. Only two species are known, found in fishes. *Les Géroflés* (Caryophyllæus). With body round, short, obtuse behind, open in front in a broad mouth, cut out and fringed. They have been found in fresh-water fishes.

XV. *Les Dragonneaux.* (Gordius.) Have the body thin and very long, absolutely similar to a thread, pointed at both ends. 1. Le dragonneau des ruisseaux (G. aquaticus). Brown; the extremities blackish. Common in spring waters, humid clays, etc. 2. Le ver de Médine (G. medinensis). Whitish. It is of hot countries, often penetrates into the **[p. 639]** flesh of men, and causes there grave ills, which can be prevented only by drawing it out with much precaution, for fear of breaking it. Some have been seen of several feet in length. The dragonneaux observed in the body of various animals bear the name of filaria. They keep rather in the cellular tissue than in the intestinal cavity. One species lives in caterpillars, and does them much harm.

# Book VIII. Des Zoophytes

**[p. 640]** **Chapter I. Of the zoophytes in general, and of their division.**

§ 1. Here are the last of the animals, as to their organisation and their faculties. The mollusques have for digestion, circulation, sensations, respiration, nearly the same apparatus of viscera as the red-blooded animals; **[p. 641]** they even approach much to the fishes. The insects, a degree lower, have no longer a distinct circulation, and breathe only by tracheae; nevertheless one sees in them a spinal marrow, nerves and well-pronounced sensitive organs. One observes the same things in many vers, and it is probable that most possess them. But, in the *zoophytes*, we find nothing more of all that: hardly any digestive viscera, and some indication of respiration in some. No more centre of circulation, no more nerves, nor centre of sensations: each point of the body seems to nourish itself by suction, and to be provided by itself with the faculty of feeling.

§ 2. So most of these animals very quickly push out again the parts one removes from them. There are even some which multiply by a simple division, like plants. It must however be observed that, in this simplicity common to all, there are many different degrees; and we shall pass successively from beings provided with feet, with tentacles, with hard and soft parts, having distinct viscera, **[p. 642]** to others whose whole body is only a gelatinous pulp diversely configured, or presents even to the strongest microscope only a point apparently indivisible.

§ 3. The first orders are disposed according to the different degrees of perfection of their organisation. The last contain zoophytes of the last order of simplicity, but united in great number to form a compound animal, which has for base solid parts, of very different substances and forms. These last beings approach plants in exterior form as in simplicity of organisation, and it is with good reason that they are named zoophytes (animal-plants). One remarks, in the respective disposition of their organs, a manifest tendency to the starred or radiating form.

**[p. 643]** **Chapter II. Of the zoophytes which have a coriaceous or calcareous envelope, a distinct interior respiratory organ, and often numerous retractile feet; or Échinodermes.**

These zoophytes are the best organised of all. Their skin is often of a more or less crustaceous nature, or even it is a true shell. Their feet pass through holes in this envelope, and can extend or collapse: they are often arranged with much regularity. One sees in their interior an alimentary cavity, preceded by a mouth provided most often with five teeth disposed in a circle; ovaries, and a very extended ramified organ, which appears to establish a perpetual circulation of water through the body of these animals, and consequently a kind of respiration: but one perceives nothing which replaces the heart or the brain.

**[p. 644]** I. *Les Holothuries.* (Holothuria.) Have a body of cylindrical form, a coriaceous and thick skin. At one of the extremities is the mouth, surrounded by tentacles more or less numerous and branched, and armed with five calcareous teeth. In some species, the alimentary canal is blind, and the excrements come out again by the mouth; in others, it ends at the opposite extremity of the body, where there is a large opening through which the water enters the body and leaves it. The respiratory organ is formed of one or two trunks ramified almost to infinity, and resembling trees: they open into this posterior opening. The feet are sometimes scattered over the whole surface, sometimes all on one side, sometimes arranged in longitudinal series. There are species which have none at all. a) Among the species which have all the feet on one side, one may remark, 1. L'holothurie timide (H. tubulosa. Lin.). Cylindrical; nearly a foot long when it extends itself: the whole upper side of its body is covered with conical papillae; the underside is furnished with a great number of cylindrical feet, retractile, and terminated by suckers; around the mouth are twenty tentacles, whose extremity is in the form of a star. It is found in the Mediterranean sea. b) Among the species which have the feet disposed in several **[p. 645]** series, and which have been named with enough justice coriaceous sea-urchins (echini coriacei), one remarks, 2. Le concombre de mer (H. pentacta). Oval, five rows of feet; the intervals of these rows smooth; ten branched tentacles around the mouth. It is found in all seas.

II. *Les Astéries.* (Asterias.) Have the body divided into five branches forming a star. The mouth is at the centre, and has five calcareous teeth, without tentacles. The stomach is short; the excrements come out again by the mouth. Each branch has an articulated bony stem, and the rest of its envelope is supported by a network of the same nature. The feet are all on the side of the mouth, arranged in several files all along these stems. The opposite side is without them, but has an infinity of small tubes which the animal extends only when it is in the water, and which appear to pump it. The interior contains, besides the ovaries, five pairs of branched organs, whose trunks end toward the mouth, and which no doubt carry back outside the water introduced by the tubes. The astéries have their branches more or less thick or slender, furnished with spines more or less long and differently disposed, sometimes smooth, and divided, in some, into a multitude of branchlets. A lost branch is soon repaired. **[p. 646]** a) Species with conical branches. 1. L'étoile de mer commune (A. rubens). Its branches are thick, rounded; their point is little acute; their surface is bristling with very small tubercles, and furnished with small mobile calcareous tubes, from which come out the small fleshy mouths which draw in the water, and which must not be confused with the feet, which are much larger, and all on the lower face of the body. Independently of the movement of each of the feet, the entire branches can bend, though with much slowness. This species is of a fine reddish colour; it is so common on our coasts that in some places it is spread on the fields instead of manure. b) Species with ramified branches. 2. La tête de Méduse (A. caput Medusæ). Its five primordial branches are thin and angular; they divide and subdivide a great number of times, always by bifurcating. The last small branchlets are fine as hairs. It is found in all seas. c) Species simply angular. 3. La patte d'oie (A. membranacea). Flattened, thin, and simply pentagonal: the upper side is all furnished with tubercles bristling with small bristles; the underside has five radiating furrows, in which the feet are placed. From the Mediterranean sea. d) Species with more than five rays. 4. Le soleil (A. papposa). With eleven, twelve or thirteen flattened rays, bristling above with silky tubercles. From the Ocean.

**[p. 647]** III. *Les Oursins.* (Echinus.) Have an oval or rounded body, more or less depressed, whose envelope is entirely stony. Its surface is furnished with tubercles of different sizes, arranged with a surprising regularity, on which articulate calcareous spines of very varied thicknesses, lengths and forms, mobile at the will of the animal. The feet are tubular and terminated in suckers, like those of the astéries; they pass through holes of the shell disposed very regularly, and which form there as it were garden walks, whose name has also been given them (ambulacra). The interior contains an intestinal canal more or less long, an ovary divided into five clusters, and a ramified organ divided into five trunks which discharge into the mouth, and which appear to serve to make the water circulate in the interior of the body. a) *Les Oursins* properly so called have the body more or less convex, the mouth in the middle of the lower face, and the anus directly opposite. The ambulacra go from one to the other, and divide the shell into ribs like a melon. There are some with circular base, such as: 1. L'oursin mangeable (Echinus esculentus). Shell more than hemispherical; ten ambulacra, approached by pairs; their intervals filled with many **[p. 648]** small tubercles, almost equal, which bear short conical and violet spines. It is found in all seas. 2. Le turban (Echinus cidaris). Shell in a spheroid flattened at the poles; five ambulacra in zigzag; in each interval, two rows of large mamillae, bearing long and thick striated spines, fifty in number in all. There is a multitude of very small ones around their bases. With oval base, such as: 3. L'oursin violet (Echinus atratus). Flat beneath, with ten ambulacra. The spines of the whole convex part resemble nail heads or paving stones, and are pressed one against another: those which surround the base are a little elongated, and flat like oars. From the Indian sea. b) *Les Brisses* have the mouth in the middle of the lower face, and the anus on this same face, between the mouth and the edge. Their ambulacra form on the upper face a kind of flower with five petals. There are some convex above and concave beneath, such as: 4. La rosace (Echinus rosaceus). Shell in the form of a shield, oval, angular; its edges are thick; the whole surface is marked with very small sunken circles; the ambulacra represent five large oval leaves; the anus is near the most obtuse extremity. It is found in the Indian ocean. Others are entirely flat on both sides, and have the edges sharp. **[p. 649]** 5. Le gâteau (Echinus hexaporus). Thin as a flat cake; the ambulacra form a small rosette on the back; opposite the extremity of each leaf is a slit which pierces from one face to the other. There is a sixth slit opposite the anus, which is situated on the lower base, quite near the mouth. From the Pacific sea. c) *Les Spatagues* have the shell oval, the mouth under the shell, a little toward the front, and the anus precisely at its posterior extremity. The ambulacra also form a rosette on the upper side; the whole surface is shagreened, and bears short and thin spines like hairs. Their mouth is furnished with tentacles in the form of brushes. 6. Le spatague cœur (Echinus lacunosus). Five ambulacra around the mouth, and five sunken ones on the back, of which that opposite the anus, being deeper than the others, gives the shell the figure of a heart. N. B. One finds a very great number of fossil and petrified oursins, several of which let themselves be referred to the divisions above; but there are also some which have forms different from those we have just indicated.

**[p. 650]** **Chapter III. Of the soft zoophytes in which one sees no respiratory organ, and which have no retractile feet.**

A. The large ones, in which one perceives fibres and intestines, vulgarly *Orties de mer*.

I. *Les Méduses.* (Medusa.) The form of their body, in the quiet state, is that of a segment of a sphere, whose convexity is smooth, and whose plane part is provided with various tentacles. Its substance is transparent and gelatinous, and it reduces almost to nothing by evaporation or cooking. One sees in its interior coloured lines, but nothing which announces a circulation. One believes however one sees, toward the edges, more multiplied vessels, which appear to be appendages of the alimentary cavity. The méduses inhabit the sea; they swim very well by rendering their body alternately more or less convex, and by thus striking the water. When the sea withdraws, it leaves many on the shore, which are **[p. 651]** without movement there; for they can absolutely only swim. 1. La méduse bleue (Medusa aurita). It is large, very flat; its colour is of a bluish grey by reflection: for, looking at it directly, it is transparent. Under the body are four openings, in which one sees bundles of membranous leaflets. In the middle is a kind of round, mobile head, bearing four large toothed and pointed tentacles, and having at its sides several salient leaflets. From the Channel. There are many other méduses, varying by the greater or lesser length of the part which is under their body, which answers well enough in form to the stalk of a mushroom, and by the number and nature of the tentacles which attach to this part, or even to the lower surface of the body, and to its edges. The ancients named them free sea nettles.

Müller and Bruguières separate from the méduses *Les Beroé* (Beroe), which have a gelatinous body, of oval form, divided by furrows into ribs, like a melon. At one of the extremities is an opening which serves as mouth, from which come out in some species two tentacles more or less long and divided. The ribs are ciliated. They use these cilia to strike the water, and thus swim while turning. They are phosphorescent, and spread during the night a brilliant glow. 1. Le beroé globuleux (Medusa pileus). Large as a hazelnut; with eight ribs; with very long and ciliated tentacles. From the Ocean and the Mediterranean.

**[p. 652]** II. *Les Actinies.* (Actinia.) Ordinarily keep attached to the sand or to rocks by their base. Their body is coriaceous, and has a great force of contraction; which makes it vary from the form of a half-sphere when the mouth closes, to that of a cylinder when it opens. In this latter state, one perceives several rows of conical tentacles, long and mobile, which surround the mouth, and which, when they are all expanded, represent a fine flower of double anemone: so the actinies have been named sea anemones. They are the fixed sea nettles of the ancients. The mouth is round, and leads straight to the stomach, which is cylindrical, with wrinkled walls. The actinie feeds on small crabs, which it seizes and envelops with its tentacles; at least it tears off some legs from the large ones. The excrements come out by the mouth. There is between the walls of the stomach and the skin only an innumerable multitude of very slender intestines, interlaced together, whose use and outlets have not been ascertained. The actinies are famous for their force of reproduction. When one cuts them in two, each half completes itself again. They easily reproduce smaller portions. The young actinies are born alive, either by the mouth, or by piercing the body of their mother, whose scar soon closes again. **[p. 653]** It is even said that its base tears spontaneously, and that the pieces which remain adherent to the rock soon become small actinies. The greater or lesser extension of the actinies has, according to Dicquemare, a constant relation with the fineness of the weather to come. They walk sometimes on their base, sometimes on their tentacles. 1. Actinie pourpre (A. purpurea). Smooth; with fine skin, purple, often flecked with light green: the barbels are red; around them is a row of small sky-blue bladders. Common on the rocks of the shores of the Channel. 2. Actinie coriace (A. coriacea). With hard skin appearing tuberculate when it contracts; of an orange-fawn colour; the tentacles whitish, with a rose-coloured ring in their middle. It is larger than the first, and fixes itself only on sand, in hollows such that in contracting it is level with the ground. One must bring near the actinies *Les Zoanthes*, which have the mouth and the tentacles like the actinies, but whose body is more slender below than above; which gives them absolutely the air of a flower borne on a stalk. 1. Le zoanthe à cinq pétales (Actinia dianthus) (Hydra dianthus. Gm.). From the coasts of England. Its disc divides into five lobes charged with small tentacles. **[p. 654]** 2. Le zoanthe à drageons (Actinia sociata) (Hydra sociata. Gm.). A fleshy tube crawls on the ground, and pushes out, from space to space, stalks or animals. From the seas of America.

B. Those which have only a gelatinous body, without apparent organisation, and grow by buds, vulgarly *Polypes*.

III. *Les Hydres*, or polyps with arms. (Hydra.) Are those beings so famous by the discovery of the generation of animals by cutting, of which they furnished the first example. They have a conical body, whose point sometimes prolongs itself into a tail, and ordinarily attaches itself to some body. The widened part is open, and furnished with six, eight or ten filiform tentacles, very long in some species. Their body is only a kind of purse or cornet. Its walls present to the eye and to the microscope only a mucilage in which one sees more opaque points. They live on naïades, monocles, and other small aquatic insects, which they seize with their tentacles and put into the pouch which makes their body. These digest there visibly, and the excrements are rejected by the mouth. A polyp from which one cuts off any part whatever soon pushes it out again. If one cuts it in two, in whatever direction, each half **[p. 655]** becomes again an entire polyp. One can graft two halves of different polyps, or two entire polyps. Finally one can turn a polyp inside out like a glove, without its ceasing to fulfil its functions. Naturally it multiplies by pushing out from different points of its body buds which develop into small polyps. These sometimes remain thus attached, and even push out others again; so that there forms a kind of tree of which each branch is animated, has its own will, and seeks to take its nourishment apart, although what it eats seems to profit all. 1. L'hydre verte (Hydra viridis). It is the polyp with arms on which Trembley made his first experiments. It is of a fine green, and has its arms shorter than the body. It is very common in still waters, above all under duckweed. 2. L'hydre à longs bras (Hydra fusca). Is rarer; of grey colour. Its body is not an inch long, and its arms have more than ten.

Among the zoophytes neighbouring the hydres, of which Bruguières has made particular genera, we shall remark: a) *Les Botrylles* (Botryllus). Have a sessile head, surrounded by tubular tentacles, pierced along their length. The opening of the point receives the water. That of the base gives into the common mouth or the belly of the animal. Two species are known. **[p. 656]** 1. Le botrylle congloméré (B. conglomeratus). With several rows of close-set tentacles, which represent a kind of mulberry. 2. Le botrylle étoilé (B. stellatus). Has only a single row of tentacles which vary in number. Several botrylles étoilés are attached to a common body, in the form of a gelatinous crust which covers various marine plants. If one touches only one tentacle, it contracts alone. If one irritates the mouth or the common receptacle, they all contract. b) *Les Corines* (Corine). Have a soft, simple stem, bearing at its extremity a vesicle of the same nature, terminated by a mouth, and whose surface bristles with filiform tentacles. They produce eggs which remain some time attached at the bottom of the vesicle. We believe we must also make a genus c) of *Les Cristatelles*, or polyps with plumes (Cristatella), which have on the mouth a kind of plume formed by tentacles borne on a common stem, and arranged parallel or in brushes. Their movements serve to bring toward the mouth the corpuscles on which the animal feeds. The cristatelles inhabit stagnant waters, and their heaps appear to the naked eye only like spots of mould. 1. La cristatelle moisissure (Crist. mucedo). The common body has several mouths or plumes, does not form a tree divided into branches, but a single round or irregular mass. The plumes have the form of two combs with a double row of teeth.

**[p. 657]** IV. *Les Vorticelles.* Vulg. polypes à bouquets. (Vorticella.) Their mouth has no tentacles; but one sees come out of it small organs in the form of cilia, which turn without cease with rapidity, and whose nature and use are unknown. Some vorticelles are sessile, others lengthen into a tail, others finally are supported by filiform peduncles; these last are often united in trees, like the hydres, and represent well enough a bouquet of lily of the valley. They inhabit still waters, and are so small that an entire heap appears to the naked eye only like a spot of mould. They multiply by divisions: one of the flowers or corpuscles splits, and each half becomes a complete flower. 1. La vorticelle poire (Vorticella pyraria). Pedunculate, forming very compound bouquets, with flowers in the form of a pear. Attaches itself to aquatic insects, etc. 2. La vorticelle muguet (Vorticella convallaria). Isolated; with flowers in the form of a bell; borne on a peduncle twisted like a corkscrew, which lengthens and shortens at will. 3. La vorticelle trompette (Vorticella stentorea). Has no peduncle, but thins into a tail. It is simple, and resembles a trumpet whose mouth would be notched. **[p. 658]** 4. La vorticelle fruit de mûrier (Vorticella cratægaria). Sessile; with round corpuscles, united in groups, representing nearly mulberries. It attaches itself to the tail of the monocles.

C. The very small isolated ones, called vulgarly *Animaux infusoires*.

Already the vorticelles escape the naked eye. There is a multitude of other animals which equally can be observed only with the aid of the microscope. Their organisation becomes more and more simple, and one can divide them, according to these degrees of simplicity, into several very distinct genera. As the species are very numerous, genera have also been made of them founded on less important considerations, such as hairs, etc. We shall not dwell on them here. What these animals have most singular is that a great number of them are met only in infusions of vegetable or animal matters; there are even some which appear there only when these begin to corrupt. Finally some species inhabit only animal liquors, such as semen, etc.

IV. *Les Rotifères.* (Rotifer.) Have, like the vorticelles, two ciliated organs which turn continually; but they are isolated and **[p. 659]** free, swimming without cease in the water with an extreme celerity, most often while turning. They have kinds of tails which they lengthen or shorten. They unfold or draw in their rotatory organs; which gives them almost at each instant a new figure. One can observe them only with the microscope: they hardly present to the naked eye the appearance of indivisible points. 1. Le rotifère de Spallanzani (Rotifer redivivus) (Vorticella rotatoria, Gmel.). Is very common in still waters, where it appears to the eye like a greenish point. Its tail re-enters into itself like the tubes of a telescope. The anterior part is as if forked, and sends out organs which resemble toothed wheels. But all these parts change figure without cease by the various contractions. One sees in the interior a kind of stomach which has a slow and irregular movement. This small animal is above all famous for the property, which it was the first to show, of being able to remain dried and without movement for entire years, and of resuming life as soon as it is moistened anew.

V. *Les Brachions.* (Brachionus.) Are free like the rotifères, and have, like them, two rotatory organs before the head, and the faculty of swimming very fast for such small bodies; but their back is visibly covered with a kind of solid scale, which differs in form according to the species. **[p. 660]** 1. Le brachion à six dents (Brachionus urceolaris). Scale toothed in front, notched behind. It is often seen adhering by its tail to the monocles and other small aquatic insects. It sometimes has behind two small packets which have the air of clusters of eggs.

VI. *Les Trichocerques.* Are very near the brachions and the rotifères; they have a tail nearly similar, sometimes very long, or double, or forked: but there is no scale on the back, and the anterior part of the body is only furnished with kinds of hairs, and has no rotatory organ.

The rest of the infusory animals present to the eye only a soft body, contractile in all directions, in whole or in part, and in whose interior one perceives only a half-transparent jelly, filled with more obscure molecules. They change form arbitrarily up to a certain point: nevertheless their body resumes in most, when they rest, a form determined for each species. Those which have hairs at one extremity have been named *trichodes*; *leucophres*, those which have the body surrounded with cilia; *kérones*, those which show kinds of horns; *bursaires*, those which have it hollow in the form of a sac. It is round and depressed in the *gones*, flat and sinuous in the *kolpodes*, flat and oblong in the *paramèces*, flat and oval in the *cyclides*, round and elongated in the *vibrions*, cylindrical in the *enchélides*. **[p. 661]** The *protées* are the most singular of all. No determined form can be assigned to their body: for it changes it at each instant, and takes successively all possible forms; sometimes rounded and gathered up, sometimes divided and subdivided into strips in the most bizarre manner. One species is found in marsh water. It is to the family of the *Vibrions* that belong 1. Les anguilles du vinaigre (Vibrio aceti). Which resemble, by their elongated form and manner of swimming, the fish whose name they bear. They are often large enough to be distinguished by the naked eye. Frost does not make them perish, but evaporation does, unless they are protected from contact with the air by some dust. It is claimed that they change skin; that there is among them distinction of sex; that they bear live young in summer, and that they then lay eggs until the end of autumn. 2. L'anguille de la colle (Vibrio glutinis). Which is found in flour paste, and has nearly the same economy as the preceding. It is distinguished by a brown spot, surrounded with white, which it has on the back. *Les Cercaires.* Are microscopic animals, with oval body, of fixed form, terminated by a tail: no intestines are seen in them. There are some in various infusions: but it is above all those which inhabit the semen of animals which have drawn the attention of naturalists, by the role it was believed they played in generation. It appears that they are entirely foreign to this animal function, and are there only as guests. **[p. 662]** *La Baccillaire* (Baccillaria) is a very paradoxical being. It appears to consist of small cylindrical animals, similar to little rods, but forced to live together by a law which is unknown. In the quiet state, they are pressed against one another, and form a long square: but they can advance by pairs or otherwise, so as to make a scarf or any other figure, as by military evolutions. This animal is found on the algae of the coasts of Denmark.

VII. *Les Volvoces.* Have a round body, of fixed form, transparent, and without any visible organ; they swim by whirling, with more or less rapidity. 1. Le volvoce sphérique (Volvox globator). Is found abundantly in summer in marsh waters: it is then reddish. One sees in its interior globes similar to it, which come out of it to whirl likewise, and which from then on contain others smaller, so that it is big at once with several successive generations. 2. Le volvoce du fumier (Volvox conflictor). In dung water; moves by whirling, to the right and to the left alternately. Its whole interior is full of round molecules, which move without order and much faster than it. *Les Monades.* (Monas.) Are the simplest and smallest of known animals: even under the strongest microscopes, **[p. 663]** they appear only like points, either round or oval, moving with celerity in every direction. There are thousands of them in the least drops of all infusions and of all still waters. One would even be tempted to believe that several of these microscopic animals form only from the decomposition of the matters submitted to infusion.

**Chapter IV. Of the zoophytes in which the animal substance traverses the axis of the horny substance which serves it as envelope, and has each of its branchlets terminated in a polyp; or Zoophytes properly so called.**

The hard part, or at least the crust which clothes them, appears to form part of their body and to grow with them by intus-susception; so that the branches which are born here and there from the trunk, in the species which do not remain simple, are true vegetations, quite like those of the polyps of the preceding chapter, and not additions which the inhabitants would build against those **[p. 664]** which already existed, as are, for example, the cells which bees add from time to time to their construction. It is therefore justly enough that the animals of which we are going to treat have been named zoophytes, or animal-plants. The solid part has taken, by a figurative expression, the name of stem, and the head of the polyps, or rather their mobile part provided with tentacles, that of flower.

The genera of the zoophytes are:

I. *Les Flosculaires.* (Floscularia.) The stem is a small conical tube, simple, which appears, under the microscope, composed of hexagonal pieces, all marked with a point. The animal is a rotifère; but it does not appear intimately linked to its case, and perhaps one ought to have placed it elsewhere than here. The head appears like a flower with four lobes which would turn perpetually in the same direction. It is found in fresh water, on leaves, etc. Sometimes new tubes fix themselves against the first.

II. *Les Tubulaires.* (Tubularia.) Have for stem a tube of substance similar to horn, sometimes simple, sometimes branched, at whose extremity shows itself an animal sometimes **[p. 665]** similar to the cristatelles or polyps with plume, other times showing two rows of tentacles, an exterior circular, radiate one, and an interior one, in a tuft, representing almost the pistil of a flower. The eggs come between the exterior tentacles. 1. La tubulaire à collet (Tubularia campanulata). Forms a small branched stem, crawling on the leaves and stems of fresh-water plants, which is gelatinous at first, and hardens little by little. The orifices of the tubes are narrower, and form kinds of collars. 2. La tubulaire ouverte (Tubularia reptans). Resembles the preceding, and is from the same places. The ends of the tubes are not narrowed. The crests of both are in a double row and in a half-circle. 3. La tubulaire sultane (Tubularia sultana. Blum.). Is of fresh water, and resembles the preceding. Its crest is only in a single conical packet, like the plumes called sultanes. 4. La tubulaire chalumeau (Tubularia indivisa). Is one of the numerous marine species; this one is common on our coasts. Its stems are very simple tubes, yellowish, two to three inches long, similar to bits of straw, which have toward their base kinds of membranous and twisted knee-joints.

III. *Les Capsulaires.* (Capsularia.) Have a papery stem, simple or branched. All the extremities are terminated by small oval capsules, open for the mouth of the animal, **[p. 666]** which is a corine, and pierced with small holes for the passage of its tentacles, which cannot withdraw into the capsule. They grow on fucus in the seas of England.

IV. *Les Sertulaires.* (Sertularia.) Have a horny stem, formed of one or two rows of vesicles placed alternately, and whose figure varies. This stem is sometimes simple, sometimes branched; and these branches are disposed in all sorts of ways, like the leaflets in the compound leaves of plants. The sensitive substance traverses the stem and the branches, as the pith of a plant would, and there comes out from each cell an animal, or rather a member of the total animal, of the form of a hydre or polyp with arms. Multiplication is made by eggs, which appear in clusters in cells larger than the others, otherwise placed, and of a different form. The sertulaires have the air of a small plant of the greatest delicacy. 1. La sertulaire goupillon (Sertularia thuya). Its stem is straight, firm, folded in zigzag. The end is very branched; the branches flat, bearing on each edge a close-set row of small conical vesicles. Those of the eggs are oval, large, and placed in the bifurcation of the branches. 2. La sertulaire plume (Sertularia pluma). The branches are simple, and disposed on both sides of **[p. 667]** the stem like the barbs of a feather. The vesicles of the hydres are bell-shaped, toothed, and in a single row. Those of the eggs are similar to pea pods; that is to say broad, compressed, pedicellate. It grows on our coasts, on mussels and marine plants. 3. La sertulaire épineuse (Sertularia spinosa). Very fine, with long ramifications, in zigzag: from each fold come out small branches, divided anew, but shorter. The vesicles of the hydres are narrow and pointed. They are seen only in the fresh plant. This species is common on our coasts.

**Chapter V. Of the zoophytes of which each polyp is adherent in a horny or calcareous cell with thin walls; or Escares.**

One does not see here, between all the parts of the animal substance, a communication as direct as in the genera of the preceding chapter. Each polyp appears isolated in its cell; and if they all communicate together, it is no doubt by the slender parts which traverse the substance of the cells themselves: for **[p. 668]** one can hardly doubt that they grow by an ordinary intus-susception, although the new branches develop by a germination analogous to that of plants. One sometimes sees, on the cells, small bubbles, believed to be the ovaries.

I. *Les Cellulaires.* (Cellularia.) Have, like the sertulaires, the form of small plants extremely slender and composed of articulations; but, instead of salient vesicles which the polyps holding to the pith traverse, there are only cells hollowed in the surface of each articulation, and a polyp is lodged in each. Moreover, the stem of the sertulaires is horny; that of the cellulaires is calcareous and friable. 1. Cellulaire rampante (Cellularia reptans). Is the most common species on our coasts. It attaches itself to fucus, to mussels, etc., and forms pretty little slender bushes of the greatest whiteness. The cells are disposed in two rows and turned to the same side: they resemble inverted cones. Each opening is provided with two small spines. From the ramifications depart small roots furnished with hooks, as in creeping plants. 2. La cellulaire cotte de mailles (Cellularia loriculata). Each articulation resembles a corselet, and has two cells directed obliquely, which represent the arm holes. It forms a straight and bushy shrub.

**[p. 669]** II. *Les Flustres.* (Flustra.) Their cells are placed entirely against one another on a single surface, nearly like a honeycomb, or like a net. Some species thus cover other bodies; others lean on nothing, and form leaves or stems persisting by themselves, of which a single side is furnished with cells in certain species, and both in others. 1. La flustre en feuilles (Flustra foliacea). Forms leaves furnished with cells on both sides, similar to the finest gauze, narrow at the base, widening toward the top, often dividing, and having the edge toothed. It is very common on our coasts. Its cells have the form of arched doors, are placed in quincunx, and furnished with a small spine on each side. 2. La flustre tronquée (Flustra truncata). Has the same texture as the preceding; but its stems or leaves are narrow, of equal width everywhere, and truncated at the end.

It is believed one must also refer to this order: III. *Les Corallines.* (Corallina.) Although their polyps have not yet been perceived. They have the form of plants more or less branched; the stem and the branches are composed of articulations coated with a calcareous substance, and **[p. 670]** whose figure varies according to the species. When the calcareous matter is dissolved, there remains an axis of horny substance, from which depart fibres of the same nature which traverse the calcareous substance to go to the surface. It is supposed that they enclose polyps which come out by imperceptible pores, or that they are themselves animated and take their nourishment by these pores. 1. La coralline des boutiques (Corallina officinalis). Covers immense spaces on the shores of the sea. Its articulations are oval, the point downward. The branches are disposed twice in succession like the barbs of feathers, and very close-set; so that the entire plant forms a small very bushy calcareous shrub. The colour varies, sometimes white, sometimes greenish, most often reddish.

**Chapter VI. Of the zoophytes which have an axis of solid substance, covered everywhere with a sensitive flesh, from the hollows of which polyps come out; or Cératophytes.**

Their axis is sometimes woody or horny, sometimes stony. It is covered with a fleshy, sensitive substance, able to contract. **[p. 671]** It forms hollow tubercles, from which come out at will heads, or rather tentaculate mouths in the form of polyps; they all belong to the same animal, like the branches of a hydre. What is taken by one profits the whole: it even appears that the total animal has a common will; for it extends itself, when necessary, to form itself a base by which it adheres to any body whatever. It extends itself also to repair the losses caused to one of the points of the cortical substance; it even covers over and coats with a new layer of the matter of its axis the foreign bodies which may have attached themselves to this axis. The axis appears produced by the bark; one perceives concentric layers in it. Those which are woody have longitudinal tubes which appear to communicate with the polyps; in the stony ones, one sees only striae. There are some whose middle is void and traversed by membranes or diaphragms: it has been given the improper name of pith. The branches are also produced by the fleshy bark which glues them against the trunk, but without **[p. 672]** their pith and their layers having communication with its own.

I. *Les Antipathes.* Vulgarly corail noir. (Antipathes.) The axis is of a horny substance, ordinarily blackish, hard, and disposed in layers. The surface is ordinarily bristling with small spines. The animal crust is purely gelatinous, and it disappears on the dried coral. The latter is sometimes simple, sometimes differently branched. 1. L'antipathe spiral (Antipathes spiralis). Stem entirely simple, of the thickness of a quill, rolling irregularly like a corkscrew; spines very small. From different seas. 2. L'antipathe éventail (Antipathes flabellum). Stem short, thin. From its two sides are born branchlets which rise in the same plane while subdividing likewise, and representing a kind of fan. The last ramifications are sometimes soldered to one another. From the Indian sea.

II. *Les Gorgones.* (Gorgonia.) The axis of the gorgones is most often horny, sometimes woody, or resembling cork or hardened leather; in this they resemble the antipathes: but the flesh which clothes this axis contains chalky particles, which, when it comes to dry, **[p. 673]** form a calcareous and friable crust which has well enough the air of a coating applied on sprigs of fucus. Some authors have even believed that the gorgones were only a calcareous layer formed on plants by polyps; but it has since been recognised that the axis belongs to the animal, like the exterior bark. 1. La gorgone éventail (Gorg. flabellum). Its small branches each unite with those nearest to it, and all remain in the same plane, so that the whole represents a net or a trellis, often of some feet in length and width; the bark is yellow. This species is found in all seas.

One can separate from the gorgones *Le Corail* (Corallium), which has, like them, a sensitive flesh, provided with cavities from which come out heads and arms of polyps, and filled with calcareous molecules which form a crust on drying. But the axis which this flesh covers is of a stony and compact tissue like marble. Its external surface is striated. It is the substance known under the name of coral, and so esteemed on account of its fine red colour, and the polish it is susceptible of taking. Its flesh or its bark is of a dawn red. The polyps have each eight toothed tentacles. The axis is sometimes white; one also sometimes sees in it concentric layers of different colours. Its base is rather broad and glues itself to rocks, or to some other fixed point: the branches are few in number, and diverge almost at a right angle. Coral ordinarily grows under some overhang of rock. **[p. 674]** It is fished by letting down a machine formed of iron branches, placed in a horizontal cross, which tear it off, and to which it catches. The coral whose polyps are dead, and which has been attacked by other animals, is less precious.

III. *Les Isis.* (Isis.) Have an axis alternately stony like that of coral, and horny like that of the gorgones, that is to say that the stony articulations are joined together by others of horny substance, and sometimes of spongy substance. The former are striated like the axis of coral. As the tree of the isis grows, the horny articulations of the stem disappear, because the animal covers them with stony layers, so that there remain no more of them except on the branches. The flesh which covers the axis is similar to that of the gorgones and of coral. 1. L'isis noir et blanc (Isis hippuris). The calcareous articulations are of a pure white, and their horny joints of a very dark brown; which produces a very fine effect. This species is found in all seas.

IV. *Les Pennatules.* (Pennatula.) Have a cartilaginous, free stem, covered with a fleshy bark, and whose upper part has branches disposed like the barbs of a feather, flattened, which have themselves kinds of barbs, but only on one side, between which appear **[p. 675]** the polyps. The pennatules swim in the sea with a movement common to all their animals. Several species spread a glow in the dark. 1. La pennatule luisante (P. phosphorea). Reddish; with stem raised with small scales; with barbs covering one another like tiles: it is the species which spreads the most glow. It is found in all seas. One could distinguish from the pennatules *Les Vérétilles* (Veretillum), which also have a free stem, but without any branch, covered uniformly with a sensitive flesh, and strewn with polyps similar to hydres, provided with eight ciliated arms. And *Les Ombellules* (Umbellula), which have a bony stem, thin, and without branches, enveloped in a fleshy membrane, and terminated by a bouquet of polyps, forming an umbel attached at its extremity. These compound animals are free and swim with a common movement. Their polyps resemble hydres, and have each eight ciliated arms. Only one species is known, which is found in the sea of Greenland.

**[p. 676]** **Chapter VII. Of the zoophytes which have an axis or a base of stony substance, in which are hollowed the receptacles of the polyps; or Lithophytes.**

These animals are in such great number in certain seas that they form entire islands there. Several of those of the South Sea consist only in heaps of madrépores, which, after having risen to the surface of the waters, were covered with silt by the waves, and have thus surmounted the level of the sea.

I. *Les Madrépores.* (Madrepora.) Are all the lithophytes whose receptacles are furnished with parallel or divergent lamellae, which most often represent the figure of a star. We shall subdivide them as follows. a) *Les Fongites* (Fungites). Are composed only of a single stony star, often very large, whose plates are vertical, and depart, in some species, from the centre to go to the circumference, **[p. 677]** dividing according to certain laws. In others, the plates are parallel to one another, and depart from both sides of a straight or curved line; which produces a coral of more or less elongated form, sometimes forked. All this stony part is covered with a kind of sensitive mucilage, on which one remarks small mobile vesicles, arranged along the plates. All that disappears and withdraws between the plates when one touches the fongite or draws it out of the water. 1. Le fongite champignon de mer (Madrepora fungites). From the Indian sea. White, round; one side of the plates is convex; the opposite side is concave, and one sees there series of small spines. Rather often there are other smaller fongites which adhere to it, but which have their plates on the concave side. It appears that this fongite is free in the sea. 2. Le fongite chenille de mer (Madrepora pileus). Of very oblong form, flat. Its plates are arranged on both sides of a line which cuts them all; they have their edge in an arc of a circle: outside there are others which begin between the extremities of the first. b) *Les Méandrites* (Meandrites). Have their surface hollowed with valleys more or less long, sinuous and branched, separated by similar hills. The plates are perpendicular to the direction of the valleys: sometimes they extend over the hills to those of the neighbouring valley; sometimes the hills are smooth. Other times the valleys are so deep and the hills so steep that they resemble striated leaves. Their animals are not yet known. 3. Le méandrite laitue (Madrepora lactuca). It is one of those which have the valleys so deep. Its hills **[p. 678]** resemble rather sharp plates, transversely striated, so that their whole has some relation with lettuce leaves. 4. Le méandrite à sillons (Madrepora phrygia). Forms, like the following, an oval mass. Its valleys are very long, little deep, narrow, little branched, almost straight and parallel. The hills are narrow and smooth. 5. Le méandrite lisse (Madrepora gyrosa). Valleys broad, little hollow, little branched, but much twisted; hills smooth; plates little salient, or rather transverse pits of different lengths, and as if cavernous. c) *Les Astroïtes* (Astroites). Have a mass either spherical, or extended in leaves sometimes flat, sometimes more or less plaited or twisted. Their surface is strewn with starred cells, scattered rather equally. Their animals are not exactly known. 6. L'astroïte rayon d'abeille (Madrepora favosa). Spherical: the stars are large, angular; the plates are toothed, and continue over the intervals, which are narrow, to join those of the neighbouring stars. From the Indian sea. 7. L'astroïte feuille (Madrepora foliosa). In broad and irregular leaves, strewn with very small stars on a lightly tuberculous ground. d) *Les Porites* (Porites). Have a stem sometimes simple, sometimes branched, more or less long, cylindrical or prismatic, but where there are stars only at the extremity of each branch. **[p. 679]** 8. Le porite gerbe-liée (Madrepora fascicularis). Stems cylindrical, striated, simple, gathered in great number into a kind of rounded sheaf. 9. Le porite branchu (Madrepora ramea). Round, divided into few branches, striated toward their extremities. 10. Le porite chardon (Madrepora carduus). Large round stem, dividing into two or three branches; plates toothed, continuing outside into longitudinal series of tubercles. e) *Les Madrépores* properly so called (Madrepora). Have a branched stem, strewn with stars on all the parts of its surface. The name of white coral is given in particular to the species of this genus which have this colour. 11. Le madrépore pointu (Madrepora seriata). The branches are few, and terminate in a point. The stars are arranged in longitudinal series. 12. Le madrépore abrotanoïde (Madrepora muricata). Its whole surface is as if bristling with spines, because the edges of its stars are salient, and represent small tubes. It varies extremely by its branches more or less divided, forming sometimes a very simple small tree, other times a rather bushy shrub, or a kind of close sheaf, like a cauliflower, etc. There are, in a word, innumerable varieties. Its colour is white.

II. *Les Millépores.* (Millepora.) Are the lithophytes whose receptacles are **[p. 680]** only simple pores, or small holes often hardly visible. a) There are branched ones, such as: 1. Le millépore solide (Millepora polymorpha). White, hard; with short, numerous branches, taking all sorts of forms. The pores cannot be perceived by the naked eye. It is so common in certain seas that it is used to make lime. b) Others are membranous, and represent irregularly plaited leaves. 2. Le millépore feuille (Millepora foliacea). Lamellous, plaited, bearing pores on both sides; it coats stones, shells, etc. It is found in the North Sea. c) Some are reticulate, that is to say thin, flat, and pierced through and through with a great number of holes. 3. La manchette de Neptune (M. cellulosa). It is borne on a stalk, and widens like a calyx; its walls are plaited, and pierced with holes so close-set and so regularly disposed that they resemble lace or netting. The pores are all on their inner face. It is one of the prettiest lithophytes. It is found in all seas.

**[p. 681]** **Chapter VIII. Of the zoophytes which have for base a spongy, friable or fibrous substance, coated with a sensitive crust, sometimes containing polyps.**

Only two genera have been made of them.

I. *Les Alcyons.* (Alcyonium.) Their interior is light, friable when it is dried, composed of fine fibres, stiff and divergent. The envelope is a soft crust, without calcareous particles, which becomes coriaceous on drying, and which is pierced with cells, from which come out the heads of the polyps. a) There are some which extend like a bark over the surface of different bodies; such as 1. L'alcyon mamelonné (Alcyonium mammillosum). Its surface is fleshy, and contains in its interior a sort of fine sand; it covers rocks, and presents, on its surface, mamillae pressed against one another, and each pierced by a cylindrical cell. b) Others form more or less rounded masses, such as: **[p. 682]** 2. L'orange de mer (Alcyonium lyncurium). Of irregularly globular form, void in the interior, adhering to rocks by one point only; surface white, uneven, pierced with many small pores disposed in quincunx. 3. La figue de mer (Alcyonium ficus). In the form of a fig or a pear, adhering by a stalk; one or two large cells on the top. c) Finally there are some more or less branched. 4. La main de mer (Alcyonium digitatum). It covers various marine bodies, forming some obtuse prominences, which have been compared to fingers. Its whole surface is pierced with round pores.

II. *Les Éponges.* (Spongia.) Are perhaps the beings which participate least in animal faculties; they consist in a fibrous tissue more or less dense, more or less flexible, which is coated, in its fresh state, with a sort of animal jelly half fluid and very thin. The only sign of life claimed to have been observed in them is a slight quivering, a little-marked contraction, when one touches them. After death, the animal jelly disappears, and only its base remains. 1. L'éponge des boutiques (Spongia officinalis). In large brown masses, formed of very fine fibres, flexible, elastic, and pierced with a great number of pores and small irregular conduits, giving into one another. **[p. 683]** 2. L'éponge œillée (Spongia oculata). Tissue white, firm; stem cylindrical, tuberculous, irregularly branched, pierced, from space to space, with holes similar to the cells of a madrépore. 3. L'éponge à canons (Spongia fistularis). Large simple and straight tubes, whose walls consist in crisp fibres, stiff, fine, almost brittle, of black colour.
