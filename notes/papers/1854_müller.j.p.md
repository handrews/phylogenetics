# Über den Bau der Echinodermen

Johannes Müller. Read before the Royal Academy of Sciences in Berlin on 26 May, 9 June and 18 July 1853. Berlin, 1854. With 9 copper plates.

**[p. 3]**

No branch of comparative anatomy is so rich in peculiar relations of structure as the anatomy of the Echinodermen, and none is attended by greater difficulties. The dissection of *Pentacrinus* gave me the first opportunity to try my hand at it; a further occasion was provided by the Asteriden, when Mr. Troschel and I worked on their systematics. Occupation with the metamorphosis of the Echinodermen then made it necessary to penetrate more deeply into the structure of the Seeigel [sea urchins] and Holothurien. What was gained thereby has been communicated to the Academy on occasion in the investigations on the metamorphosis of the Echinodermen, in particular the observations on the madreporic plates. This and other matter has passed into the anatomical studies on the Echinodermen, Archiv f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1850 p. 117 and 225, and was communicated provisionally without the illustrations. The treatise on *Synapta digitata*, Berlin 1852, contains some further advances. In studying the Clypeaster and Spatangoiden it became clear to me that much still remained to be done here; I had to draw them too into the circle if I wished to survey what is peculiar to the individual divisions and what is common to all, and if I wished to explain the homologies of the Echinodermen correctly. Some of the elements for a comparative consideration or philosophy of the Echinodermen are already contained in the fine works of Agassiz, Forbes and Duvernoy. The tasks, however, have in part been altered and heightened by our knowledge of the development of the Echinodermen, and it has become possible to open up the structure of these animals further than has hitherto been done. I now bring together everything that **[p. 4]** has become known to me from my own observation about the structure and the homologies of the Echinodermen. (1)

## I. General type of the Echinodermen.

The essence of the Echinodermen lies, apart from the radial form and division, in the calcification of the perisome and of many internal parts, in their peculiar metamorphosis and above all in their ambulacral formations, the sucker-feet, which can be distended by a peculiar system of internally ciliated canals.

The larvae of the Echinodermen have only bilateral symmetry and are still without any trace of the radial rudiment; in their circling by ciliary movement one end is also constantly directed forwards. The fully developed Echinodermen, on the other hand, are primarily laid out radially, yet in such a way that they at the same time bear more or fewer traces of a bilateral symmetry. In the Holothurien that creep on a sole and in the irregular Seeigel the bilateral symmetry is at once evident. Not all Echinodermen creep on a constant side or are sole-bearing; many Holothurien have nothing of a sole. The sole, or the side turned towards the ground, sometimes comprises an equal part of all the radii or ambulacra, so that the mouth lying in the centre of the radii occupies the middle of the sole, as in the regular Seeigel and in the Asteriden; or the mouth is at the end and the sole does not belong to all the radii, but of the 5 ambulacra only 3 are developed into the sole, as in the sole-bearing Holothurien; the ventral and dorsal sides are therefore not constant in relation to the radial form. The question of the bilateral symmetry of a radial form is made exceedingly complicated both by this and by the predominantly radial symmetry of the regular forms, and it at once becomes necessary to consult the instincts that manifest themselves in the Echinoderm during its movements.

All sole-bearing forms have the most decided instinct to turn their sole, of whatever kind it may be, and whether the mouth lies in the middle or at the end of the sole, towards the ground, and when laid on their back they strive to turn themselves over, like an insect laid on its back.

So already behaves the radial rudiment of the Echinoderm in the larva, as soon as the first ambulacral feet have broken through. The primordium of the Seeigel and of the star, dragging the scaffolding of the larval form about with it, resists by the movement of the little feet every position on the glass in which the feet are not directed towards the glass, and re-establishes by means of the sucker-feet the natural sole-down position. Without doubt it does not lie in the instinct of the animals to turn the sole or ventral side in the direction of gravitation: for insects run along the underside of solid walls as soon as they are able to adhere to them, and the Seeigel creep nimbly along vertical walls with their sucker-feet; rather it lies only in the instinct of the animals to turn the sole-side of the ambulacra towards solid walls wherever these present themselves.

The regular forms, whose sole comprises an equal part of all the radii and whose mouth lies in the centre of the sole, like the regular Seeigel, the Asterien and Ophiuren, do not creep preferentially in one direction with one constant radius foremost, but it is now this, now that radius or interradius in whose direction the animal advances. What Tiedemann states of the Asterien holds equally of the Ophiuren and Seeigel. These animals move as if they did not know at all what is front and what is back in them, definite as their distinction of ventral and dorsal side is. The quite young Holothurie with 5 sucker-feet round the mouth and without feet on the body does not yet creep on its later sole-side, but behaves as if the mouth-feet were its sole, and in doing so conducts itself like a Seeigel with its mouth directed towards the ground; its body is held erect, it attaches itself by suction with the mouth-feet and stretches them out alternately in different directions. But when the first foot has broken through ventrally at the hind end of the body, it at once makes use of this too, as if it belonged at the same time to the sole, and by using now the mouth-feet, now the single foot for suction and as a fixed point for stretching out, it is in transition to a sole-bearing Holothurie. Even in this condition the young Holothurie is not seen to move in one direction only; rather, groping about on the glass, it changes its place now hither, now thither. As for the adult sole-bearing Holothurien, I have not yet seen them creep in the direction head foremost; **[p. 6]** but I do not doubt that they do so, because they are entirely fitted for it. The reports on this are mostly unclear; they have been seen to creep, but it is not stated in what direction. Johnston says of *Thyone papillosa* (Forbes brit. starf. p. 236) that it possesses a slow progressive motion, slower than the shadow on the sundial, and that this takes place by the suckers lengthening at one spot, fixing themselves, and then drawing the body along by contraction; the suckers, however, are more often used as anchors than as feet, since this creature is of an indolent and immobile character. Without a sandy bottom the Synapten do not move preferentially in one direction, but bend and wind hither and thither; but Quatrefages has seen them, on a sandy bottom in vessels of water, bury themselves in the sand with their mouth-feet.

An important place in this question is taken by the elongate Seeigel. In the Spatangen the sole consists of portions of all 5 ambulacra, but the ventral mouth lies nearer to one end, and the anus at the other end between two radii and opposite the unpaired radius, which is therefore called the anterior radius. It seems in fact to deserve this name, because its feet are preferentially developed for locomotion. Accordingly the anterior radius has been sought in the regular forms, whose anus lies at the dorsal centre (Cidariden and most Asterien), or, as in some Asteriden, is wholly absent. According to Agassiz it was to be that radius which lies opposite the madreporic plate. According to Valentin this radius would be preferentially directed forwards in the locomotion of *Echinus*. But the madreporic plate stands in no relation whatever to the axis of the animal, and it is now certain that in many Echinodermen, and indeed in most cases, it is lateral.

Des Moulins already correctly recognised this position in his Etudes sur les Echinides. Bordeaux 1835–37. He draws attention to the fact that in *Echinoneus* and *Ananchytes* the madreporic plate deviates to the side of the longitudinal axis towards the anterior right interambulacrum. In *Echinus* the anus is approximated to one ambulacral area. Des Moulins makes it probable that the anus in *Echinus* lies in front of the dorsal pole. With reference to this there then results again the lateral position of the madreporic **[p. 7]** plate, which is expressed by Des Moulins thus: Or la plaque apiciale poreuse, dans le genre Oursin proprement dit, fait face à l'aire anambulacraire laterale gauche. In fact, in *Echinus* as well as in *Cidaris* it is the left posterior genital plate.

This is confirmed also by the Salenien, where the longitudinal axis is determined by the plaque suranale in front of the anus. In an exceptionally fine specimen of *Salenia personata* Ag. with anterior anus, preserved in the mineralogical museum, Pl. I. Fig. 9, the left posterior genital plate is porous and is the madreporic plate.

Since the position of the madreporic plate was not always correctly indicated in the excellent works of Agassiz and Desor on fossil Seeigel, I had subjected this subject to a revision in many genera of fossil Seeigel, the results of which, with the illustrations, were laid before the Academy already on 12 June 1849. What relates to this has been used in the anat. Studien über die Echinodermen, Archiv für Anat. und Physiol. 1850. p. 128. On Pl. I of the present treatise the illustrations of the apex of these Seeigel can now be surveyed; the side of the test that is posterior according to the position of the anus is in the illustrations everywhere the one directed towards the lower edge of the plate. In *Ananchytes* Fig. 13 and *Dysaster* Fig. 11 the anterior right genital plate is porous; in *Galerites* Fig. 1, *Nucleolites* Fig. 4, 5, *Pygurus* Fig. 7, *Discoidea* Fig. 12, *Micraster* Fig. 10, *Toxaster* Fig. 3 the porous middle of the test is an expansion of the anterior right genital plate, which is surrounded by the remaining genital plates and intergenital plates. In *Hemipneustes radiatus*, finally, not one but many plates of the apex appear to become porous, the genital plates as well as the intergenital plates, and only the hindmost intergenital plates seem to be exempt from the porosity. Pl. I. Fig. 2. In the careful illustrations which Forbes has given in the Memoirs of the geological survey from the genera *Nucleolites*, *Discoidea*, *Galerites*, *Ananchytes*, *Acrosalenia*, the lateral position of the madreporic plate is exactly indicated.

Of particular interest among the Cidariden are the elongate Echinometren.

In *Echinometra acufera* Bl. the anus is approximated to one radius, namely to the longest radius, so that the antero-posterior diameter coincides with the longest diameter. If one brings the unpaired middle **[p. 8]** longer radius of an *Echinometra* to the front, the madreporic plate lies constantly lateral and is the left posterior genital plate. The radii and interradii of *Echinometra* appear in this position completely symmetrical, and it is the only position in which they are symmetrical. In the genera *Heterocentrotus* Br. (*Acrocladia* Ag.) and *Colobocentrotus* (1) Br. (*Podophora* Ag.), which have branched off from *Echinometra*, the body is symmetrical only in the transverse position, which was already recognised for the first-named genus by Brandt and expressed by Corpus transversum, but was not noticed by Agassiz, who regarded these forms together with *Echinometra* as oblique. The correct orientation of these tests is with the greatest diameter of the test transverse, the smallest longitudinal, and the smallest radius to the front. In this orientation of a *Heterocentrus trigonarius*, *mammillatus*, of a *Colobocentrus atratus*, the madreporic plate is as a rule the right anterior genital plate. Among 24 specimens of *Colobocentrus atratus*, 23 had this position of the madreporic plate, but one had this plate in the left posterior interradius. Such variations depend on which side, whether the right or the left, of the larval framework the Seeigel disc with its madreporic canal forms; deviations which, as appears from my illustrations of the larvae of *Echinus* from Helgoland, have already been observed.

In *Echinometra* the transversely symmetrical condition occurs only rarely, as an anomaly; among a large number of Echinometren (about 80 individuals from all the known species) only 4 specimens were found whose radii are symmetrical only in the transverse position. Among 15 specimens of *Echinometra acufera* Bl. from the same locality (Venezuela) there was one, among 6 specimens of *Echinometra oblonga* Bl. one, that was symmetrical in the transverse position, or had the shortest radius as the unpaired one; in the rest the unpaired radius was the longest of the 5 radii.

The division of the Echinometren into genera, rejected by Souleyet (Bonite), is hereby already confirmed as in accordance with nature.

position is suspect; its pore-field in *Schizaster canaliferus* proceeds from the right genital plate and from the immediate vicinity of the right genital pore. I recently had the opportunity to confirm this condition also by internal dissection. The canal arising from the madreporic plate to the ring canal of the ambulacral vessels, the analogue of the stone canal of the Asterien, is inserted in *Schizaster canaliferus* into the ring canal between the anterior right and the posterior right ambulacral canal, that is, corresponding to the right posterior interradius. In the Clypeastern the hind side is likewise determined by the position of the anus; in this division of Seeigel, on the other hand, the madreporic plate lies exactly in the centre between the genital openings, the latter not lying in special plates. Remarkably, however, in this case too the canal coming from the madreporic plate to the ring canal, as well as the short thick heart lying beside it, is so placed that its position corresponds not to the interradius of the anus but to the right posterior interradius. In the Asterien the anus and the madreporic plate lie in different meridians. If one brings the Seestern [starfish] developed in *Bipinnaria asterigera* into the position in which the radius over which the anal tube emerges is directed forwards, then the nearest interradial space on the left is that of the madreporic plate; it is the same interradius that is pierced by the oesophagus of the larva. In several species of *Ophidiaster* several madreporic plates appear, and in *Echinaster echinites* madreporic plates all round the disc in different interradii.

In the Holothurien the madreporic plate and its stone canal lie in the dorsal part of the body cavity near the mesentery and the genital duct, which opens in the dorsal interradius. In this case the unpaired ventral radius is approximately opposite the stone canal. In the just-metamorphosed, still quite young Holothurie of 3/10‴ size, whose sides right and left are distinguished by spheres and which possesses the first foot at the hind part of the body, the calcareous sac, the analogue of the madreporic plate, is situated in the dorsal part of the body cavity, not however in the middle, but more towards the left side of the back. In *Synapta serpentina* there are stone canals with madreporic plates all round the ring canal.

From all this it now follows that both the anus and the madreporic plate can be interradial; that now the one, now the other can move out of **[p. 10]** its interradial space into the centre; that the interradius of the madreporic plate can be a different one of the 5 interradii from the interradius of the anus; that the madreporic plate, even when it lies midway between the genital pores or at the hind end of the apex, can be reduced to a lateral genital plate, and that it is connected by the canal of the madreporic plate not with the hind part of the ring canal but with a lateral part of it; finally, that not one of the 5 interradii is constantly that of the madreporic plate, but that the latter can rather have its seat in the most various interradii, now on the right, now on the left.

The madreporic plate is sometimes a porous calcareous labyrinth existing by itself, a special plate whose pores lead to the stone canal of the ambulacral vessels, as in the Asterien, among the Ophiuriden in the Euryalae, and among the Seeigel in the Clypeastriden, or an internally situated madrepore-shaped beginning of the stone canal as in the Holothurien; sometimes this labyrinth also fuses with a calcareous plate of another function, e.g. with one of the genital plates, as in most Seeigel. From these variations it already follows that this labyrinth possesses no deeper relation to one of the genital plates. In the observations on the Seeigel larvae (Echinodermenlarven, 4th treatise) it has been shown, however, that the madreporic plate develops from a pore of the larva, which is connected by means of a canal (stone canal) with the ring canal of the ambulacral vessels of the Seeigel disc. This pore belongs originally to the larvae themselves; it is the dorsal pore of the larva, which in the Asterien larvae, Bipinnaria, is already visible before a trace of the perisome of the Asterie appears. At the time when the young Seeigel becomes independent and when this pore has passed over to it, there are, however, as yet no plates at all on the perisome of the Seeigel, neither ambulacral plates nor interambulacral plates nor genital plates. The relation to the plates is therefore everywhere secondary, and the position of the madreporic plate on the Echinoderm depends on the varying spot where the canal coming from the back of the larva enters the Echinoderm; whereas the position of the pore in the larvae of the Seeigel as well as of the Asterien and Holothurien is everywhere the same, on the back of the larva to one side of the midline.

Let us now compare the unpaired radius of the five-parted Echinodermen **[p. 11]** where it is easily determinable, as in the Seeigel and Holothurien. Here it can at once be proved that there cannot be one and the same anterior radius, valid for all Echinodermen, analogous to the anterior radius of the Spatangen. In the Holothurien front and back, and in the sole-bearing Holothurien also the bilateral symmetry, are unambiguous; but the unpaired radius in the sole-bearing Holothurien lies not forwards and upwards from the mouth, in front of and above the mouth as in the Spatangen, but directly the opposite, downwards and backwards from the mouth, behind the mouth; the unpaired radius is here the middle ventral radius, and the sole is formed of 3 radii, the dorsal side of 2 radii.

Now is the belly of the Spatangen, or of *Echinus*, or of the Holothurien the true belly? I think no one will, in order to resolve this contradiction, wish to say that the Holothurien exceptionally walk on their back. If one imagines as the middle form of the Echinodermen a sphere with radial areas, the question arises: is the fundamental form of the Echinoderm carried over from the orientation of *Spatangus* to that of the Holothurie by the body rolling over in the plane of division between right and left, namely rolling from the interradius turned downwards onto the opposite radius; or must one think of this change as effected by a rotation of the fundamental form about the axis of the radii, namely in such a way that the sphere is brought from the position with an interradius downwards into the position with a radius downwards? In the first case the bilateral sides and the interradius of the anus are constant; only the designation of the bilateral sides with respect to right and left changes with the rolling-over in the plane of division. In the latter case the bilateral sides and the interradius of the anus are inconstant, but front and back constant; different radii and interradii can develop into the sole, and the interradius of the anus changes correspondingly.

If there is a homology of the Echinodermen, it can be sought only in the first conception. For by a rotation about the axis the position of *Spatangus* can indeed be approximately, though not entirely, transformed into the position of the Holothurie. But for the correction of this position, and especially for the transformation of a *Spatangus* into an *Echinus*, the other rotation, about a transverse axis or in the plane of division between the bilateral sides, is after all again necessary.

**[p. 12]**

If therefore we seek an ideal intermediate between the various actual forms of the Echinodermen, this ideal fundamental form will not be able to possess a constant front and back; it has the mouth at one pole of the ambulacra, but in the transition into the various forms of the Echinodermen it will direct forwards certain directly opposite sides of the radial body. The sides which it can direct forwards are, however, in any case definite ones; they are in all cases intersected by the same constant meridian of the radial form, i.e. the longitudinal plane of division for the bilateral sides of the ideal fundamental form is constant for all cases.

Best are those designations which are derived from a form itself and not from positions which it can change. In this sense one can bring the ambulacra of the five-parted Echinodermen into two divisions, one containing 3, the other 2 ambulacra; we then obtain a triradial and a biradial section, a trivium and a bivium. In the Seeigel of the genus *Dysaster* trivium and bivium are actually separated from one another by a large interval, but in most five-parted Echinodermen they can be found and the position of the radii determined accordingly. Between the 2 radii of the bivium lies the anus, or the anus moves from the mouth up to the apical pole and even across to the opposite unpaired radius.

If the unpaired radius is unknown, it is determined from the meridian of the anus, which in *Cidaris* and *Echinus* is approximated to one of the 5 ambulacra. If the unpaired radius is known but the anus central, as in the sole-bearing Holothurien, the meridian of the anus results from the unpaired radius of the elongate form.

On the ideal spherical form of the Echinoderm, therefore, the oral and apical pole, a triradial and a biradial section with the anal field, will have to be entered. If this Echinoderm possesses its ambulacral areas equally developed in all directions, it will be a Seeigel with the mouth side directed towards the ground. The sphere is in this position, as it were, in equilibrium; but it is intersected by an ideal plane which passes through the meridian of the unpaired radius. If it turns within this plane out of the position of equilibrium in such a way that the bivium **[p. 13]** appears ventrally more than the trivium, the orientation of the Spatangoiden results. If the sphere turns within the said plane in the opposite direction, so that the trivium alone appears, the orientation of the Holothurien results. On the ideal spherical model of the five-parted Echinoderm, accordingly, there are fixed and valid for all possible cases: the oral pole and apical pole and their axis, the opposition of trivium and bivium, the plane of division passing through the meridian of both, which divides the Echinoderm into 2 equal parts, and finally the anal field. In the creeping Echinodermen the oral pole can be directed downwards or forwards, the apical pole upwards or backwards; the trivium with the unpaired radius can be directed now forwards, now downwards, the bivium with the unpaired interradius now backwards, now upwards, the anus downwards and backwards, backwards, upwards and backwards, or upwards.

The Echinoderm has an oral pole or vertex pole of the radial divisions and an apical pole opposite to it. The mouth, surrounded by the ring canal of the ambulacra, lies as a rule in the centre of the vertex, seldom off to one side. The surface between the poles is divided into ambulacral and interambulacral segments. Through the expansion in breadth of the former, the latter can disappear entirely, as in the Holothuriae sporadipodes. In the Holothurien the ambulacra extend from the mouth to the other end; if they cease before this other end, there arises, opposite to the ambulacral zone, an antiambulacral zone, like the apex of the Seeigel, which continues into the interambulacral areas. If an Echinoderm has arms for radii, like the Asterien, the antiambulacral field continues both onto the antiambulacral side of the arms and between the arms into the interambulacral areas. Not seldom, as in many Seesterne, the interambulacral part of the perisome is distinguished from the antiambulacral field by its plating, the interambulacral plates. Also the boundary of the interambulacral and antiambulacral fields is sometimes marked between belly and back by a peripheral margin or even by special marginal plates, like the marginal plates of the pentagonal *Astrogonium* and *Goniodiscus*, which therefore lie between the ambulacral and antiambulacral zone. In the Asterien and many Crinoiden the ambulacral or ventral and the antiambulacral or dorsal side are in equilibrium. Arms are freely projecting radii with **[p. 14]** an ambulacral and an antiambulacral side, and are either simple or divided. Divisions produced by outward bulgings or incisions at the periphery of the Echinoderm are lobes if they have no distinction of an ambulacral and an antiambulacral side, as the lobes of some flat Seeigel, *Runa*, *Rotula*, *Encope*. There are ambulacral and interambulacral lobes (*Runa*). Ambulacral lobes are ambulacral on both opposite sides and can be divided again (*Rotula*). By incisions of the margin and lobe formation, therefore, the Seeigel is not brought nearer to the Seestern. If the antiambulacral field of the Echinoderm grows up to the vicinity of the mouth, arms for the ambulacra can still develop from the surroundings of the mouth, as in *Agelocrinus*, *Pseudocrinites*, and like the arms discovered by Volborth at the oral part of the calyx in *Echinoencrinus* and *Echinosphaerites*. Ambulacral pores on the antiambulacral side, beyond the arms, are a contradiction in themselves, and it appears that wherever the arms stand, at the circumference or at the oral part of the calyx, there can be no ambulacral sucker-feet and no ambulacral pores from the apical end up to the arms. The genetic development of the ambulacra, as it is explained here, therefore already makes it probable that the pores in the calyx plates of *Caryocrinus*, *Hemicosmites*, *Echinosphaerites* cannot be ambulacral pores, since they stand on the antiambulacral side and still behind the arms and have no relation whatever to the arms. This is also proved by the analogy of *Pentacrinus*, which possesses similar pores in the interambulacral areas, and indeed without feet, without any relation to the ambulacral grooves of the calyx, while the feet stand in the ambulacral grooves of the calyx and of the arms. I return to the antiambulacral calyx pores of *Caryocrinus*, *Hemicosmites* and *Echinosphaerites* in the special section on the ambulacra of the Crinoiden in general and in particular of the group of the Cystideen of L. v. Buch. In an Echinoderm which remains antiambulacral up to the vicinity of the mouth and develops the arms only from the oral part of the calyx, that has become a maximum which is present in the Seeigel as a minimum. Expressed in the manner of the Naturphilosophie, it would be said that the calyx of a *Pseudocrinites*, *Agelocrinites*, *Echinosphaerites*, *Echinoencrinus* is the apex of a Seeigel; it is, however, such an extension of the apex as **[p. 15]** encloses the entire viscera of the animal, which in the Seeigel are for the greater part enclosed by the ambulacral zone of the perisome.

The extent of the ambulacra can also be reduced to a circle of sucker-feet round the mouth, as in the Synapten, Chirodoten, Molpadien among the Holothurien. In the last case the antiambulacral field is of so great an extent only externally. Jäger has already proved that the ambulacral canals of the Synapten extend internally as far as in the other Holothurien.

Although the ambulacra converge towards the vertex pole, they are nevertheless not in all cases provided with feet up to the middle of the vertex. In *Echinus*, for example, the ambulacra are interrupted around the vertex, and in many Crinoiden too the vertex field between the ambulacral sides of the arms contains, instead of a continuation of ambulacral grooves up to the mouth, rather a uniform plating, as in *Actinocrinus* and others.

To the ambulacral canal system in the wider sense belong, besides the feet, the ambulacral canals of the radii with their branchlets into the feet and their ampullae, the connection of the 5 ambulacral canals round the mouth, and some appendages connected with the ring canal. The system of ambulacral canals shows ciliary movement over the whole extent of its inner walls; it is everywhere closed, except for the porous beginning of the stone canal at the madreporic plate. This canal from the madreporic plate to the ring canal has at present been observed in all forms of the Echinodermen, with the exception of the Crinoiden. Its porous beginning is either open to the exterior as the madreporic plate of the Asterien, Euryalae, Seeigel, or turned towards the body cavity as the porous stone sac of the Holothurien, which only in the juvenile state continues to an external pore. In the Ophiuren too the stone sac is hidden in the body cavity. On the more recent discoveries in this field, from the observations of Agassiz, Krohn and myself, a report is given in the anatomischen Studien über die Echinodermen, and the treatises on the Echinodermenlarven contain the observations on the first development of this system. The special history of the stone canals I reserve for a special section of the present work.

With the ring canal of the Echinodermen, besides one or more stone canals, two further kinds of organ are connected, the Polian vesicles **[p. 16]** and the racemose appendages. Both kinds of organ occur together in the Asterien, as is known from Tiedemann's investigations, and appear again in the Holothurien. In the latter the racemose appendages are recognisable as small vesicles which hang in pairs by short stalks on the ring canal of the young Holothurien, and in which very peculiar double granules move tremblingly, in a similar manner to the way the otoliths of the Gasteropoden tremble. In the adult Holothurien these organs can be recognised again in the wreath of many small vesicles which beset the ring canal all round and open into its interior. The Ophiuren have 4 simple vesicles, no racemes. On the ring canal of the Spatangen the appendages are absent. In the regular Seeigel five stalked vesicles are connected with this canal; they have the same position as the Polian vesicles of the Ophiuren, but their walls are cellular (Valentin) and in this respect related to the racemose appendages; in the Clypeastern, however, the ring canal is provided only with many such appendages. The sucker-feet are always closed at the end, not open, as Monro and Valentin assumed of the Seeigel. The ampullae connected with the ambulacral canals to the feet are everywhere organs for the distension of the feet; some have taken them in the Seeigel, without any reason, for internal gills. Their contractility on stimulation has been established by Erdl. Wiegm. Arch. VIII. 1. p. 57.

The ambulacral feet, filled with fluid through the ambulacral canals of the water-vascular system, are partly locomotive sucker-feet, which is the most usual case, partly tentacles, as around the mouth of the Holothurien, partly leaf-shaped or gill-shaped, as on the back in the ambulacra petaloidea of the Spatangoiden and Clypeastriden, ambulacral gills. Of quite another kind, and foreign to the ambulacral tubes, are the skin gills occurring in *Echinus*, *Diadema*, *Echinocidaris* and in the Asterien, caecum-shaped little tubes whose hollow interior communicates with the body cavity; ten tree-shaped gills in *Echinus* and its nearest relatives, placed at the anterior margin of the test in the notches (entailles) of the same; they are already absent in *Cidaris* among the regular Seeigel (Delle Chiaje), whereas the fossil *Hemicidaris*, *Salenia* and others already by their incisions at the anterior margin of the test allow the presence of the gills and the relationship with *Diadema* and *Astropyga* to be inferred.

**[p. 17]**

Among the Spatangoiden the skin gills never occur. In the Asterien they are simple little tubes which are spread and scattered over the whole dorsal side. That the skin gills of the Asterien and Echinen are not open at the outer end, as Tiedemann believed, but rather blindly closed, has been established for the Asterien by Ehrenberg, for the Echinen by Valentin.

Distinct from the ambulacral canals are the blood vessels. A connection between them, which was assumed by Delle Chiaje and others and recently again by Williams, has never been able to be proved, and the state of our knowledge of the distribution of the two systems is still the same as Tiedemann handed it down. Since then, however, important internal grounds from the structure of the two canal systems have become known in favour of his view. The system of the ambulacral canals is ciliated on its inner surface, as Sharpey and Quatrefages have described. The blood vessels are canals without internal ciliary movement, whose walls move in an undulating manner up and down. J. Müller Über Synapta digitata p. 9. In most Echinodermen one knows, according to Tiedemann, only the blood-vessel trunks on the intestine and, at the anterior part of the body, a circle which is well to be distinguished from the ring canal of the ambulacral canals and is easy to observe in the Asterien. Into this blood-vessel ring opens the heart, which arises on the back of the Asterien from the vascular circle situated there, and in the Seeigel from the circulus analis. In *Astropecten aurantiacus* a fine branch can be traced from the blood-vessel ring round the mouth onto the beginning of each of the 5 arms, where it at once divides into 3 branches, whose continuation in the arm groove has remained unknown to me. The threads from the intestine to the test of the Seeigel are only ligaments. In the Holothurien one knows only the blood-vessel trunks on the intestine. The network of these vessels entwining the branches of the one lung-tree does not spread onto the lung and remains wholly foreign to it; it is rather only a rete mirabile. If in these animals the fine ramifications of the soft vascular trunks onto the trunk of the lungs and the skin are not known to us, this is nevertheless no reason to deny their presence.

The nerves of the Holothurien and Seeigel were discovered by Krohn, Archiv f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1841. 1.; those of the Asteriden were described by me, ibid. 1850. p. 120. What Tiedemann described in the Asterien as **[p. 18]** an orange-coloured vessel round the mouth is there the nerve ring. The nerve trunks of the ambulacra divide at the mouth into equal halves, which then, diverging, pass into the nerve ring and form it alone. The nerve trunk of an ambulacrum is therefore in the Asterie twice as broad as the nerve ring round the mouth. The nerve trunks of the ambulacra are in the Seeigel even thinned towards both ends of the ambulacra.

The innumerable muscular organs on the test of the Seeigel that are subject to the will, such as the sucker-feet, pedicellariae and the muscles of the spines, receive their nerves from the nerve trunk of the ambulacrum, whose branches accompany the branches of the ambulacral vessels. The nerve trunks of the 5 ambulacra, joined round the mouth, form the stronger part of the nervous system, and since in the Seeigel they become thinner towards both ends of the ambulacrum and in their middle part far surpass the nerve ring at the mouth in strength, the nerve trunks of the ambulacra are to be regarded as ambulacral brains, which are brought to unity by the nerve ring. It is this ring which in *Synapta* is interrupted by a cut when one divides its head lengthwise on one side and thereby deprives it of the ability to break itself in pieces.

The skeleton of the Echinodermen is not a purely external shell, but consists of bone formations which take place in the perisome; it is a capsule round the viscera, but it can, as Ehrenberg rightly remarks, be everywhere still covered by living soft parts. The nerve trunks and vascular canals can lie inside and outside this shell. Usually the shell is still overlain by a layer of skin, sometimes by muscles of the spines, as in the Seeigel, or by pedicellariae; even the spines of the Seeigel are, according to Ehrenberg's fine observation, for the longest time covered by an exceedingly delicate ciliated skin, which has been disputed by some but seen again by me. Archiv f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1853. 1.

The more or less distinct layer of skin lying over the skeleton is either soft, or again contains small calcareous parts projecting freely outwards in the form of granules, as in many Asterien. The calcareous plates and joints of the skeleton themselves can also form the boundary towards the exterior and be free of membranous coverings, like the joints of the stem and the dorsal side of the arms of *Pentacrinus* and the spines of **[p. 19]** full-grown Seeigel. One sees clearly how nature has not maintained a sharp boundary between soft and calcified parts of the perisome, i.e. parts traversed by calcareous networks.

The digestive viscera of the Echinodermen are formed in the larvae according to a general plan and are uniformly articulated; their later differences in the Asteriden, Holothurien and Seeigel appear only during the metamorphosis.

With regard to the distribution of ciliary movement over the skin, the surface of the body cavity and the viscera, I refer to the fine observations of Sharpey. Here belong also the special cornucopia-shaped or slipper-shaped ciliated organs discovered by me in the body cavity of the Synapten.

## II. Analysis of the ambulacra of the Seeigel.

### Ambulacral plates.

The double pores of the ambulacra lie in the Seeigel either in the plates themselves or in the sutures; the latter occurs in the family of the Clypeaster in the ambulacra petaloidea. In this case either all the ambulacral platelets are alike, as in the ambulacra petaloidea of *Scutella*, *Laganum*, *Echinarachnius*, *Lobophora*, *Mellita*, *Encope*, *Echinocyamus*, or alternately unequal, as in *Clypeaster* and *Arachnoides*; the smaller platelets in these extend only from the outer pore to the inner pore, the larger extend from the outer pore to the inner suture. On this Des Moulins has already made good observations. At the lower end of the ambulacra petaloidea, moreover, the double pores pass readily from the sutures onto the plates themselves.

Des Moulins has expressed the conjecture that to each double pore of the Seeigel there originally corresponds a special ambulacral plate. Of the pores of the locomotive feet of the Clypeastriden, however, many come to one plate, and their number increases with the growth of the plate. On the other hand that conjecture seems to be applicable to all other Seeigel.

In the Cassiduliden the double pores usually stand in a single row; in some genera, such as *Pygurus*, *Catopygus* and others, these rows become on the ventral side towards the mouth, on each side of the ambulacrum, **[p. 20]** double or triple. *Pygurus depressus* Ag., for example, has on the ventral part of the ambulacra towards the mouth 3 vertical rows of pore pairs on each side. Through this multiplication of the pore pairs arises the so-called buccal rosette of these genera. On examination of suitable specimens it turns out that this part of the ambulacra nevertheless possesses for each double pore a small plate of its own, mostly wedge-shaped. The wedges of the plates have their points directed now to the one, now to the other side of the ambulacrum. Only every third plate has the whole breadth of the half ambulacrum. In the whole remaining part of the ambulacra, where the double pores stand on each side in only one vertical row, the ambulacral platelets are alike and extend from the outer to the inner suture of the ambulacrum; so it is also in *Dysaster* and in general in most Cassiduliden, but not in *Galerites*. In *Galerites*, indeed, each pore pair of the row has its own plate; but these plates are unequal, namely at the outer margin all are alike, but inwards they extend unequally far, which Desor has well noticed and calls primordial and intercalated plates. It can, however, be proved in the young Clypeastern that both are equally primordial; nor is it a simple alternation of two kinds of plates, as Desor expresses it (Galerites p. 8); rather, each two larger pore plates, of which one is stronger outwards, the other inwards, have a smaller pore plate between them, and only the two larger ones reach the inner suture. This triad of pieces then repeats itself on and on. See our illustration Pl. II. fig. 6. In *Scutella* like plates follow one another; in *Clypeaster* two unlike plates follow one another regularly; in *Galerites* groupings of 3 plates; and so it is also in *Echinus*, where 3 or 4 or 5–10 ambulacral platelets (according to the species) are united into a larger whole, which we may call a secondary ambulacral plate, a phenomenon which has not hitherto been noticed but repeats itself in all species of this genus.

In *Echinus* and *Echinometra* the sutures between the primitive platelets which together form a secondary ambulacral plate can be demonstrated on the inner side of the test; externally they are less recognisable owing to the development of the tubercles. On closer examination, however, one finds even the tubercles divided by these sutures. **[p. 21]** Pl. II. fig. 1. of *Echinus sphaera*. With 4 pore pairs in a secondary ambulacral plate there are 4 pieces of very unequal horizontal diameter; of these the two middle ones are smaller transversely; all 4 pieces extend to the outer suture, but only the anterior and posterior of the 4 pieces reach the inner suture and lay themselves against one another around the ends of the shorter pieces, so that the secondary ambulacral plate is inwardly again divided transversely by a suture. The same suture is found with 3 pore pairs; here the platelet of the middle pore pair is again the one which does not reach the inner margin. See the illustrations Pl. II. fig. 1. *E. sphaera*, fig. 4. *E. pulchellus* Ag. In *Echinus albus* Molina 9–10 primary pore platelets for 9–10 pore pairs form a secondary ambulacral plate. Pl. II. fig. 2. In *E. sphaera*, where 3 primary platelets are combined into a secondary ambulacral plate, sometimes only one of three reaches the inner suture. In *Boletia pileolus* Ag. 3 primary platelets form a secondary plate; of these, however, only one reaches the inner suture. Pl. II. fig. 3. In *E. albus*, of 9–10 primary platelets only 2 or 3 reach the inner suture. The pores are situated mostly in the primary platelets, sometimes, at least on the inner side of the test, also between the primary platelets.

That the smaller plates are of later origin, as Des Moulins seeks to make probable in *Clypeaster* and Desor assumes of *Galerites*, seems to me inadmissible, since all are of equal vertical diameter and an interpolation is never observed. I find in the youngest specimens of *Clypeaster placunarius* of 8‴–11‴ size the alternating ambulacral plates, unequal in breadth, completely equal in vertical diameter. In *Echinus* too, if a species has a sequence of 3 pore pairs in an oblique row, these 3 pairs are present in young as well as in old specimens of the species, as I see on comparing very young specimens of *E. pulchellus* Ag. with old ones. Specimens of 1½‴ size of the test (without the spines) have the oblique rows of 3 pore pairs. I am rather of the opinion that the new platelets arise at the apical end of the ambulacrum.

The growth of new coronal plates at the dorsal end of the corona around the apical plates was first proved by Agassiz in the regular Seeigel and confirmed by Philippi. It is all the more striking that **[p. 22]** Agassiz did not perceive this growth in the Scutellen; he even asserts that Scutellen of different ages showed the same number of plates in the longitudinal rows. This is certainly not the case in the age stages examined by me. In very young specimens of *Clypeaster placunarius* of 8‴ size the number of ambulacral platelets or pore pairs in the leaf-shaped ambulacrum is much smaller, namely 20; at 11‴ size 25; in specimens of 5″ size about 50. Specimens of *Arachnoides placenta* from India (1), very young, of only 9‴ size, have only 20 pore pairs in a row; at 13‴ size 25; a specimen of unknown locality of 2″ 9‴, on the other hand, has 46 pore pairs. The same holds of the multiplication of the interambulacral plates. The proof furnished by Agassiz in *Echinus* can therefore be carried out in the same way in the Clypeastern, whose whole underside grows, with retention of the number of plates, only by enlargement of the original plates, while the upper side also enlarges the plates but at the same time constantly adds new minute ambulacral and interambulacral platelets at the apex, so that the number of plates in a vertical row on the upper side constantly increases with growth, on the ventral side always remains the same.

The Clypeaster have a permanent equatorial periphery, the Cidariden do not. If one compares a young and an old *Echinus* of the same species, the plates which in the young one lay in the equatorial periphery have in the older one moved onto the ventral side, and the equator is occupied by a circle of plates which at the time of youth was approximated to the apex.

How, in the regular Seeigel as well as in the Clypeastern, an addition of new coronal plates round the apex is possible is made comprehensible by the observation of Philippi (Wiegm. Arch. 1837. 1. p. 244), namely that the enlargement of the coronal plates during growth takes place unequally at their margins and that they grow much more in breadth than in length. The Clypeaster show the same phenomenon. The stronger enlargement in breadth does not hold in the Clypeastern equally of all plates, but preferentially of the ambulacral plates; it is visible towards the dorsal periphery in contrast to the interambulacral areas, and is very striking on comparison of young and old specimens of *Clypeaster placunarius* and of *Arachnoides placenta*.

In no Seeigel have I observed an increase of the coronal plates in the narrower sense at the oral margin of the corona. What Valentin, anat. du genre Echinus p. 12, adduces for this, the formation of small plates around the mouth in *Cidaris*, refers to the very peculiar special buccal plates of *Cidaris*, which continue the corona proper in movable form toward the mouth. Pl. II. fig. 7. These buccal ambulacral and interambulacral plates do indeed increase in number at the buccal end of the rows, where they are smallest (perhaps also at the hind end of the rows, where the hindmost is also less broad); but they are without influence on the growth of the corona itself. The corona of the Seeigel has rather, as Agassiz rightly remarks, a fixed boundary toward the oral side, which is already recognizable from the fact that the auricular processes of the Echinen and Clypeaster run out from the outermost transverse row of coronal plates.

According to Philippi, the formation of new small plates around the apex, which he saw in the regular Seeigeln, would be lacking in the Spatangoiden. But it is beyond doubt in *Schizaster canaliferus*. I have compared specimens of 8''' length, of 2'' and of 3''. The first has in the paired anterior ambulacrum only 28, the second 40, the third 44 pairs of pores; in the paired posterior ambulacrum the first has 14, the second 21, the third 25 pairs of pores on each side. On the other hand, it is impossible to imagine an increase of the plates around the anomalous apex of the *Dysaster*.

In the growth of the Clypeaster the distance between the two pores belonging to one gill-foot increases, which is very distinct in Clypeastern of different ages; this can only happen through a successive displacement of the pores lying between the plates, not through extension of the plates. The growth of the ambulacral plates takes place only at the margins, and in breadth preferentially toward the inner side at the middle suture of the ambulacrum, as Philippi has already shown in the regular Seeigeln.

So far we have spoken only of the ordinary double pores of the ambulacral plates, which in the Cidariden, Cassiduliden and Spatangoiden are usually the only pores, but which on the ventral side of *Spatangus*, *Brissus* and allies easily run together and turn into grooves of **[p. 24]** a common passage. It is evident that each ambulacral plate is originally laid down around the double pores of one foot, or, in the family of the Clypeaster, at the leaf-shaped ambulacra, forms between each two pairs of pores. Quite different is it with the pores for the very small locomotive feet of the Clypeaster and allies, which, though different from the gill-like feet of the leaf-shaped ambulacra, nevertheless continue up into the ambulacra petaloidea. These extremely fine ambulacral pores, which are best sought on the inner side of the test, are present in numbers in every ambulacral plate, arranged in a transverse row, on the back as well as on the belly. In the leaf-shaped ambulacra on the back these finer pores are situated inward from the double pores, either between the small ambulacral plates in the sutures, or, where small ambulacral plates of unequal transverse diameter alternate (*Clypeaster*, *Arachnoides*), in the broader small plates which reach the inner suture of the ambulacrum, as 2 transverse rows of pores. The double pores of the Clypeaster, destined for the gill-like feet of the ambulacra petaloidea, lie between the ambulacral plates; the fine pores for the locomotive feet lie in the plates of the same ambulacra. Des Moulins did not know the extremely fine pores; Agassiz first saw them in *Echinarachnius* and took them for pores of respiratory tubes opening into the interior of the test. Comptes rendus de l'acad. de Paris T. XXV. p. 679. They belong, however, likewise to the ambulacral canals, just as the large double pores do. We shall return to them later. For now it may only be remarked that their number increases with the transverse growth of the ambulacral plate toward the inner side, as one becomes aware on comparing Clypeastern of different ages on the inner side of the test. Pl. III. fig. 12. 13.

When the test of the Seeigel ceases already far from the mouth and the mouth-feet sit on the buccal membrane, then both the interambulacral and the ambulacral plates end in pairs, as in *Echinus*. But if the test continues to near the mouth and the mouth-feet sit on the test itself, then the ambulacral plates run out paired, but the interambulacral unpaired, as in the Spatangoiden and Clypeastriden. At the mouth of the Clypeastriden the corona does indeed simplify, but not so much as is assumed by Agassiz; ultimately there is as a rule a circle of 15 **[p. 25]** pieces, of which 5 are interambulacral (*Clypeaster*, *Arachnoides*, *Mellita*). It is necessary to examine young specimens, yet the sutures are always recognizable on the inner side. In *Clypeaster* the first circle at the mouth contains 15 pieces, the second on the other hand only 10, since the ambulacral plates lie against one another, and this is a generic character for all species; in some species the third circle too is still completely closed by the ambulacral plates; farther on there lie between them two alternating rows of interambulacral plates. Pl. IV. fig. 1.

In *Clypeaster* the last ambulacral plates are still very large, and on them are the auricular processes for the jaw muscles, as also in *Arachnoides*; in *Mellita* the last ambulacral plates are small, the interambulacral plate is large, and here it is this which bears the auricular process, which is not double as in *Clypeaster* but simple, just as Agassiz figures it from *Laganum*.

### Arrangement of the ambulacral canals.

Most regular Seeigel, *Echinus*, *Diadema*, *Astropyga*, *Echinocidaris*, have 5 pairs of mouth-feet of the same structure as the other sucker-feet; their small ambulacral plates stand isolated in the flexible membranous continuation of the corona. Their connection with the ambulacral canal through double pores is the usual one. In *Echinocidaris* their sucker-plates are two-lobed (Delle Chiaje), as also in *Diadema*, where these mouth-feet are distinguished by their strength.

In *Cidaris*, on the other hand, both the ambulacral and the interambulacral plates continue on the flexible part of the perisome as far as the mouth with the greatest regularity in an imbricated arrangement, and thereby form a special buccal corona which no other Seeigel possesses. This formation was already noticed by Des Moulins. The ambulacral rows are longer and go as far as the mouth; in the *Cidaris tribuloides* Lam. figured in Pl. II. fig. 7 I count in each vertical double row 15 perforated pairs; the interambulacral rows are shorter and form wedges between the ambulacral rows, so that they do not reach the mouth. The hindmost plates of this wedge are double, the rest simple in *Cidaris hystrix* and the very closely related *Cidaris papillata*. In *Cidaris tribuloides* Lam. most plates of this wedge are double, so that **[p. 26]** only at the tip of the wedge do the alternating double rows change into a simple one. Pl. II. fig. 7. For the rest, these feet are entirely like the other feet of *Cidaris*.

In the regular Seeigeln of the genus *Echinus*, *Echinometra*, *Salmacis* and others all the feet, the mouth-feet included, are formed alike and are sucker-feet with a sucking disc. Not all regular Seeigel, however, have feet of only one kind, or are homoiopod according to Duvernoy's presupposition. Rather, Delle Chiaje has already stated that the dorsal feet in *Echinus neapolitanus* (*Echinocidaris aequituberculata*) are pinnate, even though the figure is poor. All *Echinocidaris*, however, show this condition. The lower feet are provided with sucker-plates (Pl. III. fig. 1), and beneath the sucker-plate there is a ring-shaped calcareous disc. In the dorsal part of the ambulacrum sucking disc and calcareous ring are entirely lost, the feet become laterally flattened, pointed at the end and incised on the flat sides. Pl. III. fig. 2. This arrangement, which seems to repeat itself also in *Astropyga* and *Diadema* (according to dried specimens), evidently presents the transition to the gill-like dorsal feet of the Spatangen. The *Echinocidaris*, moreover, have the ordinary skin-gills at the anterior margin of the corona like the *Echinus*. In *Colobocentrus atratus* the feet likewise change from belly to back; the sucker-plates gradually disappear and the feet assume on the back a flat, pointed shape without incisions, quite differently from *Echinometra*. These feet contain two canals separated by a partition, which bend into one another at the tip and at the base are connected each with one of the double pores. Hereby the significance of the double pores of the feet in the Seeigeln is cleared up: evidently they serve the circulation of the fluid which ascends and descends in the foot in opposite currents. All the above-mentioned Seeigel have the ordinary skin-gills at the anterior margin of the corona like the *Echinus* (also *Diadema* and *Astropyga*, judging from the incisions of the tests). The *Cidaris* lack the latter; the feet of *Cidaris* are cylindrical on the ventral side of the Seeigel, truncated at the end, with sucking discs; on the back of the Seeigel conical, without incisions (1).

The brush-shaped cirrus-feet around the mouth in the 5 ambulacra behave alike in all the Spatangoiden examined by me, and all possess subanal cirri as well. *Brissopsis* has on each side a subanal row of 6 feet with cirri. In *Schizaster* (*canaliferus*), where the subanal semita is lacking and a semita posterior branches off from the semita peripetala, forming its arc below the anus, there are on each side 7 cirrus-feet in a longitudinal row at a considerable distance from the anus, **[p. 30]** at the hind part of the ventral side of the test, not between semita posterior and anus, but still in front of the semita posterior. The stems of the various feet, moreover, contain transverse jagged calcareous ridges in their walls; the gill-feet do not.

In the Clypeastriden the greatest deviations occur in the formation of the ambulacra. The feet of the double pores of the ambulacra petaloidea are gill-like; they are broad, low, hollow ridges cut into small lobes, between each two pores; they are swelled by the large ampullae of the ambulacra. The locomotive feet, on the other hand, are cylindrical and exceedingly fine, so that their transverse diameter in the Clypeastern amounts to about 1/20''', in *Mellita* to only 1/40'''. They are extremely numerous, far more numerous than in the regular Seeigeln, where their number as a rule reaches about 2000 in all, and in the Spatangen, where there are only a few hundred; in the Clypeastern the number of locomotive feet can without danger of exaggeration be estimated at several myriads. They are provided at the end with a sucker-plate, which is either strengthened by a jagged calcareous ring, as in the Clypeastern and allies, or at least contains a few calcareous figures, as in *Mellita*, where at the base of the sucker there regularly lie opposite one another 2 balance-beam-shaped calcareous ridges with 2 longer and one shorter limb (*Mellita hexapora* Ag.). The locomotive feet extend from the ventral side onto the dorsal side to a greater or lesser extent. Their distribution on the ventral side is very various. The Clypeastriden fall accordingly into 2 divisions. In the one the feet are not distributed over the whole ambulacral plates, but occupy discrete branched streets on which the pores are crowded together, the pore-fasciae. These fasciae begin simple, then divide into a fork or a trident (*Echinarachnius*), and the main arms of the street mostly branch further into side branches. Agassiz has already remarked that these branches pass over onto the interambulacral plates as well. To this division belong the genera *Rotula*, *Mellita*, *Encope*, *Lobophora*, *Scutella*, *Echinarachnius*. In the other division of the Clypeastriden, comprising the genera *Clypeaster*, *Laganum*, *Arachnoides*, *Moulinia*, *Scutellina*, *Echinocyamus*, *Fibularia*, the pore-streets are entirely lacking. Some, it is true, have a simple groove in the midline of the ambulacra, like *Arachnoides*, but it is known that it is without pores. **[p. 31]** The distribution of the pores and feet in these genera had hitherto remained unknown, but can be established with certainty on the larger forms. The pores and feet are distributed over the whole surface of the ambulacral plates and pass over, in *Clypeaster*, not in *Arachnoides*, onto a good portion of the interambulacral plates as well. These, then, are Clypeastriden with pore-fields, in contrast to the Clypeastriden with pore-streets or pore-fasciae. The distinction into Clypeastriden with simple and with branched grooves does not hit the point. Some genera with pore-fields do not even have the middle of the pore-fields depressed, like *Echinocyamus* and *Fibularia*. In *Laganum*, however, the depression is lost half-way.

On the dorsal side of the Clypeastriden one must distinguish in the ambulacra petaloidea the outer field between each two rows of double pores, and the inner field. The former is in all of them beset with the ambulacral gills of the large double pores, the latter in several genera, *Clypeaster*, *Arachnoides*, *Echinarachnius*, with the extremely small locomotive feet (besides pedicellariae and spines). Where the ambulacra petaloidea cease, the inner pore-field spreads out toward the periphery as far as the margin, and in *Clypeaster* again onto a part of the interambulacral plates as well, whereas in *Arachnoides* it is excluded from these. The pores of the locomotive feet are most easily recognized on the inner side of the test when this is bare; in the large Clypeastern one sees them also externally under strong magnification everywhere easily, and still more easily in the pore-streets of the other division. The pores of the latter are round and simple. In the Clypeastern, where the inner openings of the locomotive pores are likewise simple, the outer openings of the same are elongate, mostly 8-shaped, and not seldom divided into 2 discrete pores. In *Clypeaster rosaceus* these pores have 1/30'''-1/25''' length and 1/60''' breadth.

On the inner field of the ambulacra petaloidea the locomotive pores stand so that their long diameter is directed radially, and so also up toward the peripheral margin; those standing sideways on the peripheral part of the test, however, are placed rather obliquely, and stand ever more obliquely the farther outward; the direction of the pore axis is, namely, from the periphery upward and outward. Pl. IV. fig. 5. On the ventral side of the *Clypeaster* the similarly shaped elongate pores are all directed obliquely, **[p. 32]** namely, instead of in the direction from the oral centre to the periphery, rather in directions which deviate from the midline of the ambulacrum, i.e. if one imagines the long axis of each individual locomotive pore prolonged, this line strikes the midline of the ambulacrum obliquely and forms with it an acute angle open toward the periphery. Only toward the margin does the middle part of the band become more straight. Pl. IV. fig. 4. The lines of direction for the axes of the locomotive pores on the back are the continuation of the same lines of direction of the belly. What is here called lines of direction for the axes of the pores must not be confused with lines on which the pores would be ranged, for in the Clypeastern these do not stand in rows on the outside, but scattered and without order. In *Arachnoides*, on the other hand, the pores are ordered, as internally in transverse, so externally in oblique parallel rows, whose parallelism continues from the ventral side onto the dorsal side and through the whole ambulacra petaloidea.

According to Agassiz, in *Echinarachnius parma*, which he examined alive, the locomotive feet would be replaced by tentacles which, without connection with the ambulacral vessels, open through their ampullae into the body cavity. The pores for these tubes are situated in the leaf-shaped ambulacra inward from the large pores, in regular rows; through these tubes the water is supposed to have an access to the interior of the test. Comptes rendus de l'acad. d. sc. de Paris T. XXV. p. 679. One sees from this that Agassiz is the first who saw those fine pores and their ampullae, but that the true locomotive feet, which are fed by the ambulacral vessels, are confused by him with respiratory tentacles. There are no more in these than in other Seeigeln small tubes which, perforating the test, open into the interior of the body cavity. The ampullae mentioned hang rather on the ambulacral vessels themselves, at their branches to the locomotive feet; they are the same ampullae which also outside the ambulacra petaloidea and on the ventral side hang on the branches of the ambulacral vessels, one corresponding to each locomotive pore.

This condition appears to be widely distributed in the Clypeastriden; it is found, as in *Echinarachnius parma*, of which I owe specimens to Prof. Eschricht, in *Clypeaster* and *Arachnoides*, as is evident on the **[p. 33]** specimens of *Clypeaster placunarius* collected by Messrs. Ehrenberg and Hemprich, and in like manner in *Clypeaster Rangianus* and on the specimens of *Arachnoides placenta* brought back from Mergui by Dr. Th. Philippi.

Let us first examine the inner field of the ambulacra petaloidea. The whole field from one marginal double row of large pores to the opposite double row of the leaf is beset with small cylindrical locomotive feet, which possess the same size and structure as the feet of the peripheral dorsal pore-fields and as the feet of the ventral pore-fields. The examination of the interior of the test then gives the further information. The meridional ambulacral canal furnishes, in *Clypeaster Rangianus*, *Cl. placunarius*, *Arachnoides placenta*, *Echinarachnius parma*, the ambulacrum with as many parallel side branches as there are double pores of the ambulacral gills; each side branch then stands in connection with the inner pore and the large ampulla of the gill-like foot. But up to the inner pore there hangs on the side vessel a further row of small caecum-shaped ampullae of exactly the same form as the ampullae of the peripheral dorsal and of the ventral locomotive feet. Each of these small ampullae, of 3/10''' length and 1/30''' breadth, corresponds to a very small opening where a tiny branch of the transverse side vessel perforates the test and goes to the locomotive foot on the outer surface of the ambulacrum. In *Clypeaster Rangianus* there are at the broadest part of the ambulacrum 20 pores and ampullae in one transverse row of the half ambulacrum, in *Clypeaster rosaceus* 30, in *Clypeaster placunarius* 10, in *Arachnoides placenta* 20, in *Echinarachnius parma* 15. In *Echinarachnius parma*, whose small ambulacral plates are equal, these locomotive pores lie in the sutures; in *Clypeaster* and *Arachnoides*, where the small ambulacral plates are alternately unequal, so that the inner field of the ambulacrum is formed by the broad plates, the locomotive pores lie in the plates themselves, in such a way that either two transverse rows of pores fall to one plate (*Clypeaster Rangianus* and *placunarius* and *Arachnoides placenta*), or, as in *Clypeaster rosaceus*, 4 rows of pores to one ambulacral plate. In the former, therefore, each large ampulla of the gill-foot receives the side vessel from one row of pores and small ampullae; in **[p. 34]** the latter, 2 rows of small pores correspond to each large pore. *Clypeaster rosaceus* has, according to an approximate calculation, within each ambulacral leaf about 4000, *Cl. Rangianus* about 1200, *Cl. placunarius* about 600 of these fine locomotive pores. The openings of these pores on the outer surface of the test lie in *Echinarachnius parma* also in the sutures, and hence in regular transverse rows, which is made possible by the fact that the tubercles on the test of this Seeigel are extremely small. In the Clypeastern with large tubercles, on the other hand, the row-formation of the pores on the outer surface of the test is for that very reason disturbed, and the openings are rather scattered without rows between the tubercles.

Already in *Clypeaster placunarius* small calcareous points rise up on the inner surface of the test on each ambulacral plate between the rows of pores; in *Cl. Rangianus* these points, which are also numerous on the rest of the inner surface of the test, stand on ramparts into which the ambulacral plates swell between 2 rows of pores. In *Clypeaster rosaceus* the points are lacking; on the other hand the ramparts of the ambulacral plates are raised into septa, and the septa are in turn connected by a calcareous roof in which the sutures of the ambulacral plates are repeated. The leaf-shaped ambulacra therefore have double walls (like the dome of St. Peter's in Rome); the inner wall, however, belongs only to the locomotive field, not to the field of the large or gill pores, whose ampullae are uncovered. Between the double walls, on the other hand, in this Seeigel there are regular ambulacral chambers between the septa. Each of these chambers contains 4 rows of locomotive pores. But all the transverse chambers are cut through by a longitudinal passage, open toward the transverse chambers, for the median ambulacral vessel. In the roof there are three longitudinal rows of holes, by which the ambulacral galleries are connected with the body cavity. The middle row of gaps belongs to the median gallery, the lateral ones to the transverse chambers; the lateral holes are destined for the exit of the ambulacral vessels to the large ampullae of the ambulacral gills. Since each chamber opens out in the neighbourhood of two double pores, the number of gallery holes at the sides is exactly half that of the large double pores.

On the peripheral part of the dorsal ambulacra as far as the margin, and on the whole ventral part of the ambulacra, the ambulacral vessels of **[p. 35]** *Clypeaster*, *Arachnoides* and *Echinarachnius* everywhere give off pinnate branches to transverse rows of pores, and these are again provided with just as many ampullae. In *Clypeaster Rangianus* one counts from the mouth to the peripheral margin about 100, on the back from the margin to the leaf-shaped ambulacrum about 40 transverse rows of pores; their number in one transverse row increases from the mouth to the margin and decreases again from the margin to the leaf-shaped ambulacrum; toward the margin there stand about 80-90 pores in one transverse row of the half ambulacrum.

The distribution of the ambulacral vessels to the pores lies now free, now concealed in ambulacral chambers. In *Clypeaster scutiformis* the rows of pores are uncovered throughout the ambulacral field, but the ventral and dorsal parts of the test are connected by many needle-shaped pillars; in *Clypeaster Rangianus* and *placunarius*, *Arachnoides placenta*, the rows of pores lie for the most part free, but at the marginal part of the disc in chambers which are wholly or partly common to the ventral and dorsal sides, since the partitions connect the ventral part and dorsal part of the test. These transverse parallel chambers, of which there are 6 in the above-named Clypeastern, many (about 12) in *Arachnoides*, are cut through by a middle longitudinal passage which reaches from the ventral side to the dorsal side. In it runs the median ambulacral vessel, bending round from the ventral part of the test to the dorsal part, and it gives off both in the ventral and in the dorsal part of its course 2 transverse branches for each chamber, so that each marginal chamber contains 4 vessels, 2 ventral and 2 dorsal, and just as many rows of pores. *Clypeaster rosaceus*, with its bulging periphery, has no marginal chambers common to belly and back; on the other hand the whole ambulacra lie between double walls, and the pinnate branches of the ambulacral vessel and their rows of pores everywhere in ambulacral chambers or passages. Between the two transverse rows of chambers there likewise leads a covered passage for the stem of the ambulacral vessel. To each ambulacral plate in this Daedalian work there fall mostly 4 chambers; but there are from the mouth to the leaf-shaped ambulacrum about 50 chambers on each side; at the outer end of the passages they have exits into the body cavity. Each chamber contains 4 rows of pores, whose number of pores at the broadest part of the ambulacrum amounts to about 80-90. The 2 × 50 chambers of the two halves of an ambulacrum may contain, from the mouth to the leaf-shaped **[p. 36]** ambulacrum, about 16,000 ambulacral-vessel pores for locomotive feet; if one adds to this the 4000 locomotive pores of the leaf-shaped ambulacrum, then *Clypeaster rosaceus* has in one whole ambulacrum about 20,000 and in all 5 ambulacra about 100,000 locomotive pores. Of these about 15 fall to a field of view of the outer surface of 1''' in diameter.

The interambulacral plates between the ambulacra petaloidea are without pores. The remaining interambulacral plates, on the other hand, are porous on both the dorsal and the ventral side, in such a way that on the back the pores occupy more the outer part of the interambulacral plates, and there still remains a pore-less field between the pore-fields of two ambulacra. The pore-less field between the pore-fields of two ambulacra amounts in *Clypeaster rosaceus* on the back to so much space that 6-10 tubercles stand on it transversely; on the belly the pore-fields approach one another so closely that only 2-3 tubercles stand between them (1).

To the Clypeastern with double walls of the ambulacra and parallel ambulacral chambers from the mouth to the apex belong, besides *Clypeaster rosaceus*, also several fossil *Clypeaster* whose sections I have examined, such as *Cl. altus* and *pyramidalis* (2), and in general the high *Clypeaster* whose peripheral part is not flattened. On the other hand **[p. 37]** the flat fossil forms, such as *Clypeaster scutellatus* M. de S. and allies, have nothing of this, but only marginal chambers, so that it will be justified to separate the forms with double walls of the ambulacra under the name *Echinanthus* Leske from the remaining *Clypeaster*.

One must therefore await with interest the examination of spirit specimens.

I now pass on to the Clypeastriden with ventral pore-streets. The inner field of the ambulacra petaloidea behaves in *Echinarachnius* as in the *Clypeaster* and *Arachnoides*, and accordingly it might therefore seem as if this condition held for the whole family of the Clypeastriden. I have, however, not been able to recognize on spirit specimens of *Mellita quinquefora* and *hexapora* and *Lobophora bifissa*, which I owe to Messrs. Krantz, Eschricht and Steenstrup, either the small ampullae on the side branches of the ambulacral vessel or feet on the leaf-shaped ambulacrum. As regards the peripheral dorsal part of the ambulacra, it is at any rate present. Not far from the margin, the ambulacral vessel of *Mellita quinquefora* branches in a pinnate manner on the unperforated anterior radius. These parallel curved branches lie in canals of the test, which here and there are connected with other sinuses of the test. On the peripheral part of the dorsal test of *Mellita* I have also convinced myself of the presence of the feet.

In *Echinarachnius parma* the ambulacral galleries are lacking, and only toward the periphery of the disc do transverse perforated partitions **[p. 38]** and beams appear between the ventral part and dorsal part of the test, hence incomplete marginal chambers.

On the ventral part of the ambulacrum of *Echinarachnius* one sees, from the mouth onward as far as the peripheral galleries, on the inner side of the test many short pinnate branches with attached ampullae. It is from these, then, that the pores of the median pore-street must be supplied. Corresponding to the places where on the outside the side branches of the pore-street depart, there run on the inside several long pinnate branches of the ambulacral vessel, beset with many ampullae. The further branching lies in the peripheral galleries.

In the genera *Mellita*, *Lobophora* and *Encope* the side branches of the ambulacral vessel at once hide themselves in a superficially lying labyrinth of fine canals of the test, which form ambulacral galleries and here and there are connected with still other, wider, deeper-lying sinuses which are open toward the body cavity.

In these genera the pore-streets, through their forking shortly after the mouth, already withdraw from the ambulacral middle, where the stem of the ambulacral vessel lies. Beneath the two main arms of the pore-streets, however, lie no branches of the ambulacral vessel imitating their course; rather, the pores of these two large pore-streets are fed by vessel branches which are directed transversely to the pore-streets, and proceed partly directly from the median ambulacral vessel stem, partly pinnately from branches of this vessel. But all these branches of the ambulacral vessels run in narrow ambulacral galleries, which here and there anastomose and with their last transverse, closely successive offshoots strike upon the lateral ambulacral streets; they then pass partly transversely, partly obliquely beneath the pores of the pore-street. From there, then, the pores receive their branchlets. In *Mellita quinquefora* I was able to follow the branches of the ambulacral vessels from the stem in the galleries as far as their last offshoots. The many side branches of the pore-streets also receive their supply in this way. But it is to be repeated that the branching of the pore-streets and the branchings of the branches of the ambulacral vessels diverge entirely from one another.

The ambulacral galleries of *Mellita*, *Lobophora* and *Encope* form a thin superficial layer of the test and are distinguished by **[p. 39]** the narrowness of the canals from the other, deeper sinuses of the test, which are usual in these genera.

That in these sinuses in *Mellita quinquefora* and other Clypeastriden caecum-shaped processes of the intestine are supposed to lie, I cannot confirm. In that species as well as in *Mellita hexapora*, in *Lobophora*, *Clypeaster* and in general all the genera examined by me, the intestine is without small caeca, and the intestine passes without any processes by both the sinuses and the openings of the ambulacral chambers, accompanied at the outer margin by a large blood vessel, as in the regular Seeigeln, and fastened to a ligament. In *Mellita* and *Lobophora* a part of the lobules of the genital organs reaches into the sinuses of the test.

On the oral margin of the test of the Clypeastriden with branched grooves there is at the beginning of each ambulacrum a small projection, which Agassiz has duly noticed in his beautiful monograph of the Scutellen, and which he regards as a tube with one or several openings for the reception of the gills. On this projection, however, stand only two small tentacle-shaped processes with rounded end, similar to the feet. These little feelers sit upon two shallow depressions of the projection; in each depression a fine pore leads obliquely into the interior of the test, similar to the ambulacral pores. In *Arachnoides placenta* the projection is lacking, but the openings are present at the margin of the test, separated from one another by the breadth of the groove. To these openings go the two foremost branches of the ambulacral vessel, whereby it is fully proved that they are ambulacral and not branchial, and that they correspond to the mouth-feet of the regular Seeigel. The gills of the *Echinus* are in all Seeigeln with ambulacra petaloidea, both the Spatangoiden and the Clypeastern, replaced by the ambulacral gills of the ambulacra petaloidea, whereas the Spatangoiden possess distinguished mouth-tentacles.

## III. Dissection of the arms of the Asteriden.

### Arrangement of the plates in the Asterien.

The comparison of a Seeigel and a Seestern was always an attractive point for speculation. De Blainville, Agassiz, Duvernoy have tried their hand at it with success, and the subject is not yet **[p. 40]** exhausted. In my view both the ambulacral plates and the interambulacral plates are laid out so differently in the two divisions that from this arises for the most part the peculiarity of a Seeigel and of a Seestern.

De Blainville, starting from the analogy of the ambulacral plates of the Seeigel and the plates at the bottom of the ambulacral furrows of the Asterien, compared the plates of the Asterien which are situated at the side of the furrows to the interambulacral plates of the Seeigel. Of lateral plates there seem to him always to be 3 rows: an upper, a second wholly lateral and a lower; the latter connects with the row of ambulacral pieces; the last two rows of lateral pieces bear movable spines, and are therefore analogous to the interambulacral plates of the Seeigel. De Blainville de l'organisation des animaux. Paris 1822. p. 213. One sees from the description that De Blainville had an *Astropecten* before him. In the genera with a more pentagonal body the number of the exoambulacral rows is much greater. For the rest, the analogy as De Blainville conceives it is well founded.

Agassiz enters into De Blainville's comparison and joins to it the correct and telling remark that, in order to establish a complete analogy, one must imagine the Seesterne swollen; then the back of the Seestern corresponds to the dorsal summit of the Seeigel; with this something essential is indeed expressed. Through this conception the radii of the Seeigel receive, as it were, an antiambulacral dorsal side, which is lacking in the Seeigeln, even the flat and interradially incised ones (*Rotula*), and in the Holothurien.

Agassiz says further that one must not imagine the upper and lower lateral plate of an arm as belonging together, as De Blainville seems to assume, but rather the upper lateral plate of one arm joined with the corresponding upper lateral plate of the next arm, and likewise the lower lateral plate with the lower one of the next arm. These interambulacral plates are those which in some Seesternen bear the large spines corresponding to the large spines of the interambulacral plates of the Seeigel. Prodrome d'une monographie des Radiaires ou Echinodermes. Mém. de la soc. d. sc. nat. de Neuchatel. T. I. 1835. p. 168. From this it becomes clear that Agassiz has in mind the two rows of peripheral marginal plates **[p. 41]** of the *Astropecten*. Marginal plates and interambulacral plates are, however, not synonymous; for the upper marginal plates of the Seesterne are no longer interambulacral at all, but dorsal; they close the margin still above the 5 ambulacra. The manner of imagining the lower marginal plates of both sides united, and again the upper marginal plates united, is quite ingenious insofar as, with the associated conception of the swelling of the Seestern, the united upper marginal plates must turn toward the back, the united lower marginal plates toward the belly, and thus out of two horizontal rows of marginal plates a vertical double row of plates arises.

In determining the interambulacral plates of the Seesterne, however, the upper marginal plates must in my judgment be entirely excluded. The lower marginal plates are indeed interambulacral, but there is at least one further row of interambulacral plates nearer the ambulacra, which latter is also designated by De Blainville, and in the pentagonal forms *Astrogonium*, *Goniodiscus* and others still several rows of interambulacral plates between the ambulacra and the marginal plates. Since, further, the corresponding rows of plates of two arms themselves already meet in the angle and pass into one another, and the various rows of these plates between the arms form angles lying one behind another, it is really impracticable to lay the limbs of one of these angles or arcs against one another in the form of the two contiguous rows of the Seeigel without dividing up these angles and arcs. At this point it shows itself clearly enough that in the star we have to do with something other than the Seeigel. It lies, for one thing, in the shape of the Seeigel that the two rows of interambulacral plates are united in the midline of the interradius along their whole length, and it lies in the nature of the stars that the corresponding rows of plates of two arms are connected by the facing ends of the rows in the middle of the interradius and pass into one another festoon-like.

Agassiz himself later recognized the difficulty, Catal. raisonné des Echinides, ann. des sc. nat. T. VI. 1846. p. 311, where he says: there remains some uncertainty about the relations of the two rows of interambulacral plates which in the Seestern border each side of an ambulacrum, and there is still a difficulty to be solved.

**[p. 42]**

We must further distinguish between the various interambulacral plates of the Asterien. See Pl. II. fig. 10. 11. Those which sit upon the outer processes of the ambulacral plates (a) and form the margins of the furrow have something of their own as border plates of the ambulacra or adambulacral plates (b); they always come together in the interradial mouth-angle in such a way that the angle consists of 2 adambulacral plates. The border plates usually agree exactly with the number of the ambulacral plates. The second kind of interambulacral plates is formed by the more or less distinguished lower marginal plates, marginal interambulacral plates (c) at the peripheral margin; only the lower marginal plates belong here. Between the adambulacral and marginal plates lies a more or less developed triangular field of intermediate interambulacral plates (d). In some long-armed forms this field is extremely small, and in *Astropecten* reduced to a few easily overlooked plates behind the mouth-angle; in the pentagonal forms the field is very large. The form and size of these plates is often, as in the Astrogonien, different both from the border plates and from the marginal plates; the size of the intermediate plates decreases in *Astrogonium* both toward the peripheral margin and toward the arm ends. On the triangular field one can distinguish rows which can be conceived either as arcs between the arms or else as bands from the furrow to the peripheral margin. In the latter case the number of plates in an oblique row becomes smaller the nearer to the arm end; the longest of the oblique rows lie against one another in the middle of the interradius, and there then remain some more, in the example used, of *Astrogonium cuspidatum*, 4 behind the mouth-angle at the tip of the wedge, of which one forms the tip, two lie paired, and a fourth or even fifth behaves as the middle one. If one conceives the rows of intermediate interambulacral plates as arcs from one arm to the other, then the longest arcuate rows are those nearest the border plates, the shortest those nearest the marginal plates. Behind the mouth-angle there again lie some (3) outside the arcs.

The *Astropecten* have, besides those already described, yet another special row of interambulacral plates, which is visible only on the inner side of the test. In *Astropecten*, namely, two different rows attach at once to the ambulacral plates of the furrows, of which the outer are the border plates of the furrows, while the inner row, visible only in the body cavity, connects the ambulacral plates with the lower marginal plates.

**[p. 43]**

The marginal plates and border plates, like the ambulacral plates, extend to the outermost end of the arms, i.e. to the unpaired terminal plate, so that between this end plate and those rows of plates the newly arising small plates for the ambulacra and interambulacra are formed, while toward the antiambulacral or dorsal side an increase likewise takes place here at the end of the arm. The intermediate rows (between the marginal plates and the border plates), which do not reach the end of the arm, abut with their ends against the border plates, and the rows reach the border or the border plates the earlier, the more distant the row is from the arm furrow. The multiplication of these plates cannot take place at the end of the arm; rather, if they multiply successively, it can only happen at the outermost ends of these intermediate rows. On the dorsal side of a pentagonal star, too, when there are many rows of plates, as in *Asteriscus*, not all rows of plates reach the end of the arm, but only the middle ones, while the rest reach only the border, and their multiplication must therefore in this case take place both at the end and at the borders of the arm. Nothing is so variable as the number of interambulacral rows of plates of the Asterien.

If the border is developed, the border plates are either single or double, ventral and dorsal border plates, which then both extend to the unpaired terminal plate of the arm. In the Seesterne [starfish] with a developed border, only the antiambulacral or dorsal field is perforated for the respiratory blind tubules, which field is then bounded by the single or double border plates. If the arms are rounded and the border is lacking, as in *Echinaster*, *Ophidiaster*, *Scytaster*, then the extent of the dorsal pore field and the development of the ventral rows of plates are extremely varied; sometimes there are many rows of plates from the ambulacral furrow to the point where the pore fields begin, as in *Ophidiaster*, sometimes few, down to 2 rows of plates, in *Scytaster* and *Echinaster*, so that the pore fields then spread from the back onto the sides of the arms and up to near the ambulacral furrows.

**[p. 44]**

The way in which I conceive the transformation of a Seeigel into an Asterie is this. The first step toward it would be that the apical part of the sphere expands between the ends of the 5 ambulacra and the interambulacral fields change their shape. In the Seeigel these fields are meridional segments of a sphere; in the Seestern they are triangles, fields which increase in breadth from the mouth to the end of the interambulacral field. We now have a round, disc-shaped Seestern, roughly like the disc of an *Ophiura* (imagined without arms). While retaining the contiguous interambulacral rows of plates of the Seeigel we could indeed give the round disc the form of the pentagon with incisions, but stars with such an arrangement of plates do not exist. If the arms are to become free, then the connection of the two rows of interambulacral plates must be given up, and indeed already in the pentagonal star, and the two rows must separate from one another in a fork-like manner; but there are also no stars which would have only one row of interambulacral plates on each side of the arm; rather, in the open angle of the two divided rows of plates still other rows insert themselves, which arrange themselves according to the first row, in that they form, from the end of one ambulacrum to the end of the next, arcs of varying curvature whose apices are directed toward the mouth angles; the outermost row becomes the lower border plates.

Should anyone wish to regard what we call marginal plates in the Asterien as alone the analogue of the 2 rows of interambulacral plates of the Seeigel, and to imagine that the remaining interambulacral plates of the Asterien had intruded like a wedge between the two rows of the interambulacrum of the Seeigel, then the behaviour of the interambulacral plates of *Palaechinus*, *Archaeocidaris* and *Perischodomus* (1) stands against this, where there are already more than 2 rows of interambulacral plates, but all nevertheless laid out meridionally according to the same type as the Seeigel. To wish to regard, in these oldest Seeigel, the outermost rows as genuine and the inner ones as spurious interambulacral plates would make no proper sense. We must in any case distinguish 2 types: the meridional type of two or more contiguous rows of plates in the Seeigel, and the fork-rowed type of several rows in the Seesterne.

The mouth angles of the Asteriden are interambulacral; in the Asterien they consist of the first marginal plates of two ambulacra, laid against one another, which in turn rest upon the corresponding ambulacral plates of two ambulacra. In the Ophiuren the mouth angles likewise contain 2 ambulacral and 2 interambulacral bony pieces. From this it follows that the interambulacral part of the mouth angle of the Ophiuren corresponds to the first marginal plates or adambulacral plates of the Asterien. Behind the mouth angles of the Ophiuren there lie externally, constantly, 3 shields, of which the middle one is large, the so-called mouth shield used in the description of species of the Ophiuren, and the lateral ones are smaller. They evidently correspond to the intermediate plates of the Asterien (1).

Of particular interest in the comparison of the Seeigel and Asterien are the 5 plates of the apex of the Seeigel, which on account of their position between the genital plates have been called intergenital plates, a long-customary designation, in place of which, however, Agassiz has recently chosen the name ocellar plates, which I scarcely venture to use because it is all too theoretical. Each of these plates stands at the end of an ambulacrum without itself being an ambulacral plate; it is perforated, and in the opening the little eye-knob discovered by Forbes has its seat. This, confirmed by Agassiz and Valentin, and also seen by myself (*Cidaris*), is the analogue of the coloured eye-spot of Ehrenberg at the end of the arms of the Asterien. In both cases the nerve trunk of the radius runs out into this little knob, and in the Seeigel it passes thither from within through the opening of the plate. Agassiz rightly lays great weight on this analogy and attributes to the Asterien too an ocellar plate at the end of the ambulacrum, between which and the ambulacrum the new ambulacral plates form, as in the Seeigel. Here it is also the new interambulacral plates that are formed at the tip of the arm; but this can only hold for those rows which reach the end of the arm. As already mentioned, many rows of plates do not reach the end of the arms, as in *Ophidiaster*, *Asteriscus*, *Astrogonium*. The plate in question **[p. 46]** is said to have the same relations in the Asterien and Seeigel, and the eye is said to have its seat in it in the Asterien too. Ann. des sc. nat. T. VI. 1846. p. 309. 311. Underlying this is the presupposition that the nerve of the radius in the Asterien too has an internal course beneath the ambulacral plates, as in the Seeigel, which however is the case only in the latter, not in the Asterien. Nor is there any opening in the unpaired plate which lies at the end of the ambulacrum and at the beginning of the back of the arm. The analogy of the plate in the Seeigel and Asterien nevertheless seems to be perfectly well founded. It is in all cases the end plate, the outermost part of the radius. In those Asterien which have developed the peripheral border into large border plates, it is the border plates, namely the upper border plates, which have the same form as this apical plate of the radius, form the same row with it, and the border plates are as it were repetitions of the apical plate of the radius, which connect the radii in a festoon-like manner and separate the ventral side or interambulacral side from the dorsal side or antiambulacral side. The terminal plate is smooth where these border plates are smooth (*Astrogonium*); otherwise it is covered with granules where all interambulacral and antiambulacral plates are granulated (*Scytaster*, *Ophidiaster*). As the end piece of the arm this plate, moreover, has its relations toward the antiambulacral side as well as the ambulacral and interambulacral side, and it holds for the antiambulacral side too that in its neighbourhood new small plates are formed, for the lengthening of those rows of plates which reach the end of the arm.

### Ambulacra of the Asterien.

In the Asterien the right and left ambulacral plates are movably joined together in the middle of the arm furrows, in such a way that they form a kind of joint by means of interlocking teeth; below and above the toothed junction lie transverse muscles which widen and narrow the ambulacrum. Anat. Studien über d. Echinod. p. 118. Through the interconnected apophyses of the ambulacral plates, the middle part of the ambulacrum, viewed from the body cavity, has the form of a series of vertebrae with lateral processes.

The ambulacral canal of the arm, arising from the ring canal of the water-vascular system, lies on the bony column of the ray at the deepest point in the groove of this column. Above it lie the outer transverse muscles, which are able to move the halves of the vertebra-like pieces against one another and to narrow the ambulacra. Dilatations of the vessel push themselves **[p. 48]** in between each 2 transverse muscles; here the branch to the sucker-foot arises, running transversely. The sucker-foot is set upon the hole between the lateral processes of the vertebra-like pieces and extends through this hole into the ampulla lying inside beneath the ambulacral skeleton. The ampulla is simple, whereas in the Seeigel the ambulacral vessel forms a plexus in the interior of the ampulla.

With the restriction of the ambulacra to the ventral side, all differences among the feet fall away in the Asterien. The feet of the Asterien are always locomotive and either conical and designed for the retraction of their ends, as in all Asterien without an anus, *Astropecten*, *Luidia*, *Ctenodiscus*, or cylindrical, provided with a sucking disc without a calcareous disc, in all genera of Asterien provided with an anus. The distinction of *Astropecten* and *Archaster* is accordingly easy; on the other hand it is often difficult to recognize the anus at once, which however presents no difficulty if in *Archaster* one removes the outer crown from the paxillae in the middle of the Seestern, whereby the anus is concealed in the way that the ground is darkened by the foliage of closely standing trees. *Astropecten Parelii* v. D. et K. (Kongl. Vet. Acad. Handl. f. 1844. p. 247. Tab. VII. Fig. 14-16) has recently furnished an example of the sharpness of this distinction. Mr. Sars had informed me that the rule given by me with regard to the feet suffers an exception in this *Astropecten*; I therefore suspected that this Seestern would be not an *Astropecten* but an *Archaster*, of which the European seas hitherto possessed no example. Mr. Sars has since sent me a specimen in spirit, in which I at once found the anus when I freed the paxillae in the middle of the back of their crown until the skin of the back became visible. That Seestern will therefore henceforth have to be called *Archaster Parelii*.

The nerves of the ambulacra have hitherto been misunderstood. Agassiz has expressed doubts as to whether the nerves of the Asterien described by Tiedemann are really nerves, because the nerves of the Echiniden run on the inner side of the shell, whereas the nerves of the Asterien, covered only by the outer skin, pass over the skeleton of the ambulacrum. That this reason is not valid follows already from the position of the ambulacral canal. Agassiz could easily have convinced himself **[p. 49]** that on the inner side of the shell or of the ambulacrum in the Asterien there lies nothing whatever of a cord, neither the nerve nor the ambulacral canal. Nevertheless I cannot regard what Tiedemann took for nerves as such, and have substantiated this opinion in the anatomical studies. The nerve trunks of the Echinodermen are not such thin threads as Tiedemann figures, but band-like and broad. What the same author calls the nerve ring at the outer edge of the ring-shaped blood vessel has never become intelligible to me even in the largest and best-preserved specimens of *Astropecten aurantiacus* and other Asterien, and nothing can be separated there from the vessel which does not belong to the blood vessel. On the other hand the ring beneath the angles at the oral disc, which Tiedemann calls the orange-coloured vascular ring, is not a vessel, and this is rather the nerve ring, which sends off into the ambulacral grooves 5 flat, broad continuations, soft like the ring itself, running to the end of the ambulacra, immediately covered by the skin of the ambulacra. Between the soft lamina, consisting for the most part of longitudinal fibres, which, just like the nerves of the Seeigel, can easily be split and torn lengthwise, and the ambulacrum, there is a thin but fibrous ridge, like a septum: this is presumably Tiedemann's nerve. The nerve trunks of the 5 arms divide at the beginning of the ambulacrum into 2 limbs, of which each forms with the limb coming to meet it from the next ambulacrum an arc convex toward the mouth, exactly as in *Echinus*.

In the Ophiuren the lateral halves of the vertebra-like ambulacral bones are immovably joined by suture; the ambulacral canal and the nerve of the arm again lie externally above these parts in a groove of the vertebral column and are covered by the ventral shields of the arms.

The structure of the ambulacra is therefore entirely different in the Seeigel and Asteriden, for in the Seeigel both structures lie in the interior of the shell beneath the ambulacral plates. I had indicated the difference of the two series in the treatise on the *Pentacrinus* and in the System of the Asteriden, and remarked that only the Asteriden have an internal skeleton. Both Agassiz and Duvernoy have disputed the difference emphasized by me, the former in the introduction to the Catalogue raisonné des Echinides, Ann. des sciences nat. T. VI. 1846. p. 309, the latter in his **[p. 50]** Mémoire sur l'analogie de composition et sur quelques points de l'organisation des echinodermes. Paris 1848. Extrait du T. XX. des mémoires de l'académie des sciences. Their objections proceed from an analogy that is in general correct, from the identity of the ambulacral plates, from the same position of the ampullae beneath, and of the sucker-feet above, their pores; nevertheless the difference cannot be denied, and Duvernoy himself in part admits it again; but neither of them has given us any means of explaining the contradiction in the facts and of solving the problem. I must concede that the vertebral columns of the Asterien are not separate bony pieces, but belong to the ambulacral plates. Even in this case the difference of position remains no less striking. At the outer part of these plates, where in the Asterien and Seeigel the passage from the ampullae to the feet is, agreement; at the inner part, where the plates join, in Asterien and Seeigel complete difference in a twofold respect. One must therefore conclude that the same ambulacral plates in the Asterien and Seeigel can send out entirely different processes for their union. Either these plates develop processes which meet one another above the nerve cord and water vessel of the radius, and that is the case in the Echiniden, or the same plates develop processes which meet one another beneath the nerve cord and water vessel, while above these parts the union fails to occur and they are rather only covered by soft parts (Asterien), or are even overlain by special unpaired covering shields (Ophiuren). If we imagine that the ambulacral plates of *Echinus* move apart and are united by skin, but grow round the ambulacral vessel and the nerve from below, then we obtain from *Echinus* the Asterie; if we imagine an Asterie in which the membranous structures above the nerves and vessels ossify from the ambulacral plates outward, and let the suture of the vertebra-like processes beneath the vessels gape widely, then we obtain from the Asterie the conditions of the Echinen. A third case would be that the ambulacral plates at the same time achieve the union of the Echinen above the nerve and vessel and develop the processes of the Asterien. This in fact occurs in *Cidaris* at the anterior part of the ambulacra, where the ambulacral plates on the inner side of the pore rows send off processes perpendicularly inward **[p. 51]** toward the cavity of the shell, which take the trunks of the ambulacral structures between them from both sides. Pl. II. fig. 8. 9. 6. Outward lie the ampullae. Several of these processes join with their knobbed ends into a continuous colonnade, while between the bases of the processes intervertebral passages, holes, remain for the branches of the ambulacral vessel which go to their ampullae and to the pores of the shell. A union of the vertebral processes from right and left does not occur. On the other hand, the analogy of the auricular processes at the anterior edge of the corona of the Seeigel with the vertebral processes of the Asterien, to which attention was drawn in the anatomical studies on the Echinodermen, Archiv f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1850, is more apparent than apt for all cases. It is true that the auricles in most Seeigel are processes of the ambulacral plates, and the trunks of the ambulacral structures pass through them; but in *Cidaris* it is exceptionally the interambulacral plates which furnish the auricular processes for the muscles of the jaws.

Besides *Cidaris*, *Clypeaster rosaceus* and *altus*, or the genus *Echinanthus* in general, also has that part of the ambulacral plates which is analogous to the vertebral processes of the Asteriden in the inner lamina of its ambulacral plates. Here all the ambulacral plates take part in it, and even the union of right and left by suture occurs. This ambulacral floor lies, as in the Asteriden, beneath the trunks of the ambulacral vessels and nerves. On the other hand the outer lamina of the ambulacral plates above the nerve trunk and vascular trunk is analogous to the membranous covering of the ambulacra of the Asteriden. With this it is now sufficiently proved that the structure of the ambulacra in the Echiniden and Asteriden does in fact differ entirely, and *Cidaris* and *Echinanthus* may be regarded as the key to the understanding of these differences.

### Structure of the arms of the Ophiuren.

In the developmental history, namely in the larval forms, the Asterien and Ophiuren are not related at all; rather, the Pluteus of the Ophiuren is, among all the larvae of the Echinodermen, comparable in the first instance only to the Pluteus of the Seeigel, both in shape and in the possession of the calcareous rods. After the development of the radial form, however, the Ophiure resembles in the most essential **[p. 52]** respects only the Asterien, and quite particularly in the circumstance that its ambulacral plates, although no ambulacral furrow is present and the ambulacral plates are covered by special ventral shields of the radii, nevertheless have the same relation to the nerve and ambulacral canal as in the Asterien.

The Ophiuren now depart yet one step further than the Asterien from the Seeigel. In order to establish the peculiarities of the Ophiuren as against the Asterien, we must return again to the structure of the ambulacra of the Asterien. The widening and narrowing of the ambulacra is something peculiar to the Asterien; only they possess such transverse muscles, not the Ophiuren. The connection of the lateral halves of the vertebral processes is therefore in the Asterien not a true suture, but a toothed articulation, in which rows of small rounded teeth and depressions interlock, as at the hinge of certain bivalves. See Pl. II. fig. 12.

The Ophiuren, on the other hand, have a completely firm suture between the two half vertebral pieces; the muscles of their arms connect the vertebrae in the longitudinal direction and give them the ability to bend, to extend, but quite especially the movement of abduction and adduction in the horizontal plane of the animal. In the Asterien the bendings of the arms take place by muscles between the ambulacral vertebrae, between the adambulacral plates, and between the ambulacral and adambulacral plates.

How the radii of the Ophiuren are constructed has already been provisionally set out in the introduction to the Adriatic larvae of the Ophiuren, so far as was necessary for the understanding of the metamorphosis. The vertebrae fill the arms almost completely, but continue ventrally on the disc and therefore belong to the ventral perisome, as in the Asterien; they are nothing other than the here entirely concealed ambulacral plates of the Asterien. For the ambulacral canal, and above it the nerve, lie in a groove on the ventral side of these vertebrae, but externally the ventral side is further covered by a bony shield. With the sides of the vertebrae the spine-bearing shields are firmly connected by suture, which appear to belong to the category of the interambulacral plates or exoradial plates. The lateral shields meet from right and left in a point in the upper and lower midline. Between the rings formed by them lie, on the **[p. 53]** back of the arms, the dorsal shields, on the belly the ventral shields; on the sides of the arms the covering between the lateral shields consists of soft skin; this covers the musculature of the joints. Between the lateral shields, the dorsal shield and the vertebral column lies the extremely narrowed cavity of the radius, which on the dorsal side widens somewhat into a canal. The vertebrae articulate with one another by joints in the middle of the anterior and posterior surface; the lateral parts of these surfaces are designed for the insertion of the upper and lower intervertebral muscles. The lateral branches of the nerve trunk for the tentacles pass in a transverse groove which is located on the upper side of the vertebral piece and covered by the lateral shield. The lateral branches of the tentacle canal, too, do not go off between each two vertebrae, but pierce the sides of the vertebrae themselves from the covered groove of the vertebral column, and emerge at the aboral edge of the vertebra, still in front of the lateral shield attached on the circumference of the vertebra, in a small excavation of the bone on which the tentacle sits. This behaviour is very peculiar, and it deserves to be noted that holes piercing the vertebrae as ambulacral plates perpendicularly, comparable to the ambulacral pores of the Asterien and Seeigel, are entirely absent, just as the ampullae too are lacking.

From this it can be seen how far the ambulacral plates of the Seeigel can change their form and function through the Asterien down to the Ophiuren.

The unpaired shields on the ventral side of the radius are in any case peculiar; they cannot be explained from the general plan of the Echinodermen and are therefore to be regarded as an unpaired interpolation, peculiar to the Ophiuren, above the united ambulacral bones. They are superambulacral. That they are not to be regarded as a fusion of the adambulacral plates of the Asterien, as Meckel believed, is proved by the fact that the adambulacral plates of the Asterien pass into the mouth angles, and that the same pieces are still contained in the mouth angles of the Ophiuren. The lateral shields of the arms of the Ophiuren accompany the arms also on the ventral side of the disc toward the mouth; they are not border plates, for which Meckel took them, for as such they would have to pass onto the border of the disc. But instead of bounding furrows, as do the adambulacral plates **[p. 54]** of the Asterien, they reach over the ventral and dorsal side of the arms as far as the middle.

The *Astrophyton* have the vertebra-like pieces in the radii; instead of the mere shields, however, the arm is surrounded all round only by the leathery skin, and on this skin the spines are seated, which however have rudiments of lateral shields beneath them. The membranous sheath, enclosing the ambulacral vertebrae more closely on the arm, widens on the disc into the visceral cavity, in that the ambulacral vertebrae continue beneath the ventral skin of the disc. If the arm of an *Astrophyton* is reduced to that of an Asterie, one must imagine the soft skin of the furrows of an Asterie raised and swollen until it is at the same level as the adambulacral plates; then the ambulacral plates would lie in the interior of a tubular sheath and be attached to the ventral part of it by the adambulacral plates. The skin of the arms of *Astrophyton* represents such a sheath. In the Ophiuren this sheath is divided into shields, the lateral shields, the ventral and dorsal shields, and the connection of the sheath with the ambulacral vertebrae takes place by the lateral shields. At the beginning of the arms of *Astrophyton* one recognizes in the dried skin, besides the lateral shields, also the traces of the ventral shields, and on the disc at the sides of the arms, outward from the lateral shields, a train of small plates which correspond to the intermediate plates of the Asterien. In the angle between the arms these trains unite, at the place where in the Ophiuren the mouth shields lie. From this comparison it seems to follow that the lateral shields of the Ophiuren arise from a metamorphosis and enlargement of the adambulacral plates of the Asterien. Accordingly the spines of the Ophiuriden are also adambulacral, not marginal spines.

If one attempts to reduce the relations of an Ophiure to those of the Seeigel (apart from the different position of the vessels and nerves), then the radius of the Seeigel would become similar to the radius of the former if the interambulacral part of the shell of the Seeigel were prolonged into processes over the ambulacral plates and the remaining gaps over the ambulacra were filled by special covering plates. So far do the radii of the Asteriden and Echiniden diverge from one another.

## IV. Calyx and arms of the Crinoiden.

Nature has not set up a transition from the Seeigel to the Seestern, which would be a flat Seeigel whose ventral side was ambulacral, whose entire dorsal side was antiambulacral, and whose interambulacral plates consisted of the simple double rows of the Seeigel. The only approach to this form is the pentagon of the pentagonal species of Seesterne, whose interambulacral plates always form a triangular cluster, of which only the plates at the edge of the ambulacra arrange themselves in conformity with these. Just as little is there a transition from the Seeigel to the Crinoiden. In the Blastoiden the apex has extended into the antiambulacral field of the calyx. In the composition of the interambulacral fields of the calyx, however, they depart just as much from the Seeigel as the Asterien do from these; these fields are formed in the Blastoiden partly by the 5 radialia standing in the direction of the radii, partly by the interambulacral unpaired deltoid pieces, relations which permit no comparison with the interambulacral fields of the Seeigel. The composition of the ambulacra, too, is divergent in *Pentremites*, both from the ambulacra of the Seeigel and from those of the Asterien, as appears from the analysis of the Pentremiten given by Roemer. Archiv f. Naturgesch. Jahrg. XVII. I. p. 323. On the other hand the position of the genital slits in the Pentremiten and Ophiuren is in agreement, to which I drew attention in the Monatsbericht der Akademie 1840 p. 106.

The formation of the antiambulacral side of the radii takes place in the Crinoiden either already from the base of the calyx, or from the circumference, or in the neighbourhood of the mouth, as in most Cystideen. In the last case the calyx shows, from the base up to the neighbourhood of the mouth, nothing of a radial arrangement of the plates, and this begins only at the mouth as oral arms, whose ambulacral grooves however lead to the mouth, and no less than the jointed ambulacral side of the arms make known the general plan of the Echinodermen. This explains why, in the Cystideen, so long as they were regarded as armless, the radial arrangement of the Echinoderm had to be missed.

**[p. 56]**

The distinction of the various structures which occur on the radii of the Crinoiden is not always easy; I have attempted it in what follows. Radii are radial divisions of the Crinoid for the reception of the ambulacra, and are either calyx radii or arms. Ambulacra of the calyx are grooves with suckers on the ventral side of the calyx, where there are arms, in the direction of the arms. Ambulacra of the arms are the ventral sides of the arms and of the pinnulae, provided with suckers. Calyx ambulacra without arms are possessed by the Blastoiden. Many Crinoiden, such as *Actinocrinus*, *Platycrinus* and others, have arms without calyx ambulacra, without furrows of the calyx; the Pentacrinen and their relatives have arms together with calyx ambulacra. The arms are either uniserially or biserially jointed; they are either simple or dichotomously divided. The divided arms therefore proceed from an undivided arm base, which either articulates on the calyx or is enclosed in the plating of the calyx. The arms are therefore not readily double from the origin. The pinnulae, on the other hand, always stand in double rows and are never divided or branched. They are jointed offshoots either of the calyx ambulacra (Blastoiden) or of the ambulacra of the arms. Where there are arms, they are lacking on the calyx (also on ambulacral grooves of the calyx) and begin only where the arms detach themselves from the calyx. They are either uniserially or biserially jointed, and provided on their ventral side with suckers, just like the arms and calyx ambulacra. Each simple joint of the arm or each division of the ambulacrum (Blastoiden) has for the most part only one pinnula. The pinnulae then stand alternately. Of arms without jointing of the pinnulae the *Cupressocrinus*, of pinnulae without arms *Pentremites*, furnish undoubted examples. Single-jointed pinnulae which form rows on one joint, as on the arms of *Cupressocrinus*, pass over into the nature of the marginal platelets of the ambulacra. Marginal platelets are upright platelets at the sides of the ambulacral grooves, and can occur on the ambulacral grooves of the calyx as well as of the arms and pinnulae (*Pentacrinus*). On the arms they stand so densely that several fall to one joint. *Pentacrinus* has marginal platelets and pinnulae together. Spines or bristles are unjointed appendages of the arms and occur only in the division Crinoidea costata (*Saccocoma*). They stand in pairs, namely opposite one another at each articulation of the arms, whereas here that kind of **[p. 57]** pinnulae which are jointed and alternate are lacking. Cirri are jointed offshoots on the stem of Crinoiden and on the knob of the Comatulen.

The ambulacra of the living Crinoiden are described in the treatise on the *Pentacrinus*. Abhandl. d. Akademie d. Wiss. zu Berlin a. d. J. 1841. They are grooves which continue from the mouth on the perisome of the calyx to the arms and pinnulae, lined by a soft skin and in *Pentacrinus* protected on both sides by upright calcified marginal platelets. Within the grooves there are two rows of fine openings on which the small suckers are seated. On the arms and pinnulae the calcareous formations on the ventral side are limited merely to the marginal platelets of the ambulacral grooves. On the calyx, on the other hand, the ambulacral grooves are further supported, besides the marginal platelets, by calcareous formations. Those platelets which form the edge of the ambulacral grooves have a rampart-like elevation and serve the ambulacra both as a border and as a support for the upright marginal platelets; one may call them lateral plates of the ambulacra; these, like the marginal platelets, are distinguished from the remaining ventral plates by the fact that they lack the enigmatic calyx pores peculiar to the latter. Beneath the soft lining of the groove there also lie small plates, which have already been indicated in the treatise on the *Pentacrinus*. For a comparison with the ambulacral plates of the Seeigel and Seesterne it seemed to me important to subject precisely these subambulacral small plates to further investigation. They form beneath the skin of the groove a single, hence unpaired, row and are connected with the lateral plates bounding the grooves of the ambulacra by a firm skin in which the ambulacral pores are found. Pl. VI. fig. 7. These pores mostly have their seat between the lateral and the middle row of plates. On the middle row of small plates a half-canal is excavated on their upper side, which appears to be designed for the reception of the ambulacral vessel. According to this the ambulacral vessel would, as in the Asterien, have its seat on the outer surface of the ambulacral skeleton and, as there, beneath the soft skin of the ambulacral furrow; the pores of the ambulacrum connected with the feet would perhaps be interpretable as passages to ampullae. The presence of the calcareous platelets makes an examination under the microscope impossible and permits only dissection under the lens, in **[p. 58]** which the nature of the platelets and the ambulacral pores can be observed very well, but the relation of the feet to the ambulacral vessels cannot be observed directly. The whole inner side of the calyx is loosely lined by a membrane which again contains very small microscopic calcareous platelets. For the general comparison of the ambulacra in the various orders the fact is important that *Pentacrinus* possesses both median unpaired and lateral paired plates of the ambulacra, and that the ambulacral pores are situated between the two. With this the ambulacra of the Pentremiten agree in so far as these, according to the exact analysis furnished by Roemer, possess, besides the paired plates, also a median unpaired plate, which however runs beneath the whole ambulacrum. The ambulacral vessel and its lateral branches, according to the pinnulae discovered by Roemer and Yandell, probably had their seat on these plates, not beneath them, and these ambulacra were probably covered by soft skin, as in *Pentacrinus*. The general arrangement of the ambulacral vessels follows in all Echinodermen a concordant plan, but the composition of the ambulacral skeleton and the position of the ambulacral vessels relative to it is subject in the various divisions to great variations. The ambulacral plates of the Seeigel, Asterien and Crinoiden differ essentially from one another, and just as much as does the system of the dorsal and interambulacral plating.

There are, however, in various divisions certain unpaired median pieces which, where they are present, lie on the dorsal side of the ambulacral vessel; in this series belong the subambulacral platelets of *Pentacrinus*, the large subambulacral plate of the Pentremiten, the rotulae on the jaw apparatus of the Seeigel, and those pieces of the oral skeleton of the Holothurien over which the ambulacral canals pass to the body walls.

**Cystideen.** Among the Crinoiden the Cystideen of L. v. Buch form a group which appeared to be distinguished by the fact that their reproductive organs were enclosed with the other viscera in the calyx. In the Pentacrinen and Comatulen, on the other hand, the reproductive organs are on the pinnulae of the arms; in those Crinoiden which have only one calyx opening (mouth), such as *Actinocrinus*, *Platycrinus* and others, the exclusion of the reproductive **[p. 59]** organs from the calyx is already made probable by the lack of a corresponding opening. The Cystideen, on the other hand, have at least 2, in part 3 calyx openings, of which one is distinguished by a valvular closure which, apart from the Cystideen, is quite unusual among the Crinoiden. This valvular pyramid has been determined by L. v. Buch as the genital opening. A conjecture which, to be sure, could not be proved with certainty. v. Buch in the Monatsbericht der k. Akademie d. Wiss. zu Berlin, März 1840. Abhandl. d. k. Akademie d. Wiss. zu Berlin a. d. J. 1844. It is his merit that he recognized the intimate relationship of these forms with the Crinoiden and at the same time their peculiarity, that he furnished exact analyses of their calyx and set out their genera. That these are not armless, as they were formerly generally regarded, was first noticed by A. v. Volborth; he discovered the arms in *Echinoencrinus angulosus* and *striatus*, later also in *Echinosphaerites aurantium*, in which they proceed from the mouth. Bulletin de la classe phys. math. de l'Acad. Imp. des sciences de St. Petersburg, T. III. N. 6. Verhandl. d. Kais. mineralogischen Gesellschaft zu Petersburg, J. 1845-46. Petersb. 1846. According to him, traces or remains of the destroyed arms are to be recognized in all Russian Cystideen. The figures of the Duke v. Leuchtenberg and those of Volborth of *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi* and *Protocrinites oviformis* also point, in these, to the presence of arms, although the arms themselves are not preserved. For from the mouth branched grooves pass over a large part of the calyx, but the branches of the grooves end at tubercles of the calyx, which are to be regarded as the sites of arms, which is all the more remarkable since accordingly the arms of these Cystideen must have had their seat far from the mouth. A specimen of *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi* in the collection of L. v. Buch agrees exactly with those figures. When L. v. Buch founded the Cystideen in his second treatise (1844), the arms of *Echinoencrinus* had already become known. He did not regard them as arms of the Crinoiden and called them feelers. With correct foresight he already drew *Pseudocrinites* and *Agelocrinus*, with long arms proceeding from the oral part of the calyx, into the Cystideen, but did not wish these offshoots to be interpreted as true arms. He had even, in the year 1840, called the remains of the 3 arm-like offshoots in *Hemicosmites* arms or proboscides, **[p. 60]** but was led away from the correct view by the reference to oral tubes. v. Buch's figures of the calyces of the Cystideen are very true to nature and exact; on the other hand the summit of *Hemicosmites* and *Sphaeronites aurantium*, Abhandl. d. Akad. 1844. Pl. I. fig. 11 and 21, is not correctly represented.

Forbes, in his fine monograph on the British Cystideen (Mem. geol. survey, T. II. Lond. 1848), has increased the forms with oral arms. He divides the Cystideen into 1) those with arms: *Pseudocrinites*, *Apiocystites*, *Agelocrinites*; 2) with oral pinnulae: *Prunocystites*, and 3) armless ones: *Caryocystites* and *Sphaeronites*. The British *Echinoencrinus* are also said to be armless. But all the Cystideen may well after all have been provided with arms or equivalents of arms. The arms observed by Volborth in the Russian species of *Echinoencrinus* Forbes takes for oral pinnulae. The oral arms of *Echinoencrinus* and *Prunocystites* are biserially jointed. Volborth saw them in the former beset on the ambulacral side with small platelets, which he calls tentacles, remarking that pinnulae are lacking. These platelets have the properties of the marginal platelets which in the Crinoiden (*Pentacrinus*) occur both on the arms and on the pinnulae. In *Echinoencrinus angulosus* the remains of 6 arms were present. If this number did not agree with the 5 depressions usually surrounding the mouth, this is fully explained by the fact that the number of these facets varies; v. Buch states that there are 5 or 6; I also have a specimen with 8 round depressions around the mouth, which are connected with the mouth by grooves. *Echinoencrinus striatus* has, according to Volborth, with a much narrower and more pointed oral end of the calyx, only 2 much stronger opposed arms at the mouth, which are constructed just as in *Echinoencrinus angulosus*. From this behaviour, however, it becomes probable that these are not pinnulae but arms, for it is not the manner of pinnulae to stand singly. If both are supposed to belong to a single ambulacrum, how is one to imagine a single ambulacrum at this place in the immediate neighbourhood of the mouth opening? But if they belong to two different ambulacra, then, because they are single, they can only be arms.

The arms of *Echinosphaerites aurantium* Wahlenb. (*Sphaeronites aurantium* His.) behave in essentials exactly as Volborth **[p. 61]** has described and figured them. On well-preserved specimens which I have before me, the beginnings of three jointed arms are recognizable at the oral part of the calyx. The 5 uppermost calyx plates rise into a three-sided pyramid, truncated transversely above, whose blunted edges run out into the arms. Two sides of the pyramid are broader than the third. The sutures of the 5 pieces lie in such a way that two of them fall on the broader sides of the pyramid, the 3 others into the blunted edges. To the 5 principal pieces of the pyramid, however, there are added two further supplementary pieces, which from the calyx engage in two of the edge sutures. All 7 pieces have a share in the pore rhombs of the calyx plates only on their lower circumference. The arms divide again immediately. From the middle very fine grooves run on over the arms. The grooves are beset with marginal platelets. The marginal platelets, moreover, close so tightly at the middle too, that the assumption of the mouth opening at this place does not rest on direct observation; only specimens with an incomplete summit present a wide opening of the calyx in the middle of the summit. The division of the arms, moreover, proves that we are dealing with arms, not with pinnulae. Whether these arms, like the arms of some other Cystideen, such as *Pseudocrinites*, were provided with jointed pinnulae cannot be judged, since they are broken off at the stems. That the *Caryocystites* also possess arms is not yet known, but cannot be doubtful, since they are in general not distinguished from *Echinosphaerites*.

In *Hemicosmites* the summit possesses, above and between the uppermost large calyx plates, a further three-limbed field of small plates, whose limbs extend toward the broken-off arms, Pl. VI. fig. 4; but when these small plates have fallen off, the summit shows a large three-limbed fissure of the calyx, which is enclosed by the uppermost row of the large calyx plates, Pl. VI. fig. 5. Of the 6 uppermost plates of the calyx, 3 are provided with an incision which belongs to the three-limbed opening. Each of the incisions continues into a groove; the groove leads, now wider, now narrower, to a raised facet of the calyx which has served for the insertion of an arm. The elevation no longer lies in the plates of the uppermost row, but on 3 of the plates of the second row, and is found only on specimens which are not worn down, exceptionally beautifully on a specimen which **[p. 62]** Mr. Ewald has communicated to me. The three-limbed fissure of the calyx and the continued grooves of the calyx are thus covered with small platelets, which easily fall off. In the specimen figured by L. v. Buch they are still complete and form a fine, even plating from the middle outward toward the ventral side of the three arms, which completely covers over the three-limbed fissure of the uppermost calyx plates visible in other specimens. Although the small plates close tightly against one another, they are nevertheless distinctly separated from one another by depressed lines of contact. Among these lines of contact one distinguishes three which are directed from the middle toward the three arms, from the other lines of contact, which are directed transversely to these three longitudinal lines of contact. An opening in the middle is not to be seen. These three lines recall the grooves of *Echinosphaerites aurantium*; they correspond to the fissures of the large calyx plates lying beneath them.

*Hemicosmites* and *Caryocrinus* are, apart from the position of the arms, so similar to one another in the calyx and its pores that one easily feels tempted to reduce them to one another. In *Caryocrinus* the lateral mouth appears to be the only opening of the calyx, and this is, remarkably, provided with a valvular pyramid. In fact v. Buch has had to regard it as the mouth, whereas the similar valvular pyramid of the Cystideen was determined by him as the genital opening. Might now perhaps *Hemicosmites* also be closed on the summit, and might the small plates mentioned above on the summit between the arms, instead of having a fissure between them, rather close against one another? In this case the laterally placed opening of *Hemicosmites*, which in v. Buch's specimen is closed by a valvular pyramid, would be comparable to the valvular pyramid of *Caryocrinus* and would be the true mouth. This interpretation would make the determination of the mouth opening doubtful in the same way in the Sphaeroniten and in all Cystideen, in such a way that everywhere the opening closed by the valvular pyramid could be a laterally placed mouth. Against this view, however, stands the fact that the grooves from the middle of the summit to the arms in *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi* Volb. and *Protocrinites oviformis* Eichw. are most sharply marked, and that the straight lines of the fine grooves on the summit of *Echinosphaerites aurantium*, where the platelets of both sides border on one another, are extremely favourable to the interpretation of a mouth slit situated between them in the middle.

**[p. 63]**

In the always very worn specimens of *Cryptocrinites cerasus* no indications of the arms have yet been observed.

Forbes regards the Cystideen, like the Blastoiden, as divisions of the Echinodermen distinct from the Crinoiden. The *Sphaeronites* were already counted among the Crinoiden on account of their stem, even before their arms were known; with much more reason must this now be done. Volborth and Roemer regard the Cystideen as a group of the Crinoiden, and that is also my view. The position of the arms, however, must not be admitted among their characters. For the arms in *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi* and *Protocrinites oviformis* stood far removed from the mouth, similarly as in the other Crinoiden.

The sucker-feet of the Cystideen without doubt had their seat, as in *Pentacrinus*, on the ambulacral side of the arms and calyx grooves. In the introduction to this treatise, however, it has been proved how contrary to all analogy it is that in any Echinoderm sucker-feet should stand on the antiambulacral side of the perisome from the apical end up to the arms and between the ambulacral radii. In the Cystideen, therefore, the whole calyx, with the exception of the calyx grooves, is to be regarded as anambulacral.

The genera *Pentacrinus*, *Caryocrinus* and most of the Cystideen are distinguished among the Crinoiden by very peculiar pores in the non-ambulacral plates of the calyx. Only in *Pentacrinus* do we have the opportunity of informing ourselves more precisely about these pores. I have described and figured them in the treatise on the *Pentacrinus*. Abhandl. d. Akad. a. d. J. 1841. p. 225. Pl. II. fig. 14. a. Pl. III. fig. 1. The interambulacral (interpalmar as well as intrapalmar) calyx pores of *Pentacrinus* perforate the ventral calyx plates and lead beneath the inner calyx membrane; they are without any soft prolongations towards the outside. In contrast to the ambulacral calyx pores for feet, one may call them anambulacral calyx pores. Their significance is unknown; it is certain only that they are not passages for suckers. The comparison with the respiratory pores of the Asterien suggests itself, from which soft **[p. 64]** tubules rise up outwards; it has, however, been proved by Ehrenberg, to which I can add my own observations, that these tubules are small caeca, which indeed are connected with the body cavity but are completely closed towards the outside.

The calyx pores of *Caryocrinus* are likewise without relation to the arms, and therefore resemble, although differently distributed, the anambulacral calyx pores of *Pentacrinus*. They occupy the antiambulacral part of the calyx behind the arms as far as the base.

Most of the Cystideen (*Cryptocrinites cerasus* excepted) have calyx pores which are distributed over a larger or smaller part of the calyx without radiation and in a very peculiar manner. In the forms with calyx grooves, such as *Protocrinites* and *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi*, these pores again behave anambulacrally, since, like the anambulacral pores of *Pentacrinus*, they have their seat in the fields outside and between the ambulacral grooves; here, however, their distribution is much greater, since they reach as far as the base.

According to the distribution and connection of these pores, two principal differences have become known:

I. Cystideen with pore-rhombs. The pores are arranged in rhomb-shaped figures, of which one half belongs to one plate, the other half to the adjoining plate.

Every two pores of these rhombs seem always to be connected with one another by canals or grooves, which are visible either on the outer or the inner side of the plates, in such a way that the connected pores belong to two different plates abutting on one another.

a. Pore-rhombs without external connection of the pores: *Hemicosmites*, like *Caryocrinus*; in *Hemicosmites* the connecting grooves are, according to Volborth, on the inner side of the plates.

b. In *Echinosphaerites granatum* Wahlenb. (*Caryocystites granatum* v. B.) the pores are connected by ridges projecting outwards, which contain the connecting canal of the pores, and this canal is always a single one between every two pores or even a row of pores. Since this species is the type of the genus *Caryocystites* v. B., this circumstance is of particular importance. Other Echinosphaeriten, or forms derived from *Echinosphaerites*, have namely several connecting canals **[p. 65]** between every two pores. All the more weight is to be laid upon this circumstance, as the number of calyx plates, even of the basal plates, varies in *Caryocystites granatum*, so that individual specimens possess more calyx plates one above the other than others, and specimens with 5 basal plates are also not rare. According to the arrangement of the plates, in my opinion *Caryocystites* and *Echinosphaerites* could not be kept apart at all.

A form closely related to *Caryocystites granatum*, observed by Mr. Beyrich (found as an erratic boulder near Berlin), whose calyx plates are more numerous, is distinguished by the fact that the ridges which connect the pores belong to a whole row of pores which pass through the entire thickness of the plates, so that the rows of pores appear also on the inner side of the plates. Something similar is also recognizable in many specimens of *Caryocystites granatum* in that the canals of the ridges not infrequently also show slits here and there between the end pores. To be sure, these slits can easily be explained by the abrasion of the canals; but as soon as one has seen the regular rows of pores in the form just mentioned, this interpretation becomes doubtful as sufficient everywhere.

c. In *Echinosphaerites aurantium* and *aranea* every two pores of two plates are only rarely connected by one canal, mostly by two canals, which are recognizable on the outer surface of the plates; *Echinosphaerites testudinarius*, referred by L. v. Buch to the uncertain genus *Caryocystites*, is an elongated Echinosphaerit. It agrees in the pore canals more with the aforementioned species than with *Caryocystites granatum*, yet the number of pore canals between every two pores is in places still further increased. One observes, namely, not merely 2, but also 3 and even 4 canals side by side, which open at both ends into a pore and thus connect. Pl. VI. Fig. 6.

d. The genera *Echinoencrinus*, *Pseudocrinites*, *Apiocystites*, *Prunocystites* are distinguished by possessing only a few pore-rhombs, that is, fragments of the system of pore-rhombs; these too are rightly called pore-rhombs here. In *Echinoencrinus angulosus* and *striatus* there can be no doubt that the elongated pores of these rhombs are slits which pass through the entire thickness of the plates. Forbes had remained doubtful with regard to these pores and inclined **[p. 66]** to interpret the pectinated rhombs as the site of ciliated organs, comparable to the ciliated epaulettes of Seeigel larvae. The thoroughly enigmatic nature of all pore-rhombs, or of all non-ambulacral calyx pores of Crinoiden, does not, moreover, exclude the presence of cilia at the pores and pore canals.

The number of pore-rhombs in the Echinoencrinen seems to vary, and *Echinoencrinus granatum* Volb. may be only such a variety of *Echinoencrinus angulosus*.

II. Cystideen with double pores of the calyx plates, which belong not to two different plates but to the same plate. The plates are facetted and each facet contains two pores standing close together. To this belongs a small group of Cystideen which, since it consists of several genera, might be called Diploporiten. The genera belonging here are:

1) *Sphaeronites pomum* His. Type of a particular genus, which can retain the name *Sphaeronites*, in contrast to the Echinosphaeriten with pore-rhombs.

2) *Protocrinites* (*P. oviformis* Eichw.).

3) *Sphaeronites Leuchtenbergi* Volb. Type of a particular genus, which might be called *Glyptosphaerites*. That the Russian *Sphaeronites pomum* Leuchtenb. or *Sph. Leuchtenbergi* Volb. is not the Swedish *Sph. pomum* His., Volborth has already found probable from Gyllenhal. The specimens of the Swedish form in the mineralogical museum here place this beyond doubt. Calyx grooves are not present in the true *Sphaeronites pomum* His.; rather, the outermost 5 calyx plates rise into a three-sided pyramid truncated at the mouth, as in *Echinosphaerites aurantium*. The edges of the pyramid are broken off in all specimens and leave one in doubt as to the form of the arms, which were probably present. The base of the calyx is transversely truncated and, in relation to the diameter of the calyx, very broad; it consists of 6-7 pieces. The position of some other Diploporiten relative to these genera is still unknown. Several Cystideen described by Forbes, which he referred to *Caryocystites*, namely *C. Litchi* F., *C. pyriformis* F., *C. munitus* F., do not belong at all to the **[p. 67]** genus *Caryocystites* v. B., and are rather Diploporiten related to *Sphaeronites pomum*, which are still to be investigated further.

### Crinoiden with net-like hands.

A fossil Crinoid with net-like hands from Gothland had long been known as such in Stockholm, but has not yet been described and figured. Several years ago Mr. Prof. A. Retzius communicated to me fragments of the hand, with reference to the peculiarity of this Crinoid. Numerous dichotomously multiplying rows of ossicles are connected by lateral processes of the ossicles into a leaf-shaped form. I had never seen anything of the kind and could hardly comprehend that these could be parts of a Crinoid.

When I informed L. v. Buch of these fragments, he recalled that he possessed similar, equally enigmatic fragments from Gothland. We brought them on the same day to the Gesellschaft naturf. Freunde, and it was at once apparent that it was the same object. L. v. Buch was also willing to share with me what he possessed of it, with the friendship he has always shown me. I had, however, to give up explaining the nature of the animal from the pieces then available without the calyx; I harbour, moreover, a thorough aversion to the hasty naming customary in this field, which burdens science disproportionately for small gain. I handed over the fragments that had come to me to the mineralogical museum, as the most suitable place for their preservation. In this museum there were found still further fragments of this Crinoid from Gothland, which Mr. Beyrich communicated to me.

When Mr. Peters visited Stockholm in the spring of this year, he made further inquiries after the remains of the Gothland animal. Mr. Lovén had the extraordinary kindness to cede to me for description the magnificent pieces which he possessed of it. In one of these specimens the greater part of the calyx with part of the hands is preserved, in another a part of the calyx with the leaf-shaped hands. Yet a third specimen consists of the hands. Mr. Prof. A. Retzius has also communicated to me a beautifully preserved specimen of the hand. Who can view without joyful surprise these remains, in which one of the most remarkable forms of the Crinoiden stands out at once clearly with the peculiarity of its structure? See Pl. VIII. Fig. 1-4.

**[p. 68]**

The base of the calyx, whose plates are completely smooth, is not entirely preserved, but seems to consist of 5 Basalia; upon this followed a circle of 5 Parabasalia, alternating with these 5 arm bases, Radialia, which touch one another, with the exception of a small intermediate piece between two of the 5 arm bases. This arrangement would thus agree with *Cyathocrinus*. The parabasals are six-sided, their base is to their height as 3 : 2.

The arm bases are extremely low, three times as broad as high. On each arm base sit 3 ossicles, one of triangular form on the hollowed-out middle of the front margin, two on the sides of the front margin; these lay themselves with their inner part upon the middle piece until they touch one another. These two lateral pieces are the bases for all rows of ossicles of both halves of the hand. On each of the two there sit next 2 ossicles, an inner and a much broader outer one. The broader one is the first of the longitudinal row of broader ossicles which lies along the outer margin of the beginning of the hand; very broad at first, they become successively narrower, in that their outer margin corresponds to the outer margin of the hand, while the inner margin is successively cut away in stair-like fashion, from 2 ossicles to 2 ossicles, by so much that upon the steps of the next lower ossicles thereby produced a row of ossicles is again articulated. The stair-like steps thus skip one ossicle, and further on even several ossicles. The rows of ossicles soon divide again dichotomously, and the dichotomy proceeds continually. At a small distance from the arm bases one already counts over 30 longitudinal rows of ossicles across the breadth of one hand; at 1 inch distance from the base of the hand there are already about 80 rows of ossicles across the breadth of the hand, and so the multiplication proceeds. The ossicles lie not merely in regular dichotomous longitudinal rows, but in equally exactly regular arcuate transverse rows, and are at the sides articulately linked by opposing processes, so that all the ossicles of the hand together present a leaf with innumerable small gaps. These 5 hands have at their peripheral part an extraordinary breadth; in the expanded state they would without doubt not overlap, like the spread leaves of a 5-leaved corolla; in the folded-up state they cover one another entirely just like the folded-together leaves of a corolla; indeed their sides are even completely rolled in.

**[p. 69]**

The ossicles of the fused fingers are in general as long as broad or scarcely longer than broad.

The dorsal side of the ossicles is flat; the lateral processes are situated at the middle of the length of the ossicles, mostly however somewhat further forward, so that the ossicles, viewed from the dorsal side, acquire the form of a cross with very short arms. In that these ossicles connect lengthwise by their front and hind ends, and transversely by their lateral processes, a net with regular meshes arises. At the beginning of the arms the meshes are not yet developed and the ossicles are not yet cross-shaped, but four-sided.

Close above the calyx the ossicles are much stronger in thickness, i.e. in the direction from the dorsal side to the volar side, than in length. The thickest are the lowest, which rest upon the Radiale of the calyx. From there they decrease successively in thickness, so that they soon have only ⅓ of the thickness which they possess close above the calyx. Through the strong development of the first ossicles towards the interior, there arises, in connection with the Radiale of the calyx, a roofing of the periphery of the calyx cavity. Fig. 5. One notices on the articulation surfaces at the end of the ossicles a nutritive canal. On the cross-section one also sees that the volar side of the ossicles is deeply hollowed out, which hollow is enclosed by two ridges. Fig. 6.

Further on the thickness of the ossicles decreases rapidly; one inch from the beginning they are still 2 times as thick as long, but soon they are no longer thicker than broad; they retain the hollow on the volar side, which forms a deep canal over the volar side of the longitudinal rows of ossicles; this canal is covered transversely by small plates, which mostly interlock alternately. At the sides, on the volar side of the ossicles, bordering the ambulacra, stand extremely delicate pinnulae or narrow marginal plates, of which several (3-4) go to the length of one ossicle. These pinnulae are unjointed; only at the base does a small piece sometimes seem to be set off. The height of the pinnulae equals, at the broader part of the hand, the thickness of the ossicles. The vola of the hand was thus, corresponding to the dichotomy of the rows of ossicles, provided with hundreds of dichotomous ambulacral grooves, which were protected by delicate calcareous marginal plates. Fig. 7. 8.

**[p. 70]**

Where the rows of fingers have broken out of the rock and left behind the impressions of their volar side in the rock, these impressions appear as rounded reefs or dams with closely set regular transverse or zigzag incisions, which seem to correspond to the position of the covering plates between the pinnulae. Fig. 10. 11.

On a cross-section of the hands one obtains a picture of the rolling-in of the sides of the arms. On a longitudinal section perpendicular to the calyx one surveys not only the interior of the calyx and the previously mentioned roofing of the peripheral part of the calyx space, but one also recognizes the ventral perisome above the calyx, which, developing from the hands, lies still above this roofing and passes away over the middle of the calyx, as a line. The delicate pinnulae or marginal plates of the rows of ossicles of the hands continue at the calyx onto the ventral side of the calyx and can be traced on the section as far as the middle, where the mouth probably was. Fig. 5.

The constitution of the stem is at present still unknown.

Among the numerous Crinoiden of Gothland described and figured by Hisinger in the Lethaea suecica one searches in vain for a picture of reticulated arms; to be sure, there are among them not a few of which the arms are not preserved. It is difficult to imagine that among the many Crinoid remains he had seen there could have been nothing at all that belongs to the reticulated Crinoid. And in fact there is found a figure pointing to it, not of the net-like hand, but of the calyx and the first ossicles. It is his *Cyathocrinus pulcher*, calycis articulis hexagonis, margine striatis, manibus circiter 35, brevibus, linearibus, puncto medio profundo, angulo recto infractis. Leth. suec. supplem. II. Tab. 39. Fig. 5. From the perhaps deficient figure of the calyx pieces our Crinoid could not be guessed; nor do the figured marginal striae of the calyx pieces fit at all. For in the Crinoid with reticulated arms the margin of the calyx pieces is, at the marginal surfaces with which they touch one another, in places excavated and in places entire, without the outer surface of the calyx pieces themselves being incised or striated. But what noticeably points to our Crinoid is the figure of the lowest arm ossicles still sitting upon the calyx, which reach as deeply from outside inwards as in the Crinoid with reticulated arms, **[p. 71]** and possess the canal at the same place and the hollow on the volar side. Hisinger took these ossicles, with their exposed articular surfaces, for short linear hands, which were bent in at a right angle. The middle deep point which he indicates is the nutritive canal appearing on the articulation surface of the ossicles. The recognition of Hisinger's figure is very difficult and would not be possible at all without knowledge of the inner structure of the ossicles, such as is peculiar to the Crinoid with reticulated arms. In any case the object figured there is related to ours; but it would be justified neither from the description nor from the figure to identify it with it.

In the royal mineralogical museum here there is the plaster model of an English Crinoid from Dudley, whose arms, by their extremely numerous and delicate rays and by the regular rows of the small ossicles transversely and longitudinally, have some resemblance to the net of the Swedish Crinoid. The calyx agrees with *Cyathocrinus rugosus* Mill., i.e. *Crotalocrinus rugosus* Austin, and possesses the same sculptures of the calyx plates. At first sight of this model, which came from Mr. Krantz, one is inclined to ascribe to this English Crinoid the same net-like formation of the hands as to the Swedish Crinoid, and to regard both as species of one and the same genus or of two related genera. On careful examination, however, one convinces oneself that in the English Crinoid no conviction can be gained of a transverse connection of the ossicles. Rather, the departure of the very numerous rows of ossicles from the calyx is such that the notion of their union into 5 hands meets with considerable difficulties. On this, information could be obtained only from the original, and especially from various well-preserved pieces.

Of the genus *Crotalocrinites* Austin says, annals nat. hist. Vol. XI. 1843. p. 198:

Dorso central plates five; first series of perisomic plates five; second series five. On the latter are a series of wedgeshaped plates which bear the rays: the exact number of these plates is unascertained. Column with a pentapetalous perforation.

**[p. 72]**

*C. rugosus*.

The plates surrounding the body agree with the generic character. Rays numerous, probably amounting to one hundred. Column composed of thin joints articulating into each other by radiating striae. The columnar canal is pentapetalous. The rays are remarkably small in proportion to the size of the animal.

The authors, who were able to compare Miller's specimens, remark that Miller erred with respect to the plates which he incorrectly described as scapulae with a single hollow for the articulation of the arm ossicles. These plates had no hollow at all, but upon them rested a regular row of wedge-shaped plates, from which the rays, amounting to about 100, proceed.

The description which M'Coy gives in his Synopsis of the classification of the brit. palaeoz. rocks P. II. p. 55 of the genus *Crotalocrinus* and of *C. rugosus* confirms me in the view that the model of the English Crinoid refers to *Crotalocrinus rugosus*. The description of the calyx plates agrees exactly. Of the 5 scapulae it is said that on each of these rests a row of small pentagonal plates, which for the whole breadth of each plate bear a large number (?15 or 16) of very thin rays. None of the English writers mentions a net-like connection of the rays. I must therefore leave it uncertain whether this English Crinoid stands in any more distant or closer connection with our object.

Under these circumstances it becomes necessary to found a particular genus for the Crinoid with reticulated arms from Gothland, for which I propose the name *Anthocrinus*, species: *Anthocrinus Loveni* M. Evidently the Crinoiden with net-like arms stand by themselves and form a particular small division, of which at present only the one form from the Silurian formation of Gothland is known. On their relations to the other Crinoiden of the transition limestone I shall be able to declare myself only later, when the Crinoiden of this formation have become more completely known to me.

## V. Oral skeleton of the Holothurien, Seeigel and Ophiuren.

Meckel equated the bony ring of the Holothurien with the foremost part of the corona of the Seeigel and its auricular processes, later with the jaws; others have compared it with the teeth, with which it has in any case no resemblance. This ring of the Holothurien consists either of 10 pieces, as in most Holothurien, or of 12, as in some Synapten (*S. Duvernaea*, *digitata*, *inhaerens*), or of 15, as in other Synapten (*S. Beselii*, *vittata*, *serpentina*, *lappa*) (1). 5 of these pieces serve for the attachment of the 5 longitudinal muscles or of particular fascicles departing from them. Over these pieces the ambulacral vessels also pass in *Holothuria*, or through them in *Synapta*. In *S. Beselii* and *vittata* a large cartilaginous plate is attached to the bony ring, which at its lower part is perforated by a circle of holes. It reaches as far as the ring canal, covers both the oesophagus and the canals ascending from the ring canal to the tentacles. In *Thyone fusus*, *Phyllophorus urna*, *Hemicrepis granulata* Nob. (*Psolus granulatus* Grube) and *Molpadia Chilensis* (2) the radial pieces are prolonged behind into 2 jointed processes.

The ambulacral plates of the Seeigel and Asteriden are always double. Even when the coronal plates of the Seeigel run out as far as the mouth, the last ambulacral plates are still paired. The examples in which double ambulacral plates were supposed to close with a single plate hold nowhere, as has been shown above. If we now compare the bony ring of the Holothurien with the front margin of the corona, no proper agreement appears. The pieces of this ring lying in the direction of the radii, to which the longitudinal muscles attach and over which the ambulacral canals pass, are always unpaired. The bony ring of the Holothurien is therefore a movable ring suspended in the oral aperture of the perisome, whose parts find their analogy rather in certain pieces of the lantern of the Seeigel.

The 5 radial pieces of the bony ring of the Holothurien are to be compared with the radii of the lantern. The interradii of the lantern base correspond **[p. 74]** to the interradii of the bony ring of the Holothurien.

The jaw framework of the regular Seeigel consists, according to the complete analysis furnished by H. These compasses are present in the regular Seeigel; they are entirely lacking in the Clypeastriden.

The two epiphyses of a pair of jaws are, in the regular Seeigel, provided with processes which in *Echinus* and others unite into an arch at the base of the jaws; in *Cidaris* and *Echinocidaris* the processes are present, but no longer united into arches; in *Diadema* the processes of the epiphysial pieces are entirely bent in. The Holothurien lack the jaws and their teeth.

The Clypeastriden possess exactly the same composition of the jaw framework as the regular Seeigel, with a somewhat different form of the epiphyses and rotulae. Pl. VII. fig. 13-16. That their jaw framework should consist only of 5 pairs of jaws and everything else be lacking, as Agassiz asserts of the genera described in his monograph of the Scutellen, cannot hold for any genus of this family. The apparatus consists rather, in all genera of Clypeastriden, of 25 bony pieces, namely 10 jaw **[p. 75]** halves, their 10 articular epiphyses and 5 rotulae.

Des Moulins has already observed the rotulae of the *Clypeaster*, but wrongly found them wanting in the other genera. Pl. VII. fig. 13. In *Lobophora* the rotulae as well as the epiphyses are very low, corresponding to the flattening of the jaws. Short thin muscles pass from the auricles to the under side of the jaws.

The teeth of the *Clypeaster*, fixed in the groove of the jaws, are bare only at their outermost end; the remaining part is covered by a special soft membrane, which is to be regarded as the sac-shaped matrix of the tooth.

Of *Echinoneus* I have examined a specimen which was still provided with both the buccal and the anal plates and, although dry, had lost nothing from its interior. From the interior of the shell, however, I obtained no teeth, but only a little coarse sand, small snail shells and fragments of shells, such as sea sand contains, derived from the intestinal contents.

The star-shaped gap in the skeleton above the mouth of the Ophiuren and Asterien is, as is well known, not the mouth and forms only the propylaea of the mouth. They are, namely, movable and set upon the mouth-corner plate by two muscles. These muscles have their seat in deep hollows or holes of the mouth-corner plate lying in pairs. Pl. VII. fig. 7. 8. The hollows in part perforate the torus angularis and reach still into the bases of the mouth corners. The rows of movable ambulacral plates of *Cidaris* end at the mouth above the dental apparatus as 5 lobes surrounding the mouth; they differ from the mouth corners of the Ophiuren in that they are ambulacral or radial, while the mouth corners of the Ophiuren are interradial. Thus the dental apparatus of the Seeigel is something of its own, which the Ophiuren do not possess.

One may, moreover, regard the unpaired mouth-corner plate of the Ophiuriden as the last unpaired interambulacral plate, comparable to the last unpaired interambulacral plate of the Clypeastriden and Spatangoiden.

## VI. History of the stone canals in the Asteriden, Echiniden and Holothuriden.

Among the various appendages on the ring canal of the ambulacra (see above p. 15) the stone canal is the most constant.

### Asteriden.

Tiedemann had described the stone canal of the Asterien, arising from the madreporic plate and opening into the ring canal of the ambulacra, without knowing its internal structure.

After Ehrenberg had drawn attention to the fact that the content of the stone canal of the Asterien is not mere lime, but a tissue of calcareous fibres, v. Siebold taught us the structure of the rolled leaf projecting into the canal, or the labyrinth of the stone canal. Archiv f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1836. p. 291.

Sharpey and Agassiz have demonstrated the porous structure of the madreporic plate and the connection of the pores with the interior of the stone canal.

On the stone canal of the Ophiuren I have reported in the anatomical **[p. 82]** studies.

---
(1) In the Spatangen the mouth lies eccentrically to the aperture of the corona and to the ring canal lying at the margin of the corona, and close to the hind lip-like margin of the aperture of the shell. The front lip is formed not by the opposite margin of the shell, but by the plated buccal membrane.

The madreporic plate of the Euryalae at one of the mouth angles was already known. In *Astrophyton* as well as *Asteronyx* this plate is porous. In the System der Asteriden attention was also drawn to the fact that one of the 5 mouth shields of the Ophiuren is sometimes distinguished by an umbo. This umbo is sometimes raised, as in *Ophiolepis ciliata*, sometimes depressed, as in *Ophioderma longicauda*; but never porous, as in the Euryalae, but always smooth and without trace of pores. If one prepares the shield in question in *Ophiolepis ciliata* from within (Pl. VI. fig. 10), a cavity corresponding to the umbo is shown, in which the stone canal begins. The latter is a membranous tube, strengthened in its walls with latticed calcareous plates, which by a sac-shaped dilatation of the same structure joins the ring canal of the water-vascular system (fig. 11). On this canal there hang further 4 Polian vesicles, distributed over the 4 remaining interradial spaces. The Polian vesicles are without calcification of the walls and correspond to the Polian vesicles of the Asterien and Holothurien. From the stone canal the ring canal and the Polian vesicles can be inflated, and from these in turn the vesicle of the stone canal and the ambulacral canals and feet departing from the ring canal. In the vesicular dilatation of the stone canal lies a pulpy mass, which seems for the most part free, but near the opening of the stone canal into the sac grown onto the latter.

Since the beginning of the stone canal in all Echinodermen is porous and thus open, it is to be supposed that here the openings will be internal ones and either have their access from the genital slits or communicate with the visceral cavity. It often seemed to me that the stone canal of *Ophiolepis ciliata*, where it emerges from the inner excavation of the mouth shield, has a slit on both sides between the calcareous plates; but of this matter I have not become certain.

### Echiniden.

The central part of the ambulacral canals in the Seeigel was not yet known to Tiedemann. Delle Chiaje and Valentin confounded these relations with the blood vessels. The anatomical studies on the Echinodermen contain an exposition of the same.

**[p. 83]**

When the ambulacral canals of *Echinus* have passed through the auriculae and reached the lantern, they ascend on the outer side of the lantern over the interpyramidal muscles (musculi interpyramidales) (they are designated by Valentin, anat. du genre Echinus, as vessels from the gills to the lantern, and also figured, Fig. 135). Thus they reach the radii of the lantern; from there on they are covered by the 5 rotulae, which are the radii of the lantern; here they lie between the rotula and the interpyramidal muscle and between the epiphyses of the jaws, which abut against the rotula. The vessel fills this radial space entirely and is grown fast to the walls of the covered passage. From this point the water-vascular system of the Seeigel can at once be displayed in its entire connection. If one cuts into the vessel at its entrance and blows into the covered passage, the ring canal around the oesophagus, the 5 vesicles connected with it and the water-vessels of the 5 ambulacra inflate, so that one can survey at a single glance the whole extent of the ambulacral system. Mr. Peters has been able to convince himself of this connection on my preparations. The 5 vesicles lie upon the membrane which closes the upper part of the jaws or pyramids, and are still somewhat covered by the transverse muscles of the 5 compasses.

The stone canal of the Seeigel was first recognized and understood by Agassiz. Comptes rendus XXV. p. 679. Frorieps Notizen, III. Reihe, V. 1848. p. 145. He says that the jointed calcareous lamellae of the stone canal of the Asterien seem destined only for the protection of the membranous tube; they are absent in the Seeigel, whose sieve openings lead directly from the madreporite plate into a membranous tube not provided with a firm sheath. With this a good step was taken in the knowledge of the ambulacral system of the Seeigel. Now we already know this system in its full completeness in the Seeigel larva at the time of the formation of the Seeigel disc. See my 4th treatise on Echinodermen larvae, Pl. IX. fig. 3. 4. In the anatomical studies the stone canal of *Cidaris* is also described, which itself has calcified walls and is completely stiff. The stone canal of the Seeigel arises from the small basin of the madreporite plate, then ascends close to the blood-vascular heart, but without connection with it **[p. 84]** (just as in the Asterien), upward to the oesophagus and, having reached the lantern, inserts itself into the ring canal of the water-vascular system. It is a very fine canal; in *Echinus*, *Spatangus*, *Clypeaster* soft, in *Cidaris* densely furnished in its walls with small calcareous plates, so that it resists pressure and shows a round lumen in section. The labyrinth of small calcareous plates in the interior, which the Asterien have in the stone canal, is absent in the Seeigel. On the stone canal of *Spatangus* and *Clypeaster* see above p. 9.

### Holothuriden.

The Polian vesicles on the ring canal are present once or several times in all Aspidochiroten and Dendrochiroten. But *Cladolabes peruanus* Nob. n. sp., with 20 branched tentacles, has around 100 Polian vesicles all round the ring canal. Among the footless Holothurien the greatest differences occur in this respect. *Molpadia chilensis* Nob. (1) has one Polian vesicle. In *Synapta Duvernaea* Quatrefages has not mentioned such an organ at all, which however is hardly ever absent. In *Fistularia fusca* Quoy, Gaim. Voy. de l'Astrol., many Polian vesicles are figured. In *Synapta digitata* and *S. inhaerens* there are 1-3 Polian vesicles on the ring canal, in *Chirodota violacea* Peters several or many on one side of the ring canal. In some large species of *Synapta* which I examined, the Polian vesicles were uncommonly numerous and distributed all round the ring canal; so in *Synapta Beselii* Jaeg., *Synapta serpentina* Nob. from Celebes, *Synapta lappa* Nob. from the West Indies (2). In *Synapta Beselii* I counted about 50 of the thin little caeca on the ring canal, and in the two other species their number was not smaller.

The stone canal occurs in all Holothurien, but loses the connection with the surface of the animal, which in the Holothurien it possesses only in their larval state, and is therefore directed toward the body cavity, which in the Holothurien, as in all Echinodermen, is filled with a mildly salty fluid. It is the sac, present once or several times, **[p. 85]** with calcareous walls, of which Tiedemann and Delle Chiaje assumed that it was connected with the genital organs, while by others it was regarded as a gland connected with the gullet. I pass over the historical matter and start from the first more important observation. Krohn discovered its insertion into the ring canal. Frorieps N. Not. 1841. N. 356. In this organ the canal and the terminal piece, the sac, are to be distinguished. The walls of the latter are entirely porous and sieve-like, as can be beautifully seen under the microscope on excised pieces of the walls. One distinguishes 3 layers: the middle one is composed of a layer of branched calcareous fibres. This layer contains regular passages for soft tubules, which connect the holes in the outer and inner skin. The holes are surrounded by a ring-shaped ciliated rim. Pl. IX. fig. 6. 7. 8. In *Holothuria tubulosa* the tubules have 1/60''' diameter in breadth. Anat. Studien über die Echinodermen, Arch. f. Anat. u. Physiol. 1850. p. 148. This form is possessed by the organ, hanging down into the water of the body cavity, in the Holothurien of the genera *Sporadipus*, *Holothuria*, *Bohadschia*. In many Dendrochiroten, such as *Anaperus*, *Pentacta* and others, the calcareous sac is a bony capsule without pores; but in the calcareous walls there is a slit winding to and fro, like the holes in the body of a violin. Pl. IX. fig. 9. This forms the transition to the form of the madreporite plate of the footless Holothurien, *Molpadia*, *Synapta*, *Chirodota*. The softer canal, whose walls also contain calcareous fibres, is here crowned at its end with a regular madreporite plate of the usual meandrine form and porous structure. J. Müller über *Synapta digitata* Pl. I. fig. 10. 11.

Quatrefages, in his *Synapta Duvernaea*, has mentioned no such organ and nothing of the stone canal, which nevertheless occurs in all Holothurien and which I have never found missing in any species of *Synapta*.

The number of stone canals and madreporite plates varies just as much as the number of Polian vesicles. In *Molpadia chilensis*, Pl. IX. fig. 1, I found only one such organ attached to the ring canal, likewise in *Synapta digitata*, *inhaerens*, *Chirodota violacea*; in *Synapta lappa* one or several; in *Synapta serpentina*, fig. 5, they beset the ring canal all round in great number; in *Synapta Beselii*, fig. 2, the stone canal is branched and **[p. 86]** all the branches are crowned with madreporite plates; besides this, single stone canals also open into the ring canal in the neighbourhood of the larger one. The stone canals are sometimes more straight, sometimes curved, helicine or wound to and fro.

The analogy of the calcareous organ and canal of the Holothurien with the madreporite plate and the stone canal of the Asterien has already been indicated by Krohn and Goodsir (in Forbes), and Delle Chiaje has at least pointed to the similarity of the calcareous formation of the madreporite plate of the Seesterne and of his corpo peniforme of the Holothurien. That the similarity was grasped is all the more to be acknowledged, since the stone canal in the Holothurien examined up to that time ends in a sac, while the formal agreement first comes out in the madrepore-shaped plate at the end of the canal in the Synapten, Chirodoten and Molpadien.

The most essential property of the madreporite plates and madreporite sacs, however, lies in their pores, demonstrated in the Asterien by Sharpey and Agassiz, in the Holothurien by myself. This system shows the most complete agreement in the larvae of the Asterien, Seeigel and Holothurien through the dorsal pore observed by me in all of them, the beginning of the stone canal, which in the Asterien and Seeigel transforms itself into the madreporite plate, whereas in the Holothurien this develops as a madreporite sac on the part of the canal lying in the interior of the body, while the peripheral part of the tube disappears. As regards the Holothurien with many madreporic canals, it is not necessary that all were primitive canals of the larva and proceeded from just as many pores of the larva; they may presumably form either as outgrowths of the primitive canal, where the madreporite plates belong to branched stone canals (*Synapta Beselii*), or arise, with obliteration of the primitive canal, as outgrowths of the ring canal, where the madreporic canals hang numerously all round the ring canal (*Synapta serpentina*). Of the multiple madreporite plates of some Asterien it is at present still uncertain whether they derive from several primitive canals and pores of the larvae.

The exchange of the water in the stone canal through the madreporite plate probably takes place under the influence of the ciliary movement occurring in the water-vascular system and already at the pores of the plate, which **[p. 87]** can be recognized under the microscope on excised small pieces of the calcareous sac of a living Holothurie also on the outer surface, in that the small particles floating in the water rush rapidly toward the pores and for the most part also go back again.

## VII. Peculiar glandular organs of the Holothurien.

Organs of restricted distribution among the Echinodermen are the glandular tubules attached to the cloaca or to the trunk of the respiratory tree, which were seen by Cuvier and Jaeger and which I call the Cuvierian organs. Cuvier erroneously compared them with the male genital organs, Jaeger (de Holothuriis Turici 1833) with the kidneys. They have also already been confused with worms.

Cuvier and Jaeger have seen only the one form of these organs, the caecal form, which is figured by Jaeger in *Bohadschia*. I give figures of other gland-like forms, such as are peculiar to several genera.

There are 3 types of the Cuvierian organs. The one is the caecum-shaped. Several or many caeca proceed from the cloaca or from the beginning of the respiratory trunk: genera *Bohadschia*, *Sporadipus*, *Stichopus*. The caeca are transversely wrinkled and therefore look as if ringed, similar to worms. On drawing out the wrinkles or folds they become very much longer. In some *Sporadipus* their length equals that of the body, and they lie in coils in the neighbourhood of the cloaca. I found these caeca most developed in size and number in *Stichopus Troschelii* n. sp. from Celebes, where they form an enormous convolution in the hinder part of the body. Their insertion extends from the cloaca upward along the trunk of the respiratory tree as far as beyond the first branches. At the insertion the thick tubule is drawn out into a thin tube. The genus *Holothuria* also possesses the caeca, but mostly very small; they escaped Tiedemann and are easily overlooked if they have not already been expelled along with the viscera. In *Holothuria tubulosa* the number of the caeca is not great; in some other true Holothurien their number is great, and bundles of several hundred fine unbranched tubules sit upon the cloaca or upon the beginning of the respiratory trunk.

**[p. 88]**

The second form of the organs, the racemose one, is found in the Molpadien. In *Molpadia chilensis* two racemose red-brown organs (Pl. IX. fig. 12) are connected with the cloaca, consisting of very small vesicles (1/15 - 1/50''') of yellow-brown content; the very fine and long excretory ducts of the vesicles are received by a common little stem, so that this stem is beset with them along its whole length. Many thousands of vesicles sit on one such bush.

The third type is peculiar to the genera *Muelleria* and *Pentacta*. Here there are a number of tubules which are narrow at the insertion, then thicker, and further on become thin and thread-like again. On the thread-like duct there sits at the end a whorl of some glandular tubules, which again divide dichotomously and gradually become thinner. The glandular tubules are canals which are beset along their whole length with microscopically fine stalked vesicles, like the Meibomian glands of man and the glandular tubules of the insects. I found these organs in all species of the genus *Muelleria* Jaeg. (Pl. IX. fig. 10. 11) and in entirely the same manner in the large northern *Pentacta*, *Cucumaria frondosa*. In the Pentacten with unequal tentacles, such as *P. doliolum*, in the Holothurien of the genus *Thyone* v. D. et K. (*Anaperus* Tr.) and other Dendrochiroten I have not yet found them. The lungless Holothurien lack the Cuvierian organs.

With these organs of the Holothurien may be compared the caeca which in the Asterien with an anus hang on the rectum, in the anus-less Asterien on the base of the stomach.

The organs of the Holothurien just described have had very strange fates. Cuvier saw them first, namely mentioned and briefly described them in his lectures on comparative anatomy. He says that in the neighbourhood of the anus one notices a quantity of whitish worm-shaped threads, each of which consists of a thin, rather elastic, spirally wound and easily unrolled thread. From this description the organs are at once recognizable, namely in the form which is peculiar to most of the Aspidochiroten. The alleged spiral fibre is nothing other than closely set wrinkles which unfold on stretching.

Delle Chiaje also has an observation on this in his Memorie T. III. p. 68, in a Holothurie which he calls *H. fusus*, and of which he **[p. 89]** says that he found various intestinal worms attached to the respiratory tree; he calls the supposed worms *Taenia echinorhyncha* and figures them Tab. XXXV. Fig. 13-14. The figure of the regular transverse wrinkles leaves no doubt that Delle Chiaje had before him the Cuvierian organs in the form in which they occur in *Sporadipus*, *Bohadschia*, *Stichopus*, *Holothuria*. In the new work Animali senza vertebre T. IV. p. 5 it is said of the same Holothurie, which he now calls *Phyllophorus fusus* and refers to *Psolus granulatus* Grube, that it has in the neighbourhood of the cloaca some crooked bodies full of granular white matter. The animal of Delle Chiaje belongs to a genus of the Dendrochiroten. In *Psolus granulatus* Grube, which is the type of a special genus *Hemicrepis* Nob., I have not seen the large caeca; such I have in fact so far noticed only among Aspidochiroten. But *Psolus granulatus* Grube may have been confused by Delle Chiaje with a specimen of *Sporadipus impatiens* Grube, which have some similarity on account of the warts of their body. In *Sporadipus impatiens* the caeca are extraordinarily large. It would thus be possible that for the figure of the whole animal with the tree-shaped tentacles *Psolus granulatus* served, and for the examination and figure of the caeca a smaller specimen of that Aspidochirote.

Jaeger was then the next who saw the organs again, namely in *Bohadschia*, *B. marmorata* and *Argo*. The foregoing remarks are extracted from the anatomical studies on the Echinodermen, Archiv 1850, p. 139, for the purpose of now illustrating them by figures.

The other viscera of the Echinodermen, such as the respiratory organs, intestinal canal and genital organs, I can pass over as sufficiently known.

## Explanations and supplements.

### Seeigel.

Our Museum possesses 3 species of Clypeastern with marginal chambers: 1) *Clypeaster Rangianus* Desm., ambulacris omnibus apertis. Our specimens correspond to the description, not to the figure, of Des Moulins. Marginal part flat, height from a straight base to the summit 1'' to 4'' **[p. 90]** breadth of the Seeigel and 5½''' thickness of the marginal part. 2) *Clypleaster placunarius* [sic] Ag. (from the Red Sea), depressissimus ambulacris omnibus clausis. Height 10''' to 4'' breadth, marginal part flat, very thin, 3''' thick. 3) *Clypeaster tumidulus* Nob., ambulacro anteriore aperto, ceteris clausis. Timor. Height 1'' 2''', breadth 3'' 2'''; marginal part thick, sloping above, finally bluntly rounded.

### Asterien.

Of the plates of the Asterien, ambulacral, adambulacral, intermediate interambulacral, marginal and yoke pieces have been distinguished. The intermediate ones form rows both in the direction between the arms, parallel to the adambulacral plates, and in the direction from the ambulacra toward the margin. In most Asterien one sees them ranged in the one direction as well as the other. In *Ctenodiscus*, on the other hand, the intermediate system is very decidedly arranged transversely from the ambulacra to the margin, in that the paired rows of scales are united into transverse bars, and the bars in turn are bordered on their edges by papillae. It seems to follow from this that the arrangement of the rows from the ambulacra to the margin is the properly determining one for the intermediate plates.

The yoke pieces occur, remarkably, only in the family of the anus-less Asterien with conical sucker-feet, namely in *Astropecten*, *Luidia* and *Ctenodiscus*. Within the body cavity they connect the ambulacral plates with the intermediate ones (*Ctenodiscus*), or, in so far as the intermediate plates drop out, with the lower marginal plates (*Astropecten* and *Luidia*). In *Astropecten*, on the arms, where the intermediate plates are absent, the yoke pieces are placed between the ambulacral and lower marginal plates; on the innermost part of the disc, where intermediate plates appear, between the ambulacral and intermediate plates. In the Asterien with anus and sucker discs of the little feet I have missed the yoke pieces entirely, whether there are marginal plates or not, e.g. *Archaster*, *Astrogonium*, *Solaster*, *Asteriscus*. In this respect too *Archaster* departs entirely from *Astropecten*.

The peculiar unpaired plate on the inside between each two ambulacra is general in the genera of the Asterien and thus independent of the presence of the yoke pieces. At this spot end the membranous **[p. 91]** septa between the arms of *Astropecten*, *Astrogonium*, and the calcareous plates which in *Archaster*, *Solaster*, *Asteriscus*, *Oreaster*, *Culcita* are contained in the septa.

The muscles of the ambulacra (p. 47 and 52) are:

1. Upper and lower transverse muscles between the right and left halves of the ambulacral vertebrae.
2. Muscles which connect the ambulacral vertebrae in the longitudinal direction; they correspond to the intervertebral muscles of the Ophiuren, but are much smaller.
3. Muscles which connect the adambulacral plates in the longitudinal direction (*Astropecten*).
4. Muscles between the adambulacral plates and the lateral part of the ambulacral plates. They attach themselves to an adoral expansion of the lateral part of the ambulacral plates and effect a rotation of the ambulacral plate about the transverse axis.

The remaining part of the perisome of the Asterien is moved by muscle bundles which are more or less distinct on the inner surface in the body cavity or in the layers of the perisome. Between the marginal plates of *Astropecten* and others there are no muscles.

At the mouth of the Asterien lies outermost the nerve ring, close beneath it the blood-vascular ring, still deeper the ambulacral ring canal. At the arm grooves of the Asterien lies outermost the nerve, beneath it the branch from the blood-vascular ring mentioned on p. 17, beneath that the outer transverse muscles, beneath that, deepest, the ambulacral canal.

### Crinoiden.

The genus of Cystideen *Stephanocrinus* Conrad has been elucidated by Roemer, Archiv f. Naturgesch. XVI. Jahrg. 1. p. 365. Pl. V. Kidney-shaped warts at the end of the rays of the star-shaped field on the summit surface point to the presence of arms.

*Agelacrinus* agrees with the Cystideen in the possession of the valvular pyramid, but according to Roemer is unstalked, the sessile base of the calyx uncalcified, without plates. Roemer's observations also depart from the view of the arms of this genus put forward by Forbes. Verhandlungen des naturhistorischen Vereins der Preufs. **[p. 92]** Rheinlande und Westphalens, VIII. Jahrg. Bonn 1851. p. 370. The radii of *Agelacrinus* lie, according to Roemer, entirely at the level of the surface of the disc and are immovably fused with the adjoining small plates, hence no movable arms, but radial fields, areae, comparable to the radii of the Pentremiten. On the impression of the inner surface of the shell of *A. rhenanus* the radii were likewise impressed.
