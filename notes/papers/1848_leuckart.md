The scientific task of general zoology consists, partly, in demonstrating the lawful connection between animal forms through the changes they undergo, and partly, in summarizing the wealth of diverse prominent and subordinate structures according to their intrinsic nature. It corresponds to a certain aesthetic need of the human mind, which finds its satisfaction less in the search for possible differences than in the reduction of apparent differences. The rich diversity of organic forms is not accidental, not random and irregular, not without limits. Everywhere there is plan and law, everywhere an inner connection.

What even a superficial observation of nature reveals—that the individual animal forms are not all equally different from one another, but rather agree in many respects—is shown to an even greater degree by careful comparison.  Everywhere we find a definite, lawful relation of forms, everywhere a greater or lesser kinship between them. A series of transitions often connects the most diverse structures. Amidst the manifold variations, a specific, pervasive type is revealed.

The zoological system in its perfected form gives us the complete expression of this inner kinship of animal structures.

Numerous and diverse have been the attempts at such a representation. Depending on the prevailing perspective, the amount of available material, and the knowledge thereof, the value of these individual attempts is, of course, very different. Moreover, in the older zoological systems, this truly scientific task, while not entirely overlooked, was mostly subordinated to a certain practical interest. Like a kind of dictionary, these systems provide little more than a means of identifying the various animals.

The name Cuvier marks a new and brilliant epoch in the history of our science. With the keen insight of genius, this famous researcher recognized the profound importance of a more general, I would almost say, an artistic conception of organic forms. What he strove for through his system was not the greatest possible practical utility, but rather an understanding of the interconnectedness of the various animal forms, a mutual understanding of their individual diversity. A consistent consideration of the entire external and internal organization opened his eyes to the relationships between creatures, a perspective that would have remained forever closed to the older methods of investigation and the resulting classification of different forms according to one or another, often superficial and irrelevant, principle of division.

An immediate benefit of the method of investigation employed by Cuvier is the demonstration that the development of animal forms does not proceed in a single ascending direction, nor within the confines of a single common circle. Just as in architectural works of art, so too in organic forms there are different typical structural patterns, certain varying norms that, in individual instances, allow for the most diverse and manifold modifications without thereby losing a specific characteristic imprint. Naturally, however, these different types are not entirely without connection or correspondence. The very concept of animal organization, to which they are subordinate, presupposes a common set of specific characteristics. Moreover, even in their most general aspects, the laws of animal form development are the same in all cases. Only the number and the ratio of the points of attack, only the combination of the individual morphogenetic processes that determine the final product, are variable. In one type, this particular use of the formative forces predominates, in another, a different one. The capacity for modification of the resulting form, in turn, depends on the nature of this combination. Even the number of individual factors involved here gives rise to the greatest differences.

On the other hand, within the different types, we also find numerous analogies in the design of the individual parts, and many correspondences in the use of the various forces. And it is precisely in the multitude of such coincidences and points of intersection that a dangerous pitfall threatens natural systematics. It can only be avoided by a comprehensive understanding of organic form based on developmental history. The time is approaching when this science will gain an equally crucial influence on zoology as comparative anatomy has exerted since Cuvier. However, the latter must never be thrown overboard as useless ballast—as has recently been prophesied. It will constantly remain the surest support of zoology; supported and permeated by developmental history, it will pave the way for a scientific understanding of animal form, a morphology.

It is precisely in the consideration of the external form, which always constitutes the primary object of zoological investigation, that the great importance of such a morphological approach becomes apparent. It alone informs us of the relative value and the relationship of the form to the general plan of organization. And only through this do we gain insight into the relationships of kinship of a creature. Similarity in external appearance does not in itself justify the assumption of a real correspondence. A similar form can have a very different meaning and have arisen in the most diverse ways. Only knowledge of development can guide us here. It alone can reliably decipher the value of the morphogenetic processes employed and their relationship to the ideal basic type. The modifications of form that we encounter within the individual typical basic forms of the animal world are everywhere limited to the relative development of the constituent parts, united according to a specific plan.  Position, form, and size, direction, value, and number are the variable factors. However, the scope for variations is not unlimited here either.  There are certain laws to which the plasticity
of animal forms is subject.

We know that every organ of the body must pass through a series of stages for its complete development, which differ considerably from one another. If development is inhibited in any way, the earlier embryonic arrangement and form persist in the structure in question.  This is the case in pathological instances during the development of the individual. Creative nature also makes use of this same law of developmental inhibition infinitely often to produce the diverse forms of a particular structure belonging to one or another type.  In one creature, a form remains that in another only constitutes a transitional stage, necessary for reaching a higher stage of development.  Not infrequently, however, the relations of a particular part are modified in a somewhat different way in individual creatures.  This part also only develops up to a certain stage; but then it does not simply stop, but becomes the scene of a new process. Often it undergoes regression! — in other cases it is further developed according to a specific, more or less deviating plan.

The complete deciphering of these manifold riddles in organic form development is reserved for a later time.  For now, it suffices to know and keep in mind the task of such an investigation. That our efforts will finally be crowned with success is guaranteed by the morphological truth of the content, which expresses itself with eternal regularity in the context of the individual formal phenomena.  It is possible that one day we will succeed not only in fully recognizing the laws of organic form development, but also in reducing their application in individual cases to certain more or less simple mathematical relationships. However, there is hardly any prospect of this at present. Our knowledge of the development and significance of animal forms is still far too incomplete and uncertain. We still understand far too little how to distinguish the accidental from the essential, the conditional from the conditioning.

Of paramount importance for the morphological treatment of zoology is the identification of the individual main types, the architectural styles in the world of animal forms.

Very early on, the need for larger divisions, transcending the individual classes, made itself felt in systematic zoology. The systems of Aristotle, Ray, Linnaeus, etc., demonstrate this. However, Cuvier was the first to decisively state that such larger divisions must each represent a specific typical basic form in the animal world, while the subdivisions of these should offer mere modifications, not essential changes to the respective basic plan.

However, if we apply such a standard to the system of this master with our current knowledge of the structure and development of animals, we can no longer approve of it in every respect. The four large divisions of the animal kingdom established by Cuvier, however much they stand as an admirable monument to their creator for that time, no longer meet the requirements of a natural system of classification. All these divisions, with the exception of the first, the division of vertebrates, require a greater or lesser reorganization.

Although we are still far from a complete understanding of the individual relationships among the various animal forms, much can already be considered a well-founded result of our recent investigations into their morphological connections. Regarding the methodology of classification, we must above all demand that the individual groups, created according to a specific principle and considered equal, are indeed truly equivalent. From this perspective, we must first reject the common division of animals into vertebrates and invertebrates, which originated with Cuvier and was later frequently used, particularly by Lamarck, as unnatural. These two divisions clearly have very unequal value. While the former exhibits only a single, specific body plan, the latter contains several different ones, each of which deserves to be considered equivalent to the vertebrate type. With the same justification that one places vertebrates parallel to invertebrates, one could also divide all animal forms into radial and non-radial – as Blainville attempted in his groups of Actinimorphes and Artiomorphes – into segmented and non-segmented (Milne Edwards), or into cephalic and acephalic – as Latreille did with invertebrates when he separated them into Cephalidia (Mollusca and Articulata Cuv.) and Acephala (Zoophyta Cuv.). In all these cases, one appeals to the existence of a specific characteristic belonging to one or the other type (which does not even have the same significance everywhere) and excludes the remaining forms that lack this characteristic – a procedure that should hardly ever be used in establishing natural groups, not merely disconnected, irrational collections. If one still wishes to establish a division of invertebrate animals, one should, as a very excellent zoologist has quite rightly observed, intend to designate nothing more than "animals other than vertebrates," without in the least considering them as internally related forms. It then forms only an indefinite appendix to a specific group and contains no general concept that would stand in opposition to another general concept.⁴

In natural classification, one must be very careful about establishing divisions held together solely by negative characteristics. These divisions are usually (like Cuvier's division of the Zoophyta, for example, or Burmeister's main group of Gastrozoa—Cormozoa Streubel—in which the zoophytes, but without the Entozoa, are combined with the mollusks, etc.) less natural and quite different in value from the other groups with which they are combined. But even with a truly natural group, deciding on its relative value is not always easy, especially since the greater or lesser plasticity of the inherent type allows for a wide variety of possible subdivisions. In my opinion, the morphological dignity of the characteristic features alone can be decisive here—without regard to the number and diversity of the forms united in the groups in question.

Equally insufficient and also to be rejected in the establishment of the smaller or larger divisions of the animal kingdom, it seems to me, is any a priori construction, as well as the mere one-sided consideration of one or another anatomical system. The former often leads us to a different result than the empirical investigation of formal phenomena—not because the world of the latter perhaps does not present a rational connection corresponding to the regularity of logical thinking, but because in such an operation of our mind, the most diverse prejudices can so easily assert themselves. The anatomical arrangement of a single organic part or system, however, must therefore be of very deceptive value in determining the various groups, especially the larger typical main divisions of the animal kingdom, because, as already mentioned, the combination of the individual morphogenetic processes within them reveals numerous intersections and points of coincidence that in themselves do not necessarily indicate a true architectural relationship or correspondence. From such viewpoints, we must consider both the systems of the so-called natural philosophical school as insufficient (according to which, for example, animals are divided into Protozoa, Gasterozoa, Thoracozoa, and Cephalozoa), as well as those in which the nature of either the external form (Dumeril, de Blainville) or a specific anatomical system, such as the nerves (Rudolphi, Granit), the blood (Wilbrand), the respiratory system (Schweigger), the skin and muscles (Oken), etc., is taken as the sole basis. Even more inappropriate is it to use specific physiological characteristics as a criterion for classification in systematic zoology, which should only consider the formal appearance, as Lamarck did when he established the types of "animaux apatiques," "sensibles," and "intelligents" in his natural history of invertebrates.

The path that Cuvier has shown us, this path alone promises to lead to the goal. Only a balanced consideration of both the external appearance and the structure and mutual relationship of all individual anatomical systems in their fully developed state and during the earlier stages of development—in short, a balanced consideration of the entire morphological character—can lead us to an understanding of the plan of organization and thus to a knowledge of the individual natural divisions.

In presenting the individual basic forms, nature has repeatedly made now this, now that part the main carrier of the typical plan. In these parts, as in the focal point of light rays, the individual characteristic features of the organization are concentrated. It is these parts, therefore, that the zoologist must primarily keep in mind when establishing his groups. From them, in doubtful cases, and based on the inherent unity of truth in animal forms (according to the so-called law of coexistence), he can deduce the general outline of the entire organization according to the existing analogies. However, since, as mentioned, these points of concentration vary in the individual types, it is entirely consistent with the formal appearance that now this, now that part is given preferential consideration in the establishment and characterization of the individual divisions. Determining which of these features are the most prominent remains the object of empirical research. Thus far, we have recognized the abundance and diversity of animal forms according to their intrinsic nature and their interrelationships.  The divisions of Coelenterates, Echinoderms, Worms, Arthropods, Mollusks, and Vertebrates might then emerge as representatives of the various main types of the animal kingdom created by nature.

We will leave the Infusoria out of consideration here. Despite the classic works of Ehrenberg, we still know too little about them to consider ourselves justified in forming even a somewhat well-founded judgment. However, it seems very probable to me that the entire division of these enigmatic creatures, at least as a distinct, cohesive group, will later be removed from the zoological system. Many forms included in this group are certainly merely undeveloped or stunted individuals, others are even low-level plant organisms or their spores. The similarity of certain infusorians (apart from the rotifers, which undoubtedly have a completely different organizational plan) to other more highly developed animals, particularly polyps and worms, has also been pointed out repeatedly, especially in the past. The purpose of the following investigation is to demonstrate that the main divisions (so-called provinces or classes) of the animal kingdom, as understood according to our current knowledge, are natural and well-founded, and to show how they are delimited from one another and how they are structured into classes and orders based on their essential differences. The vertebrates are omitted from this discussion. They are the most thoroughly understood in terms of their morphological relationships and would hardly allow for any doubt regarding the full justification of their typical division.

First, a few words about the relative positions of these individual divisions. In themselves, as the respective representatives of a specific structural plan, they possess equal value. They form just as many parallel series. However, it cannot be denied that the most perfectly developed creatures within them occupy very unequal ranks. That, for example, man exhibits an infinitely greater degree of perfection than an insect or an annelid hardly needs special mention. It is a different matter, however, when we descend within the individual types to the lowest forms, where only the simplest, I would say, most rudimentary features of the common morphological plan are presented. Even if we do not wish to deny that Amphioxus, for example, in which the outlines of a vertebrate type are most simply sketched, still stands relatively higher than, for example, a polyp, the same could hardly be maintained in a comparison with Sepia or Octopus.

With each division in nature, a new epoch of formation begins, as it were, in which, however, the individual phases of development must also be traversed anew, up to the highest flowering of perfection of which each respective epoch is capable. In the totality of these individual epochs, however, a specific, ever-improving, and artistically perfecting course of development is undeniable. Nature has attempted ever new, more purposeful, and nobler combinations of the formative processes in them, aimed at a specific ideal goal. — The sequence in which I have arranged the individual types above seems to me to correspond most closely to this gradual perfection of organic structural styles. That most German zoologists consider the arthropod type to be more highly developed than that of the mollusks, and therefore place the latter below the former, is something I cannot endorse, even though I readily admit that an insect exhibits a relatively greater degree of perfection than a tunicate. However, it is not the elegance, the precision, and the uniform perfection of the external structure that should be decisive here, but rather the formative capacity of the type. In this latter respect, I have no hesitation in declaring the cephalopods, which belong to the division of mollusks, to be the most highly developed forms of all invertebrate animals.

Echinodermata

The echinoderms, which were first recognized in their mutual relationship by Cuvier, and which, according to this great zoologist, form a class within the division of radiate animals equivalent to the polyps, acalephs, and helminths, are also, it seems to me, the representatives of a distinct type in the diverse world of invertebrate animals.² Like the coelenterates, worms, arthropods, mollusks, and vertebrates, they must, in my opinion, be considered a coherent, multi-segmented main division among animal forms.

With the coelenterates, to which they are related on one side, they share the peculiar radial structure of the body, which, however, is distinguished in them partly by the predominance of the number five (instead of the number four, which is common in coelenterates), and partly also shows much greater variations than is ever the case there. In accordance with the body structure, the mouth in echinoderms is also located at the anterior pole of the central longitudinal axis. However, what strictly separates the animals belonging to this group from the coelenterates is especially the arrangement of the digestive apparatus. Everywhere there is a considerable intestinal canal, isolated from the body cavity, which is sometimes closed blindly at the posterior end, but sometimes also leads to the outside through a separate anus. As a rule, this is located opposite the mouth opening, at the posterior end of the central axis of the body. Not infrequently, however, it also shows a greater or lesser eccentricity in its position. This deviation from the actual norm, which is quite analogous to the manifold asymmetries of animals with a lateral body plan, is hardly based on an original difference in the developmental laws that determine the radial type, but rather merely on a later modification of these laws through other morphological processes; the change in position is undoubtedly secondary. Besides the digestive tract, which is attached along its length by a mesentery in the spacious, undivided body cavity, there is also a separate blood vascular system, which is likewise absent in coelenterates and is represented here by the body cavity. Its arrangement, as well as that of the nervous system and the genital apparatus, is, according to the type, radial. Only in the elongated forms of holothurians, which, particularly through the Sipunculids, approach the worms, is this arrangement, at least in part, obscured and combined with certain features otherwise found only in animals with a bilaterally symmetrical body plan. The same lateral arrangement is also evident in the structure of the internal gills of holothurians.

Just as we described a specific typical similarity in the construction of the nervous system in coelenterates, we find the same in echinoderms.  In them, the central parts of this apparatus form a pentagonal ring that surrounds the upper part of the digestive tract and sends out the main nerve trunks from its individual corners.

Although this reveals a new fundamental difference between echinoderms and coelenterates, on the other hand, there are also many analogies and similarities. This includes, in particular, the abundance of calcium salts in the outer coverings, which is common to both echinoderms and anthozoans. These salts are often deposited in the most peculiar forms. They also usually harden the body's covering into a very firm skeleton with numerous hook- or spine-shaped projections. Even the internal parts sometimes calcify. However, to still enable locomotion in echinoderms, there are numerous contractile feet (ambulacra) grouped in longitudinal rows at the periphery of the body, which serve for attachment and, upon contraction, pull the body behind them.

The manifold variations in the external form of echinoderms can be explained, in the same way as with coelenterates, by a central elongation or depression of the original spherical shape (which persists in cystoids and echinoids).  What distinguishes echinoderms from coelenterates, however, is the tendency to form manifold radial extensions of the peripheral body margin, the so-called arms, which must be morphologically strictly distinguished from the tentacles of coelenterates. Their structure in the individual echinoderms is, incidentally, very diverse. From the longitudinal ridges on the bodies of some holothurians (the pentagonal forms of the genus Pentacta) to the blunt corners of some asteroids (the species belonging to Schweigger's division Corpore angulato) and the hollow arms of the Asteracanthia, etc., there is a gradual transition from these to the solid, articulated, and sometimes even branched arms of the ophiuroids and crinoids.  These radial arms are direct extensions and continuations of the body (like the lobe-shaped extensions of the body in the genus Ephyra among the discoid jellyfish), which gradually grow out of it during development, but are not special, independent appendages, like the tentacles of the coelenterates, which also occur in an analogous way in the echinoderms and are found here in the so-called external
gills of the echinoids and holothurians in the vicinity of the mouth opening.

The majority of the echinoderms belonging to the group of so-called crinoids are attached by means of a calcareous stalk, which originates from the posterior pole of the body, opposite the mouth opening. The other echinoderms are free, but usually not in all stages of their life. In their juvenile stages, they are usually attached in a completely analogous way. This is the case with the comatulids (according to Thompson!, who initially described the undeveloped forms of C. mediterranea as Pentacrinus europaeus), and also with the echinoids (according to Dufossé). The rudiment of the stalk serving for attachment is the so-called button in the comatulids, and the madreporite plate in the echinoids. Since the latter characteristic also occurs in the Asteroids (as well as among the Ophiuroids, specifically in the genus Euryale), it becomes probable from the outset that these too are attached in their youth.  This is indeed the case, as the observations of Sars*) have shown, which, together with the interesting discoveries of J. Müller5) on the development of the Ophiuroids, yield some striking results for the morphology of these animals and their relation to the forms considered earlier.

The investigations carried out by the former on Asteracanthion Mülleri and Echinaster Sarsii (sanguinolentus S.) show that the embryos of these starfish, after having moved freely for a time as infusoria-like creatures of oval shape (just like the embryos of coelenterates, echinoderms, but also annelids and other invertebrates) by means of an external ciliary covering, gradually develop four wart-like appendages at one end of their body, originating from a common point, by means of which they attach themselves, like the echinoids and most coelenterates (though probably not all, since the so-called siphonophores likely remain free). Undoubtedly, the point corresponding to the attachment point is the same as in those forms, i.e., the posterior pole of the body and the vertical axis extending from it is the longitudinal axis of the animal, which coincides with the central axis of the echinoids, crinoids, etc. However, the flattening of the body in the embryos of these starfish begins remarkably not along this longitudinal axis, but from the sides, so that the central axis of the developed animal does not, as is usually the case, coincide with the actual longitudinal axis, but intersects it at a right angle. The longitudinal axis of the body in the developed starfish therefore runs in a straight line through the madreporite plate, which, as mentioned, corresponds to the original point of attachment, and the center of the disc. In a surprising way, Agassiz's insightful conjecture about the lateral symmetry of asteroids, about the actual existence of an anterior and posterior end (deviating from the central axis) in their developmental history, is thus confirmed. However, these circumstances demonstrate once again how a specific organizational plan can be realized even under altered conditions. Despite the deviating direction, the body of the starfish develops perfectly according to the laws of the radial type.

Although Sars' observations clearly demonstrate such a remarkable difference between the longitudinal and central axes of the body in the manner just described in starfish, this behavior nevertheless requires closer attention and understanding, all the more so since, in the development of echinoderms, many other very divergent conditions seem to exist. For example, Müller discovered that ophiuroids and some armless echinoderms, which he initially considered to be echinoids, but which are probably—since a different mode of development has been observed in them—spatangoids, do not simply develop gradually from the yolk mass of the egg, but are subject to a highly striking alternation of generations. The embryos of these creatures are formed, like embroidery in a frame, within a peculiar, frame-like nurse animal (Pluteus paradoxus), which consists of four interconnected, skeletal longitudinal rods covered with a skin-like membrane. It has a stomach with an (eccentric) mouth opening and is externally covered with a ciliary layer, by means of which it swims freely. The relative position of the embryo to this nurse animal is similar to that of the young asteroids to the attachment structures mentioned above. In fact, the analogy between these parts and that nurse animal seems so great to me that I cannot suppress the conjecture that they might correspond in their morphological significance. The difference between them would then consist solely in their relatively very different development and would be reduced to the fact that in the asteroids, this apparatus is formed simultaneously with the embryo, as it were, as a mere appendage of the latter, from the yolk mass, while in the other case it arises solely from the metamorphosis of the yolk and can therefore develop into an independent, freely moving animal. Moreover, the fact that this apparatus sometimes does not serve for attachment and can reach a considerable size in a different way is shown by the discovery, recently confirmed by Koren and Danielssen, of Sars, that the creature previously described by him as Bipinnaria asterigera is merely a developing starfish equipped with a large swimming apparatus.

It is very regrettable that our current knowledge of the development of echinoderms is not more comprehensive, that we still know nothing more about the holothurians than that they are initially the size of a barleycorn and resemble a whitish maggot. Certainly, later research will provide us with many highly important insights into the morphology and the relationships of the various groups.

Regarding the systematics of echinoderms, Cuvier, as is well known, classified them according to the presence or absence of ambulacra into Echinodermata pedicellata (with the families of Asteroids, Echinoids, and Holothurians) and Echinodermata apoda (with the Sipunculids).  However, the structures upon whose characteristics this classification is based evidently have far too little typical significance to be given precedence over the more fundamental and important organizational relationships that are expressed in the anatomical arrangement of the internal organs and the skeleton. Much more noteworthy in this respect is the classification by Latreille, who separated the holothurians (Scutoderma Schulz, Sceytodermata Brmstr.) as a distinct group from the true echinoderms (the Asteroids, Echinoids, and Crinoids) and, combining both with the tunicates, created his class of Actinozoa, which is equivalent to the Phytodacea. However, how unnatural the combination of tunicates with Cuvier's echinoderms is, is easily apparent. Nor has this combination ever found any favor, while the first two orders established by Latreille have otherwise been adopted in the same scope by many, especially by Burmeister! Unfortunately, however, the latter author (Lamarck) still combines the Acalephae with these two orders, which, as I believe I have demonstrated, undoubtedly belong together with the polyps.

According to another principle of classification, based on the differences in external form, Lamarck formed the orders of Stellerida (Asterida), Echinoidea, and Fistulida from the division of Echinoderms, the latter of which, as already mentioned above, contained the Actiniae in addition to the Holothurians and Sipunculids. Lamarck completely separated the Crinoids, which Cuvier had placed among the Asterida, from the Echinoderms, in order to assign them—with the exception of Comatula and Alecto, which remained with the Asterida—to the polyps, specifically the Halopterids; a procedure which, incidentally, now that we have become more familiar with the organization of these animals through Miller³ and especially through J. Müller⁴, hardly deserves any further refutation. However, these same investigations have shown us that the Crinoids should by no means be combined with the Asterida, but rather that they form a distinct group, distinguished, among other things, by fundamental differences in skeletal formation.

Thus, Cuvier's Echinoderms would be divided into four, or, if one considers, as has frequently been done in more recent times, the Ech. apoda (Sipunculacea Brdt., Gephyrea de Quatref.) as a separate group, into five orders, which are indeed considered by many zoologists as natural and equivalent groups. While these groups may be natural, which I readily admit, in my opinion, they are hardly equivalent.

First, as Streubel has already suggested, the asteroids and echinoids must be united into a single larger division. Their very close relationship is already proven by their developmental history. In their earliest stages, asteroids and echinoids, as well as ophiuroids and spatangoids in particular, show a very striking analogy. The former are, as mentioned, attached with the help of a special stalk-like apparatus, while in the latter, the remarkable nurse cells and the first embryonic forms of the permanent animals correspond perfectly. The later differences that appear in development each belong to the specific plan of one or the other group of this division. Even the fact that, as we have stated above, a change occurs in the direction of the central axis in the asteroids, which serves as a point of attack, so to speak, for the radial type, cannot prevent us from a closer union of the animals in question. Otherwise, we would probably also have to remove the spatangoids from the order of the echinoids. Moreover, the essential agreement in the anatomical structure of the individual organs also justifies the union of the two groups into a common larger division. We only need to imagine the body of an echinus strongly flattened in the direction of its central axis to obtain an obtuse-angled starfish, with opposing central mouth and anus openings, with a nerve and vascular ring radiating towards the five corners, and with an equally radially grouped reproductive system and locomotor apparatus. The fact that in the asteroids these tentacle-like ambulacra are found only on the upper oral surface is repeated in an analogous manner in the spatangoids, where they are restricted to the dorsal side. Furthermore, those remarkable grasping organs, the pedicellariae, are found uniformly in echinoids and spatangoids, and only in these groups. The occurrence of a madreporite plate is likewise restricted to these two groups. The differences expressed in the skeletal structure are no greater than those found in the individual orders and groups of other classes, such as the polyps, where sometimes only an external skeleton is present, and sometimes both an external and an internal skeletal system occur simultaneously.

What seems unnatural to me in the above-mentioned classification of echinoderms into five equally ranked orders is the complete separation of the Sipunculids from the Holothurians. Admittedly, it cannot be denied that in the former of these animals, the radial arrangement, which otherwise stands out so strikingly, disappears even further than in the latter—as is particularly evident in the structure of the nervous system, which is built almost entirely like that of the annelids and arthropods—yet this, I believe, alone does not yet justify completely separating the Sipunculids from the Holothurians and even, as has been repeatedly suggested in recent times, classifying them with the worms. Numerous very important similarities to the Holothurians still remain. The presence of internal gill sacs (in Echiurus and Thalassema) and a mesentery, the location of the genitals in the anterior part of the body are characteristics that the Sipunculids share with the true Holothurians and which are, in part, completely alien to the type of worms. The absence of ambulacra in the Sipunculids should not be given too much weight; they are also absent in Synapta, although this animal undoubtedly belongs to the Holothurians. In general, the presence of such locomotory organs is only made necessary by the rigid nature of the outer coverings.  Where this has ceased to appear as a formal skeleton, where the body coverings are soft and flexible (as is the case with Synapta and the Sipunculids), there they have lost their significance and may be absent. The three larger groups of echinoderms resulting from such a combination of the echinoids and asteroids on the one hand, and the holothurians and sipunculids on the other, which were already established in the same way by Streubel, can, I believe, be considered, according to their systematic significance, as so many classes, equivalent to the polyps and acalephs in the division of the Coelenterata, and each of which, in turn, is divided into individual orders.

The first of these three classes, for which I would like to propose the name Pelmatozoa, comprises a large series of prehistoric forms, whose few still living representatives, as one of our greatest geologists has observed, constitute only a very sad remnant of the splendor and extent of these magnificent creatures in the seas of the past. Characteristic of the echinoderms belonging to this group is that they are stalked and sessile throughout their lives or at least for a considerable period during their youth. Where, as in Comatula, the stalk is lost in the course of development, the so-called knob remains as a rudiment on the dorsal side of the calyx. In one order of this class, the Cystoidea, all of which are buried in the oldest formations of our earth's surface, in the Silurian strata, the stalked body is of a simple rounded or oval shape, while in the other order, which comprises the true Crinoidea, special, numerous, articulated arms are found on the upper peripheral edge of the calyx, whose skeletal elements always belong to the perisome and always originate from the dorsal pole.

The second class of echinoderms, which I would like to designate with Latreille's name Actinozoa, is composed of two entirely corresponding orders, the Echinoids and Asteroids. The former, which, through their rounded shape, repeat the shell-like form of the cystoid heads, but differ from them by the presence of the madreporite and movable spines, have a simple dermal skeleton immovably joined together by sutures. The Asteroids, on the other hand, which are provided with arms, like the Crinoids, and with a flattened, disc-shaped body, have, in addition to a less developed dermal skeleton, also a special internal skeleton, which, corresponding to the arms, consists of several articulated rows of segments, which, however, never belong to the perisome and always originate from the ventral side of the disc, specifically from the mouth.

The third class, that of the Scytodermata Brmstr., whose members are primarily distinguished by the location and arrangement of the reproductive organs and internal gills, as well as by the elongated cylindrical shape of the leathery body and the considerable development of the tentacle-like head appendages, comprises the orders Holothuria and Sipunculida. The latter show partly a longer, worm-like shape, and partly a different arrangement of the nervous system, in which a pharyngeal ring and a median ventral nerve cord can be distinguished. A clearer manifestation of lateral symmetry, which attains its most powerful development in the following divisions, is no longer to be overlooked.

It is possible that the others are still present, although very rudimentary, or that, if they are indeed absent in the fully developed state, they are initially laid down symmetrically during the first stages of development and then only later undergo the process of regression. It remains to be determined whether the median ventral cord of the Sipunculids corresponds to only a single one of these radial trunks, or perhaps to two adjacent ones, as is the case in arthropods. The fact that only a single commissure is present in it cannot alone decide this question. We find the same thing in some insects, such as the Diptera, etc. Either two lateral trunks may have completely fused during the course of development, or—which is also not foreign to the type of lateral development—both morphological elements may have been replaced from the very beginning by a single median element. The first question might perhaps be resolved by the anatomical examination of those species of holothurians in which a difference between the ventral and dorsal surfaces already exists (e.g., Psolus, Cuvieria).
