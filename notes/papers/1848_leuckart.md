**[title]** On the Morphology and the Relationships of the Invertebrate Animals. A Contribution to the Characterisation and Classification of Animal Forms. By Dr. Rudolf Leuckart. Braunschweig, printed and published by Friedrich Vieweg and Son. 1848.

**[dedication]** Dedicated to my dear teacher Rudolf Wagner.

The following pages contain the attempt to carry over into the field of zoology a conception and manner of presentation which, since the classic works of H. Rathke and J. Müller, has exercised a significant influence on the shaping of our anatomical knowledge. The more the empirical material gains in mass, the more perceptible becomes a want of unity, the more pressing a need for subordination under more general points of view that govern the detail. Descriptive zoology, the doctrine of the form of the individual animals, must admit the same comparative, the same morphological treatment as anatomy, the doctrine of the formal relations of the individual systems.

This it is that for years has hovered before me in my zoological studies, that has guided me in the composition of the present little work. Well do I know the manifold difficulties and dangers of such a conception. Well do I know how far I have remained from the goal of my striving. Nevertheless I venture to hand my work over to the judgement of public opinion. I am encouraged by the firm conviction that the path I have entered upon will one day open the freest view into the connection of animal structures. —

And now I turn to you, my honoured teacher. I beg your pardon for having set your name before the following sketches. Yet nothing is more agreeable to me than to make public confession of what I owe you. It is you who introduced me into the holy temple of a science before whose gates the boy had already waited with longing to enter, who inspired me through the living word that has flowed from your lips. Your counsel, your support it is that has stood at my side, determining and furthering, everywhere. To the pupil you have granted the rights of a friend. You have received him under your hospitable roof, into the circle of your amiable family.

Gratitude, love and reverence unite in me in the one, the warmest wish for your welfare. May you grow strong, may you be given back to us in the same youthful vigour and freshness with which you have so long and so beneficially worked upon the movements in science and in life.

Rud. Leuckart.

**[p. 1]** *"Bounds are set to animal nature, within which the formative force seems to move in the most wonderful and almost in the most arbitrary way, without being in the least able to break through the circle or to leap over it."* v. Göthe, zur Morphologie Pt. I, p. 156.

The scientific task of general zoology consists partly in proving, through the change of animal shapes, their lawful connection, partly also in gathering the fullness of the diverse prominent and subordinate structures according to their inner content. It answers to a certain aesthetic need of the human mind, which finds its satisfaction less in seeking out possible differences than in the reduction of apparent ones. Not by chance is the rich diversity of organic forms, not accidental and without rule, not without limits. Everywhere there is plan and law, everywhere an inner connection.

What even a superficial view of nature lets us recognise, that the individual animal forms are not all equally different from one another, that they rather agree in many ways, a careful comparison shows in still higher degree. Everywhere we find a definite, lawful relation of shapes, everywhere a greater or lesser kinship of forms. A series of transitions often links the most different structures. Beneath the most manifold variations a definite pervading type reveals itself.

The zoological system in its finished form gives us the complete expression of this inner kinship of animal structures. **[p. 2]** Numerous and manifold have been the attempts at such a presentation. Dependent on the mode of view of the time, on the quantity of the material at hand and the knowledge of it, the value of these attempts is naturally very different. Moreover, in the older zoological systems that properly scientific task, if not exactly mistaken, is mostly subordinated to a certain practical interest. Like a kind of dictionary, they furnish little more than a means of recognising the different animals.

The name Cuvier marks a new and brilliant epoch in the history of our science. With the sure eye of genius this famous investigator recognised the high significance of a more general, I might almost say an artistic, conception of organic structures. What he strove for through the system was not the greatest possible practical usefulness; it was rather an insight into the connection of the different animal forms, the mutual understanding of the scattered diversity. An even attention to the whole outer and inner organisation opened to him a view into the kinships of creatures which would always have remained closed under the older mode of investigation and the grouping of the various shapes, dependent on it, by one or another often superficial and indifferent principle of division.

An immediate gain of the method of investigation applied by Cuvier is the proof that the unfolding of animal shapes does not move in a single ascending direction, not within the bounds of a single common circle. As in works of architecture, so too in organic structures there are different typical manners of building, certain changing norms, which in individual relations admit the most various and manifold modifications without thereby losing a definite characteristic stamp. **[p. 3]** Naturally these different types are themselves not without all connection, all agreement. The very concept of animal organisation, to which they are subordinate, presupposes in them a common sum of definite peculiarities. Moreover, in their most general content the laws of animal form-building are in all cases the same. Only the number and the relation of the points of attack, only the combination of the individual morphogenetic processes that condition the final product, is what varies. In one type this, in another another employment of the formative forces prevails. On the nature of this combination in turn depends the capacity for modification of the resulting form. The mere number of the individual factors brought into play conditions here the greatest differences.

On the other hand we find also, within the different types, manifold analogy in the shaping of individual parts, manifold agreement in the employment of individual forces. And it is precisely in the mass of such coincidences and crossing points that a dangerous reef threatens natural systematics. Only through an all-sided conception of organic form by the hand of developmental history can it be avoided. Near is the time when this science will gain over zoology an influence as highly important as comparative anatomy has exercised since Cuvier. Never, however, may the latter be thrown overboard as useless ballast — as has lately been prophesied of it. It will remain constantly the surest support of zoology; it will, supported and penetrated by developmental history, pave the way to a scientific conception of animal form, to a morphology.

Precisely in the consideration of outer form, which always forms the first object of zoological investigation, the high significance of such a morphological conception shows itself. **[p. 4]** It alone instructs us as to the relative value and the relation of the shape to the general plan of organisation. And only through this do we gain an insight into the kinships of a creature. In itself, likeness in outer appearance by no means entitles us to assume a real agreement. A like form can have a very different meaning and have arisen by the most different path. Only knowledge of development may guide us here. It alone is able to decipher with certainty the value of the morphogenetic processes brought into play and their relation to the ideal ground type.

The modifications of shaping that we meet within the individual typical ground forms of the animal world are everywhere restricted to the relative development of the constructing components united according to a definite plan. Position, form and size, direction, value and number are the changing factors. Here too, however, the room for variation is not without limit. There are definite laws to which the mobility of animal shapes is subject.

We know that every organ of the body, for its full development, must run through a series of states which differ among themselves not inconsiderably. If now development is arrested in any way, the earlier embryonic arrangement and form persists in the structure concerned. So it is in pathological cases in the development of the individual. Of this same law of arrested development creative Nature too makes use infinitely often for the production of the various shapes of a given structure belonging to one or another type. In one creature a form remains which in another forms only a point of passage, necessary for reaching a higher stage of development. Not seldom, however, in a somewhat different way, **[p. 5]** the relations of a particular part are modified in the individual creatures. It too rises only to a certain stage of development; then, however, it does not simply stand still, but becomes the scene of a new process. Often it suffers a retrogression (1) — in other cases it is developed further on a definite, more or less divergent plan.

To a later time is reserved the complete deciphering of those manifold riddles in organic form-building. For now it suffices to know the task of such investigation and to keep it in view. That our efforts will at last be crowned with success is guaranteed by the morphological truth of the content, which speaks with eternal lawfulness in the connection of the individual formal phenomena. Possibly we shall one day succeed not merely in recognising the laws of organic form-building completely, but also in reducing their application in individual cases to certain more or less simple mathematical relations. So far, indeed, there is hardly a prospect of this. Our knowledge of the development and the significance of animal forms is still far too little comprehensive and sure. Far too little do we still understand how to distinguish the accidental from the essential, the conditioned from the conditioning.

Of the highest importance for the morphological treatment of zoology is to prove the individual main types, the architectural styles in the world of animal forms.

Very early in systematic zoology the need made itself felt for larger divisions overtopping the individual classes. The systems of Aristotle, Ray, Linné and so on show this. Yet it was Cuvier **[p. 6]** who first declared with decision that such larger divisions must each stand as the representatives of a definite typical ground form in the animal world, while their subdivisions might offer mere modifications, not essential alterations, of the respective ground plan. But if with our present knowledge of the structure and development of animals we lay such a measure against the system of this master, we can no longer approve it in every respect. The four great divisions (1) of the animal kingdom set up by Cuvier, however much they stand as a monument to their creator admirable for its time, no longer answer today to the demands of a natural systematics. All those divisions, with the exception of the first, the division of the vertebrates, need a lesser or greater reshaping.

Though we are still far from a full understanding of the individual relations among the different animal forms, yet already at this moment much may result as a well-founded outcome of our newer investigations into their morphological connection.

As to what concerns the method of classification first, we have above all to make upon the individual coordinate groups created on a definite principle the demand that they really be of equal value. From this point of view we must first reject as unnatural the usual division of animals into vertebrates and invertebrates, which originally derives from Cuvier (2) and was later much used, especially by Lamarck. Quite **[p. 7]** evidently these two divisions have a very unequal value. While in the former only a single definite building plan expresses itself, in the latter there are found several different ones, each of which deserves to be set beside the type of the vertebrates as of equal value. With the same right with which one sets the vertebrates over against the invertebrates as parallel, one could divide the whole of animal forms into radial and non-radial — as Blainville (1) attempted in his groups (2) of the Actinimorphes and Artiomorphes — into articulated and non-articulated (Milne Edwards), or into head-bearing and headless — as Latreille (3) did with the invertebrate animals when he separated them into Cephalidia (Mollusca and Articulata Cuv.) and Acephala (Zoophyta Cuv.). In all these cases one appeals to the existence of a definite mark belonging to one or another type (which does not even everywhere have the same meaning) and excludes from it the remaining forms lacking this mark — a procedure which, in the establishment of natural groups, not merely of disconnected irrational heaps, may hardly ever be brought into use. If one nevertheless wishes to set up a division of the invertebrate animals, one may thereby, as a very excellent zoologist quite rightly remarks, wish to designate nothing more than "animals other than vertebrates", without in the least regarding them as internally connected forms. It then forms only an indeterminate appendix to a definite group and contains no general concept that would stand over against another general concept (4).

**[p. 8]** In natural systematics one has in general to guard against setting up any divisions held together by merely negative characters. Usually these (as e.g. the Cuvierian division of the Zoophyta (1), or the Burmeisterian main group of the Gastrozoa — Cormozoa Streubel — in which the zoophytes, though without the Entozoa, are joined with the molluscs, and so on) are less natural and quite different in value from the other groups with which they are held together. But even otherwise, with a really natural group, the decision as to its relative value is not in all cases quite easy (2), especially since, because of the greater or lesser formative capacity of the indwelling type, a very different possibility of articulation is present. The morphological dignity of the characteristic marks alone can, in my view, decide here — without regard to the number and the diversity of the forms united in the groups concerned.

Equally insufficient, and likewise to be rejected in setting up the smaller or larger divisions of the animal kingdom, seems to me further every a priori construction, as also the merely one-sided consideration of one or another anatomical system. The former often leads us to a different result from the empirical investigation of the formal phenomena — not because the world of the latter perhaps offered no rational connection answering to the lawfulness of logical thought, but because in such an operation of our mind the most various prejudices so easily make themselves felt. The anatomical arrangement **[p. 9]** of a single organic part or system, however, must for that reason be of very deceptive value in establishing the different groups, especially the larger typical main divisions of the animal kingdom, because, as already said, according to the combination of the individual morphogenetic processes there are found in them manifold crossings and points of coincidence which in themselves by no means allow one to infer a real architectural kinship or agreement. From such points of view we must designate as insufficient both the systems of the so-called natural-philosophical school (according to which e.g. the animals fall into Protozoa, Gasterozoa, Thoracozoa and Cephalozoa), and also those in which quite one-sidedly the constitution either of the outer form (Duméril, de Blainville) or of a definite anatomical system, the nerves (Rudolphi, Grant), the blood (Wilbrand), the respiratory system (Schweigger), the skin and muscles (Oken) and so on, is laid as foundation. Still more unsuitable is it to bring into use as a dividing moment in systematic zoology, which has to consider formal appearance alone, definite physiological characters, as Lamarck did when in his natural history of the invertebrate animals he set up the types of the Animaux apathiques, sensibles and intelligents.

The path which Cuvier has shown us, this path alone promises to lead to the goal. Only an even consideration of the outer habit as well as of the structure and the mutual relation of all the individual anatomical systems in the developed state and during the earlier stages of development, in short an even consideration of the whole morphological character, can lead us to insight into the plan of organisation and thereby to knowledge of the individual natural divisions.

Variously has Nature, in presenting the individual ground forms, made now this, now that part **[p. 10]** the chief bearer of the typical plan. In them, as in the focus the rays of light, the individual characteristic traits of the organisation are gathered. Such parts, now, it is above all that the zoologist, in setting up his groups, will have to keep in view. From them he can in doubtful cases, given the unitary truth of content in animal forms (according to the so-called law of coexistence), infer, in accordance with the analogies present, the more general outline of the whole organisation. Since however, as said, these points of concentration change in the individual types, it is also quite in agreement with formal appearance that now this, now that part is preferably considered in establishing and characterising the individual divisions. Which, however, these prominent marks are, to determine this remains the object of empirical research.

So far as we have hitherto recognised the fullness and diversity of animal forms according to their inner content and in their connection, perhaps the divisions of the *Coelenterata*, the *Echinodermata*, *Vermes*, *Arthropoda*, *Mollusca* and *Vertebrata* may result as the representatives of the different main types of the animal kingdom created by Nature.

The Infusoria we leave out of consideration here. Despite the classic works of Ehrenberg we still know too little of them for us to hold ourselves entitled to an even somewhat founded judgement about them. Very probable, however, it seems to me that the whole division of these enigmatic creatures, at least as a particular group belonging together (1), will later be excluded from the zoological system. **[p. 11]** Many forms reckoned there are certainly merely undeveloped or stunted individuals, others even lowly vegetable organisms (1) or their spores (2). Also, variously and especially in earlier times, the likeness of certain infusoria (apart from the Rotifera, which indisputably have a wholly divergent plan of organisation) to other more highly developed animals, especially to polyps and worms, has been pointed out. Bory de St. Vincent, v. Baer, F. S. Leuckart and others have emphasised such likeness above all in order to support their proposal to dissolve the group of the Infusoria entirely.

To prove the above-mentioned main divisions (so-called provinces or circles) of the animal world natural and well founded according to the state of our present knowledge, to show how they bound themselves off against one another and articulate themselves according to their more essential differences into classes and orders, is the purpose of the following investigation. The vertebrates are passed over in it. They are the most exactly known in their morphological relations and would hardly admit any further doubt of the full justification of their typical division.

First a few words on the mutual position of these individual divisions. In themselves they possess, as the respective representatives of a definite building plan, an equal value. They form just so many parallel series. Yet it cannot be mistaken that in them the most perfectly developed creatures have a very **[p. 12]** unequal rank. That e.g. man offers an infinitely greater perfection than an insect or an annelid hardly needs special mention. It is another thing, however, when within the individual types we descend to the lowest forms, where only the simplest, I might say the rudest, traits of the common morphological plan are offered (1). Even if we do not exactly wish to deny that Amphioxus, for instance, in which the outlines of a vertebrate type are most simply sketched, still stands relatively higher than e.g. a polyp, the same could hardly be maintained in a comparison with Sepia or Octopus.

With every division there begins in Nature, in a manner, a new epoch of formation, in which, however, at the same time the individual phases of development up to the highest bloom of perfection of which the respective epoch is capable must be run through anew. In the totality of those individual epochs, however, a definite, ever more perfecting, artistically self-completing course of development is likewise not to be denied. Ever new, nobler combinations of the form-building processes, more suited to a definite ideal goal, have been tried in them by Nature. — The sequence in which I have set the individual types beside one another above seems to me to answer most to this gradual perfecting of the organic building styles. That most German zoologists hold the type of the Arthropoda to be more developed than that of the Mollusca, and for that reason set the latter below the former, I cannot approve, even if I willingly grant that an insect offers a relatively greater perfection than a tunicate. Not the elegance, the precision and the even completion of the outer structure may decide here, **[p. 13]** but rather the formative capacity of the type. In the latter respect I have no hesitation in declaring the Cephalopoda, which belong to the division of the Mollusca, the most developed forms of all invertebrate animals.

**Coelenterata.**

With this name I designate a great division of the animal kingdom newly set up by me some time ago (1), which comprises a part of the Cuvierian polyps (Anthozoa Ehrenberg) together with the Acalephae; a division which, because of the sharp bounding of a common type outward as well as its articulation inward, certainly deserves by right to be set beside the other main groups of animal forms. As is known, Cuvier, the creator of our modern systematics, gathered the polyps and acalephs, as two coordinate classes (2), with the infusoria, intestinal worms and echinoderms in the great circle of the Zoophyta or Animalia radiata, in whose relations of organisation he believed he recognised a definite common type. Despite the contradiction to which this union was exposed from some quarters, it has nevertheless down to the most recent time found great recognition and manifold imitation (the Animaux apathiques Lam., Acephala Latr., Asphycta Ehrbg. answer wholly to the Cuvierian zoophytes). According to what we now **[p. 14]** know of these animals, however, the division of the Zoophyta can no longer stand, even if one separates from it the Entozoa, as has been proposed from several sides. There still remain united in it several typical main groups very different from one another, of which, as said, we believe we have found one in the division of our Coelenterata. What especially characterises them is partly the completely radial form of the body, partly also the peculiar arrangement of the body cavity, which radiates from the central axis to the periphery and is connected with the digestive apparatus through a wide opening in the bottom of the simple stomach tube, when such a one is present at all. Nervous system, sense organs and genitalia show the same radial (1) grouping which expresses itself outwardly in the form of the body. Characteristic, as it seems to me, for the Coelenterata is also the position of the nervous system in the hind end of the body, at the bottom of the body cavity. Here — in the sole of the foot — it lies in the Actiniae (cf. Leç. d'anat. comp. par Cuvier, N. Ed. T. III, p. 376), where Spix already found it, here in the Ctenophora. The position in the Discophora (in the periphery of the bell-shaped body) is also essentially the same. A pharyngeal ring or nuchal ganglion is constantly lacking (2).

How little the inner agreement of the Anthozoa and Acalephae has hitherto been considered is shown by the **[p. 15]** great applause with which the union of the acalephs with the echinoderms (as Radiaires mollasses and Radiaires échinodermes) deriving from Lamarck has been received by the newer (German) zoologists. Latreille and recently also Sars (1) are, to my knowledge, the only ones who have pronounced on the close kinship (2) of the polyps and acalephs (from which two the former created his class of the Phytodacea) (3), yet without more closely designating the limits of the common formative type which lies at the base of both. For it is by no means the whole group of the polyps, in the usual extent, which admits a union with the acalephs. Already the investigations of Ehrenberg (4), as of Milne Edwards and Audouin (5), have shown us how one had formerly gathered under the polyps two quite different animal forms, which differ from one another completely, especially through the organisation of the intestinal canal, the body cavity and the genital apparatus. Ehrenberg separated both as Bryozoa and Anthozoa and distributed (6) them under two different groups of his Asphycta, setting the former among the tube-animals (with unbranched intestine), the latter beside the acalephs among the grape-animals (with branched intestine). Milne Edwards (7) also separated the Bryozoa from the proper polyps and joined them as Ascidioidea (or Bryozoaires) with the tunicates.

**[p. 16]** On the natural position of the Bryozoa we shall have more to mention later. For the moment it suffices to have emphasised their typical difference from the polyps, from which they must be separated with the same right with which one parts the Tubulibranchiata and Dentalia from the Serpulacea.

The same holds of the so-called Foraminifera or Polythalamia, which, on Ehrenberg's statement (1) that they agree very much in their organisation with the Bryozoa, are likewise sometimes joined with the polyps. They too must without all doubt be separated from them. Their nearest kin they find, as especially Dujardin (2) has so aptly shown, not among the cephalopods, as d'Orbigny (3) thought, or among the Capitibranchiata, where Johnston (4) placed them, but among the infusoria in the groups of the Amoebaea and Arcellina.

So there remain to us of the polyps only the so-called Anthozoa, yet these too not in the whole extent originally given them by Ehrenberg (5). For here it has resulted, through the interesting discoveries on the development of the medusae, that a whole group of these animals, the family of the so-called Hydroida (Exoarii Rapp, Sertulariens M. Edw., Anthozoa oligactinia Ehrbg.), the same which — with the exclusion of Hydra — because of many peculiar relations of organisation was in a later writing of Ehrenberg (6) separated as the family of the Dimorphaea from the proper Anthozoa **[p. 17]** and set nearer the Bryozoa, comprises no developed animals at all, but mere preparatory generations of medusae, so-called nurse-animals (1).

The true Anthozoa alone remaining of the polyps form, with the acalephs — of which moreover, as we shall see further below, likewise certain definite forms, being mere nurse-animals, must vanish from zoological systematics — my division of the Coelenterata.

As to their further division, the *polyps* and *acalephs* appear in it as two very natural classes. The characteristic difference of the two rests in a different development of the body cavity (2). In the former it is very roomy and traversed only by some lamellar longitudinal partitions of the outer coverings, which radiate from the periphery to the centre, while in the others it is much less wide and in the form of radial vessels (as the so-called water-bearing vascular system) traverses the parenchymatous body mass. In the animals of the first class, one might perhaps express it, the enveloping body wall grows centripetally into the body cavity; in the animals of the second the body cavity grows centrifugally into the body wall. In both cases the radial arrangement is unmistakable, even though the numerical relations in which it is stamped vary considerably. Very generally, however, the number four with its multiples seems to prevail in the structures radiating from the central axis or at least grouped radially about it.

**[p. 18]** A sure guarantee for the inner typical connection of these two classes, and so also for the justification of our division of the Coelenterata, is offered, beside the morphological agreement mentioned, also by the development of the animals belonging there, which in part shall be considered more exactly later. Here let the simple remark suffice that the acalephs, so far as we know their developmental history, everywhere present in their young or nurse states a perfect polyp form. In this relation shows itself the same important law of which we have for a long time already had knowledge in other natural divisions — especially the vertebrates — according to which, namely, the higher-standing animals of a group run through in development definite forms which persist for life in the lower animals of the same group (1). If now we recognise in the former the highest unfolding of a definite ideal type, we can under such relations regard the lower forms — in relation to those — as arrested in their development, and designate the whole morphogenetic process of which Nature makes use in such cases as the process of arrested formation, as has also been done above.

If before the special consideration of the two classes of the Coelenterata we cast a glance at the outer relations of form of the various animals belonging to them, we cannot in these either mistake, despite the manifold modifications, a definite agreement. The ground form of the Coelenterata is the form of a globe or an egg, as we perceive it in most comb-jellies and also still in the Actiniae. If **[p. 19]** this stretches in length, there results from it a cylinder, as in most polyps (a form which, to be sure, is very often more or less effaced by the imperfect budding of these animals), while by the opposite process, by a flattening from the poles, the disc form of the Discophora (1) can very easily be derived. The mouth opening lies constantly at the front (according to the position of the animal the upper or lower) end of the body in the central axis, which here, moreover, not seldom, especially in the flattened body of the disc-jellies, has lengthened into a more or less developed stalk. In the circuit of the mouth opening (on the edge of the head disc; in the disc-jellies in the periphery of the proper body) there usually stand larger or smaller cylindrical processes in very different number, the so-called tentacles, whose inner cavity as a rule communicates with the common body cavity.

Quite peculiar structures, morphologically very divergent from these tentacles, are the paired fishing threads of the Ctenophora.

So much on the Coelenterata in general. As to the first class of them, the polyps, their systematics, despite the manifold attempts of our **[p. 20]** newer zoologists, especially Ehrenberg, Johnston (1) and Dana (2), still lies very much in disorder. Their organisation and the remarkable phenomena of their multiplication by imperfect division and budding, the same phenomena on which the structure and connection of the polyp stocks (3) rest, are at the moment still too incompletely known for a classification based on them to be able to satisfy all our demands. So much, however, seems to me certain: the polyps fall according to their structure (4) into two coordinate orders (5), to one of which, which contains by far the greatest majority of these animals, the name *Anthozoa* may remain, while the other, which alone up to now comprises the gen. Lucernaria, I should like to designate as cup-polyps, *Cylicozoa* (6). How unnatural it is to range these latter among the Anthozoa, and especially, as usually happens, in the family of the Actiniae, has already been felt more than once. Lamarck (7) and Cuvier (8) too placed them (the latter, to be sure, together with the Actiniae) among the acalephs, and really they have, as Sars already remarks, a greater likeness in several respects with these, especially with the umbrella-jellies. **[p. 21]** Nevertheless they must in my judgement remain with the polyps. The relation of the body walls to the very roomy body cavity is the same as in the other polyps, and precisely on a different relation of these parts rests, after all, the difference of the Coelenterata belonging to this class from the acalephs.

The Actiniae are likewise polyps, and indeed Anthozoa, although they too have been several times associated with other groups. So Cuvier at first brought them (like the Zoanthina and Lucernariae) to the acalephs (1) (as A. fixes), Lamarck (2) by contrast, as also Oken (3) and Schweigger (4), to the echinoderms, where the first even formed out of them and the Sipunculida a common group, that of the Fistulida.

The two orders of the polyps which we have just set up differ above all by a different behaviour of their digestive apparatus. In the former, the Anthozoa, there is a particular stomach tube, a short cylinder which projects from the mouth opening into the cylindrical body and at the bottom communicates by a wide opening with the roomy body cavity, which, as already mentioned above, is divided by a greater or lesser number of radial partitions fastened to the stomach sac into a corresponding number of peripheral pockets or blind sacs. The free edge of those partitions bears the structures described by me as mesenterial filaments, behind which, likewise on the partitions, the genital apparatus is placed. The Cylicozoa **[p. 22]** by contrast lack such a stomach tube. The whole body cavity, especially perhaps the front part, the so-called mouth part projecting from the head disc of the cup-shaped body furnished behind with a stalk-like foot, forms the digestive apparatus. Radial partitions of the body cavity are found here too, though in somewhat different arrangement. The sexual organs lie at the sides of these partitions, embedded in the head disc. The mesenterial filaments of the Anthozoa have become free tentacle-shaped threads, which with their base insert themselves on the partitions at the place of transition of the front body disc into the stalk-shaped hind body.

The formation of a skeleton (1) is restricted in the class of the polyps, and indeed in the whole division of the Coelenterata, to the Anthozoa, but is found here in greatest extent. The possibility of this formation rests solely on the quantity of lime salts present in the body of these animals. Mostly the skeleton is an outer one, arisen from the hardening or calcification of the body coverings, in which, however, sometimes (in the Madrepores) the inner muscular partitions of the body cavity also take part. Much less widespread is the occurrence of an inner so-called core scaffold, which, as it seems, is found preferably only in the smaller colony-forming forms and here serves as support of the common animal stock. This inner skeleton runs constantly in the axis of these stocks and is without proper connection with the individual animals, although it may indisputably likewise be regarded merely as their product.

The second class of our Coelenterata comprises, as mentioned, the acalephs. Yet these too cannot **[p. 23]** be received here in their whole extent. For as the most important investigations of Sars (1) have shown us in most recent time, the one group of them, that of the Siphonophora, which one hitherto regarded as developed animals, contains, quite like the group of the Hydroida, only so-called preparatory or nursing generations of other acalephs. For the family of the Velellidae, which Eschscholtz likewise reckons to the Siphonophora, such a relation has indeed still to be proved, yet it seems very probable for them too by analogy with the other forms.

If now we exclude the Siphonophora, as undeveloped forms, from the acalephs, there remain to us among these animals only the two groups of the *Ctenophora* and *Discophora*, set up by the excellent Eschscholtz (2) beside those, which in my judgement form two very natural orders. Both differ in their organisation in quite the same way as the two orders of the polyps set up by me above. Only the Ctenophora have a proper stomach tube (if one leaves out of account the rudiment of it occurring in some disc-jellies), of quite the same structure as in the Anthozoa. Even in their shape the comb-jellies might soonest be compared with the Anthozoa, while the Discophora also thereby rather join the Lucernariae, with which they likewise agree in the want of a particular stomach. The whole body cavity is here the seat of chymification, though preferably perhaps the front section lying between the mouth lobes. — Even the position of the generative organs shows in the corresponding orders of the acalephs and polyps a great analogy. In the Ctenophora, which moreover are hermaphrodites and not, **[p. 24]** like the other Coelenterata, of separate sex, they lie — answering in a manner to the lamellar longitudinal partitions of the Anthozoa — in the side parts of the body; in the Discophora, as in Lucernaria, in the front surface of the body disc.

By their capacity for locomotion, moreover, the acalephs are very different from the polyps. For while these either sit fast by the hind part of the body, or at most can only push themselves slowly forward by help of the hind body disc (the so-called foot of the Actiniae) or the tentacles (Arachnitis Sars), the acalephs in the developed state, where they constantly appear as isolated individuals and never form connected animal stocks, show a very free mobility. In the Discophora this is mediated by the rhythmic contractions of the umbrella- or bell-shaped body disc. In the order of the Ctenophora, by contrast, it had to be made possible in another way because of the divergent globular shape of the body (which is only rarely somewhat altered by a lateral compression — in Cestum). Here there is found a particular apparatus of cilia, destined expressly for this purpose, set in several (4 or 8) longitudinal rows on the periphery of the body, which by their vibrations move the body along.

On the development of the comb-jellies we unfortunately as yet know nothing at all — which is the more to be regretted as the remarkable phenomena relating to this process in the disc-jellies perhaps allow one to infer analogous relations in those. The disc-jellies arise from a polyp-shaped nurse-animal, now by the division of the original body into several disc-shaped sections lying one over another, now also by buds which sprout forth on the nurses. In the latter case, where the bodies of the nurses do not, as in the former, perish directly through the process of multiplication, **[p. 25]** the individual preparatory individuals (mostly joined with one another into very developed animal stocks) as a rule outlast several generations and, strikingly, are even capable of an independent sexual propagation (1). In such cases, now, the nurses of the medusae have been entered in the system by zoologists down to the most recent time as definite animal forms of their own. Yet certainly wrongly. Even if they offer in their morphological relations many interesting and striking phenomena, they can as little find a special place in a natural system of the animal kingdom as the larvae of insects, even when these differ ever so much from the forms of the developed animals concerned.

Best known among these nurse forms are the so-called Hydroida. In the constitution and the form of the body they resemble, as already stated, the polyps. In this, however, they differ from these, that they neither possess a separate stomach tube, nor do those lamellar longitudinal partitions project into the body cavity, which takes up the whole inner space of the body, which belong to all developed Coelenterata. Mesenterial filaments and inner sexual organs are also lacking. The body cavity forms an oblong cylinder which traverses the body, opens upward, as in the Discophora, by a mouth opening, and in Hydra (though, as it seems, only here) also extends into the arms.

This type, which one has long since recognised in Hydra (2), **[p. 26]** is found also in all the other Hydroida. The body cavity (1) forms the digestive and at the same time the circulatory apparatus, without help of a particular anatomically different organ. That besides it (in Hydra) a formal stomach tube occurs, as v. Siebold (2) states, or rather that the front section of the body cavity, so far as it lies in the so-called head, is also anatomically to be conceived as stomach, of this I have not been able to convince myself. The body cavity indeed shows a particular lining different from the outer coverings (for which reason I must share v. Siebold's doubts about the accuracy of the known Rösel experiments, according to which a polyp turned inside out like a glove finger could still digest completely in this state), but this does not yet entitle us to the assumption that this lining belongs to a stomach sac of its own. On the contrary, the circumstance that it lies directly, without an interval, on the inner surface of the body walls, and that also (in Hydra) the cavity of the arms stands in direct connection with the enclosed space, speaks for the older view. The latter relation would, if that space really represented a particular stomach, be without analogy in the whole division of the Coelenterata. Everywhere the cavity of the tentacles communicates only with the body cavity. The opening through which, according to Siebold, the stomach of Hydra is said to stand in connection at its bottom with the narrow, tubular cavity of the cylindrical foot lying behind it, I can only hold for a sphincter-like narrowing of the body cavity, which in other Hydroida (e.g. in the Sertulariae) is still more strongly marked and **[p. 27]** divides the whole space into two sections, a front one, which perhaps exclusively digests, and a hind one, which perhaps is preferably circulatory apparatus. Very distinctly, however, one can perceive, especially in the hydroid colonies, that the two are in no way different from one another. — Only on the hinder of these sections do the buds develop by which the Hydroida propagate asexually and develop into a colony, when the young formed from the buds, as is mostly the case, do not separate completely from the mother animal.

The same typical arrangement is shown by the Siphonophora, at least the Physophoridae (1) and Diphyidae, which agree essentially completely with one another and therefore are also only very wrongly regarded by zoologists as two different families (2) coordinate with the Velellidae. In them too there is found a simple body cavity which traverses the body, not, as has been assumed, a particular stomach and a water-vascular system separate from it. The difference from the Hydroida rests in this, that partly the tentacles of the cylindrical body in the circuit of the mouth opening have vanished, partly however also a particular system of fishing threads (answering to the fishing threads of the comb-jellies) and — in agreement with the way of life — a peculiar, **[p. 28]** powerfully developed locomotor apparatus has been added. The latter consists of a differing number of bell-shaped swimming bladders with cartilage-like envelope and an inner contractile sac, whose contractions move the body along in an analogous way as the disc- or umbrella-like body does the Discophora. This apparatus is set at the hind or — if we consider the position of the animal — the upper end of the body, which morphologically answers to the fixed foot of the Hydroida and Anthozoa.

In the Diphyidae there are only two such swimming bells, in the other Physophoridae (with the exception of Physalia) a greater number. In the former they mostly lie somewhat one behind the other and take the proper animal body between them, yet so that its last, blind end (the juice cavity according to Eschscholtz) extends a stretch into the cartilaginous envelope of the front swimming bell (the so-called suction-tube piece). In those Diphyidae which really are only simple animals, in Ersaea e.g., the swimming pieces form by far the greatest part of the body. The proper body is only very short and hardly projects beyond the edge of those structures. It consists partly of the hind embedded part, partly of the front free part into which that continues, a part which is furnished at its end with the mouth opening and bears among zoologists the name of the suction tube. At the place of transition of the two sections, which here too, as in the Hydroida, is somewhat narrowed, sit the fishing threads.

In many other animal forms belonging here, as in Diphyes, the hind part of the body continues beyond the place of insertion of the first suction tube into a longer canal (the so-called reproductive canal), on which toward the front a greater number of suction tubes sit, with fishing threads at the base and mostly also each with a particular cartilaginous covering shield (which is lacking in Abyla and Cymba, hence **[p. 29]** inessential). These individual suction tubes arise only gradually through a continued budding.

As soon as by an unprejudiced investigation one has reached the result that this swimming apparatus of the Diphyidae is without essential significance for the morphological conception of these animals, one must under the given relations recognise a creature like Diphyes as an animal stock, as a colony of individuals — although this way of viewing contradicts the usual assumption. Such a creature stands the comparison with a polyp stock in every respect. Each of the individual animals (suction tubes) has its body and its body cavity, each its fishing threads and covering shields. Common to them all is a stem (the reproductive canal) — whose inner cavity, as in the Hydroida, connects the body cavities of the individual animals with one another — and at its end that peculiar locomotor apparatus, which one might perhaps not quite unsuitably compare with the stalk of the Halopteridae.

Very easily now can the form of the other Physophoridae be derived from that of Diphyes. In Agalma, Agalmopsis and others too the upper part of the common body (the swimming column) with its cavity, in which the so-called swimming bladder certainly has only a very subordinate significance (1), is set with the cartilaginous locomotor organs, **[p. 30]** while the lower free part, as there too, bears a very considerable number of suction tubes with fishing threads etc. In Physophora this lower free part of the body shortens. From a long and narrow cylinder it becomes a short and wide sac (1), from whose lower surface then the numerous suction tubes, fishing threads etc. hang down side by side — still a sign that the form in question represents a compound animal stock (2). In the gen. Physalia, finally, the front part of the common body, the swimming column with its cartilaginous locomotor instruments, has been completely lost (3). The whole body is (like the lower part in Physophora) a simple large bladder, to which below the individual animals with their appendages are fastened.

As to the Velellidae, we still know far too little of these animals, in regard both to their organisation and to their development, for us to be able to determine anything with certainty already now about their position and their kinships. If the statements of Hollard (4) on their structure should be confirmed, it seems to me even doubtful that they — if they really are developed animals — belong to the type of the Coelenterata, although on the other hand again very much **[p. 31]** seems to speak for such an assumption and especially for a kinship with the Physophoridae. Later careful investigations must decide on this.

**Echinodermata.**

The spiny-skinned animals or echinoderms, which were first (1) recognised by Cuvier in their mutual connection and in the opinion of this great zoologist form, in the division of the radiate animals, a class standing equal to the polyps, acalephs and helminths, are likewise, as it seems to me, the representatives of a definite type of their own in the form-rich world of the invertebrate animals (2). Like the Coelenterata, the worms, arthropods, molluscs and vertebrates, they too must in my view be regarded as a connected, variously articulated main division among the animal forms.

With the Coelenterata, on which they border on one side, they share the peculiar radial structure of the body, which however in them is marked partly by the prevalence of the number five (instead of the number four usual in the Coelenterata), partly also already shows manifold greater fluctuations than is ever the case there. In agreement with the structure of the body, in the echinoderms too the mouth lies at the front pole of the central longitudinal axis. What however **[p. 32]** strictly parts the animals belonging here from the Coelenterata is especially the arrangement of the digestive apparatus. Everywhere there is found a considerable intestinal canal, isolated from the body cavity, which is now closed blind behind, now however also leads outward through a particular anus. As a rule this lies, opposite the mouth opening, at the hind end of the central axis of the body. Not seldom, however, it also shows a lesser or greater eccentricity in its position. Hardly, moreover, is this divergence from the proper norm, which is quite analogous to the manifold asymmetries of animals with lateral type, founded in an original difference of the formative laws conditioning the radial type, but rather merely in a later modification of them through other morphological processes (1); the change of position is without doubt a secondary one. Beside the intestinal canal, which is fastened along its length by a mesentery in the roomy undivided body cavity, there is found everywhere also a particular blood-vascular system, which is likewise lacking in the Coelenterata and is there represented by the body cavity. Its arrangement, as also that of the nervous system and the genital apparatus (2), is, in accordance with the type, a radial one. Only in the long-stretched forms of the holothurians, which especially through the Sipunculida approach the worms, is it, in part at least, effaced and combined with definite relations which otherwise belong only to animals with laterally symmetrical type **[p. 33]** (1). The same lateral arrangement shows itself in the holothurians also in the structure of the inner gills.

As we have above stated for the structure of the nervous system in the Coelenterata a definite typical agreement, so we find it also in the echinoderms. In them the central parts of this apparatus form a pentagonal ring, which embraces the upper part of the intestinal canal and sends out from its individual corners the main nerve trunks.

Even if in this a new fundamental difference of the echinoderms from the Coelenterata expresses itself, yet on the other hand there show themselves again various analogies and agreements. To these belongs especially the wealth of lime salts in the outer coverings common to the echinoderms with the Anthozoa. Not seldom these salts are deposited in the strangest forms (2). They also usually harden the envelope of the body into a very firm skeleton with manifold hook- or spine-shaped processes. Even the inner **[p. 34]** parts calcify at times. In order nevertheless to make locomotion possible for the echinoderms, there are found beside these numerous contractile little feet (ambulacra) grouped in longitudinal rows on the periphery of the body, which serve for attachment and on contraction draw the body after them.

The manifold differences in which the outer form of the echinoderms appears can be explained in the same way as in the Coelenterata, from a central lengthening or depression (1) of the original globular shape (persisting in the Cystidea and Echini). What however distinguishes the echinoderms from the Coelenterata in this is the tendency to form manifold radial processes of the peripheral body edge, the so-called arms, which must be strictly distinguished morphologically from the tentacles of the Coelenterata. Their development in the individual echinoderms is, moreover, very different. From the longitudinally running ridges on the body of many holothurians (the pentagonal forms of the gen. Pentacta) to the blunt corners of many asterids (the species belonging to Schweigger's division Corpore angulato) and the hollow arms of the Asteracanthia etc., from these to the solid, jointed, here and there even branched arms of the ophiurans and crinoids there is a gradual transition. These ray-shaped arms are immediate radiations and continuations of the body (as among the disc-jellies the lobe-shaped continuations of the body in the gen. Ephyra), which grow out of it only gradually (2) in the course of development, not **[p. 35]** however particular independent appendages, like the tentacles of the Coelenterata, which in an analogous way also occur in the echinoderms and are here found again in the so-called outer gills of the Echini and holothurians in the circuit of the mouth opening.

The majority of the echinoderms belonging to the group of the so-called Crinoidea are attached by means of a calcareous stalk which goes out from the hind pole of the body, opposite the mouth opening. The other echinoderms are free, yet usually not in all stages of their life. In their young states they are rather mostly fastened in a quite analogous way. So the Comatulae (according to Thompson (1), who at first described the undeveloped forms of C. mediterranea as Pentacrinus europaeus), so also the Echini (according to Dufossé (2)). The rudiment of the stalk serving for attachment is in the Comatulae the so-called knob, in the Echini the madreporite plate. Since the latter moreover belongs likewise to the Asteriae (as among the ophiurans (3) to the gen. Euryale), it becomes thereby already probable from the outset that these too are attached in their youth. Really it is so, as the observations of Sars (4) have shown us, which together with the interesting discoveries of J. Müller (5) on the development of the ophiurans yield many striking results for the morphology of these animals and their relation to the forms considered before.

Indisputably the place answering to the point of attachment is the same as in those forms, the hind body pole therefore, and the perpendicular axis going out from it the longitudinal axis of the animal, which coincides with the central axis of the Echini, Crinoidea etc. Now, however, the flattening of the body in the embryos of those Asteriae begins, strikingly, not in this longitudinal axis but from the sides, so that the central axis of the developed animal does not, as is otherwise the case, coincide with the proper longitudinal axis, but cuts it at a right angle. The longitudinal axis of the body goes, then, in the developed Asteriae in a straight direction through the madreporite plate, which, as said, answers to the original point of attachment, and the midpoint of the disc. In a surprising way the acute supposition of Agassiz on the lateral symmetry of the asterids, on the real presence of a front and hind end (divergent from the central axis), thus finds its confirmation in developmental history. Anew, however, it is to be seen from such relations how a definite plan in the organisation can be realised even under altered conditions. Despite the divergent direction the body of the Asteriae develops completely according to the laws of the radial type.

**[p. 37]** Even though the observations of Sars prove with evidence such a remarkable difference between the longitudinal and central axis of the body in the manner just stated in the Asteriae, this behaviour nevertheless needs closer attention and understanding, the more so as in the development of the echinoderms, as it seems, many very divergent relations are also otherwise found. So Müller discovered that the ophiurans and some armless spiny-skinned echinoderms, which he at first took for Echini, but which probably — since in these another mode of development has been observed — may prove to be spatangids, do not form themselves simply by a gradual metamorphosis out of the yolk mass of the egg, but are subject to a most striking alternation of generations. Their embryos, namely, are formed, like an embroidery in a frame, in the interior of a strange frame-like nurse-animal (Pluteus paradoxus), which consists of four skeleton-like longitudinal rods connected among themselves and furnished with a skin-like coat, has a stomach with (eccentric) mouth opening, and is outwardly covered by a ciliated coat by whose help it swims about freely. The relative position of the embryo to this nurse-animal is similar to that of those young asterids to the attachment structures mentioned above. In general the analogy of these parts with that nurse-animal seems to me so great that I cannot suppress the supposition that the two agree in their morphological significance. The difference between them would then consist solely in the relatively very different development, and reduce itself to this, that in the asterids that apparatus is formed together with the embryo, in a manner as a mere appendage of it, out of the yolk mass, while in the other case it proceeds alone from the metamorphosis of the yolk and can therefore also develop into an independent, freely mobile animal. That moreover also otherwise **[p. 38]** at times that apparatus does not serve for attachment and can reach a considerable size in a divergent way is shown by the discovery (2) of Sars, recently confirmed (1) by Koren and Danielssen, that the creature formerly described by him as Bipinnaria asterigera is only a developing starfish furnished with a large swimming apparatus.

Much is it to be regretted that our present knowledge of the development of the echinoderms is not more comprehensive, that especially of the holothurians we still know nothing more than that they are, young, of the size of a barleycorn and like a whitish maggot (3). Certainly a later investigation will here still furnish us many most important disclosures on the morphology and the connection of the different groups.

As to the systematics of the echinoderms, Cuvier, as is known, divided them according to the presence or the want of the ambulacra into Echinodermata pedicellata (with the families of the asterids, echinids and holothurians) and Echinodermata apoda (with the Sipunculida). However, the structures on whose constitution this division rests evidently have far too small a typical significance for them to be set before the other more pervasive and more important relations of organisation which express themselves in the anatomical arrangement of the entrails and the skeleton. Much more noteworthy in this respect is the arrangement of Latreille, who separated from the proper echinoderms (the Asteriae, Echinida and Crinoida) the holothurians (Scutoderma Schulz, Scytodermata Brmstr.) as a particular group and out of both, in connection with the tunicates, created his class of the Actinozoa, standing equal to the Phytodacea. How unnatural, however, the union **[p. 39]** of the tunicates with the Cuvierian echinoderms is, is easily evident. Nor has this union probably ever found any applause, while the first two orders set up by Latreille have otherwise been variously accepted in the same extent, as especially by Burmeister (1). Unfortunately, however, the latter joins with these two orders also the acalephs (following the precedent of Lamarck), which indisputably, as I believe I have shown, belong together with the polyps.

According to another principle of division, according to the difference of outer form, Lamarck formed out of the division of the echinoderms the orders of the Stellerida (asterids), Echinida and Fistulida, of which the last, as already mentioned above, contained beside the holothurians and Sipunculida also the Actiniae. The Crinoidea, which had been placed by Cuvier among the asterids, Lamarck separated completely from the echinoderms in order to range them — with the exception of Comatula s. Alecto, which remained with the asterids (2) — with the polyps, and indeed the Halopteridae; a procedure which, moreover, now that we have come to know the organisation of these animals more closely through Miller (3) and especially through J. Müller (4), hardly deserves a special refutation any more. These very investigations, however, have shown us that the Crinoidea may by no means be united with the asterids, that they rather form a particular group, distinguished among other things by fundamental differences in skeleton formation.

Thus the Cuvierian echinoderms would fall into four, or, if one, as has been done several times especially in recent time, regards the Ech. apoda (Sipunculacea **[p. 40]** Brdt., Gephyrea de Quatref.) also as a particular group, into five orders, which by many zoologists are also really set side by side as natural and coordinate. Now, these groups may indeed, as I willingly grant, be natural; coordinate they hardly are in my opinion.

First, as Streubel has already proposed (1), the asterids and echinids must be united with one another into a common larger division. Their very close kinship is proved already by developmental history. In their earliest stages Asteriae and Echini, and especially ophiurans and spatangids, show a very striking analogy. The former are, as mentioned, attached by help of a particular stalk-shaped apparatus, while in the latter the remarkable nurses and the first embryonic forms of the permanent animals agree completely. The differences appearing later in development belong each to the special plan of one or another group of this division. Even the circumstance that, as we have stated above, in the Asteriae a change in the direction of the central axis occurs, which serves the radial type in a manner as point of attack, cannot keep us from a closer union of the animals concerned. Otherwise we should probably also have to remove the Spatangi from the order of the echinids. Moreover the essential agreement in the anatomical behaviour of the individual organs also justifies the union of the two groups into a common larger division. We need only think the body of an Echinus strongly flattened in the direction of its central axis to get a blunt-cornered starfish, with opposite central mouth and anal opening, with a nerve and vascular ring radiating to the five corners, with an equally radially grouped sexual system **[p. 41]** and locomotor apparatus. That in the asterids these tentacle-like ambulacra are found only on the upper mouth surface repeats itself in an analogous way also in the Spatangi, where they are restricted to the dorsal side. Quite uniformly, moreover, there are found in echinids and spatangids, and indeed only in them, those remarkable grasping instruments, the pedicellariae. Equally restricted to both groups is likewise the occurrence of a madreporite plate. The differences expressed in the skeletal structure are not greater than in the individual orders and groups of other classes, as e.g. of the polyps, where likewise now a merely outer, now at the same time also an inner skeletal system appears.

Unnatural seems to me, in the above-mentioned division of the echinoderms into five coordinate orders, also the complete separation of the Sipunculida from the holothurians. It cannot indeed be mistaken that in the former of these animals the radial arrangement, which otherwise comes forth so strikingly, vanishes still further than in the latter — as expresses itself especially in the structure of the nervous system, which is built almost quite as in the annelids and arthropods —, yet this, I believe, alone does not yet entitle us to separate the Sipunculida completely from the holothurians and even, as has been several times proposed in recent time, to range them among the worms. There still remain manifold very important approaches to the holothurians. The occurrence of inner gill sacs (in Echiurus and Thalassema) and of a mesentery, the position of the genitalia in the fore part of the body are relations which the Sipunculida share with the true holothurians and which in part are completely foreign to the type of the worms. That in the Sipunculida the ambulacra are lacking one may not rate too highly; in Synapta too they are missed, although this without doubt belongs to the holothurians. In general the occurrence of such locomotor organs is made necessary solely by the **[p. 42]** rigid constitution of the outer coverings. Where this has ceased to appear as a formal skeleton, where the body envelopes are soft and pliant (as precisely in Synapta and the Sipunculida), there they have lost their significance and can be lacking.

The three larger groups of the echinoderms arising from such a union of the echinids and asterids on the one hand, and of the holothurians and Sipunculida on the other, which have in the same way already been set up by Streubel, we can, as I believe, regard according to their systematic significance as so many classes, which stand equal to the polyps and acalephs in the division of the Coelenterata and each in turn fall into individual orders.

The first of these three classes, for which I should like to propose the designation *Pelmatozoa* (1), comprises a great series of forms of the former world, whose few still living representatives, as one of our greatest geologists remarks, form only a very sad remnant of the splendour and the spread of these magnificent creatures in the seas of the former world. Characteristic for the echinoderms belonging here is that they are stalked and sit fast for life, or at least for a longer time in youth. Where, as in Comatula, the stalk is lost in the course of development, there remains, as rudiment, on the back of the calyx the so-called little knob (2). In the one order of this class, the *Cystidea* (3), which all lie buried in the oldest formations of our earth's surface, in the Silurian strata, the stalked body is of simple roundish **[p. 43]** or oval form, while in the other order, which comprises the proper *Crinoidea*, there are found on the upper peripheral edge of the calyx also particular numerously jointed arms, whose skeletal pieces always belong to the perisome and always take their origin from the dorsal pole.

The second class of the echinoderms, which I should like to designate with the Latreillean name of the *Actinozoa*, is composed of two quite corresponding orders, the *Echinida* and *Asterida*. The former, which by their roundish shape repeat the shell-like form of the cystidean heads, but differ from them by the madreporite plate and the occurrence of mobile spines, have a simple dermal skeleton immovably joined by sutures. The asterids by contrast, which are furnished with arms, like the Crinoidea, and with a flat, disc-shaped body, have besides a less developed dermal skeleton also a particular inner skeleton, which, answering to the arms, consists of several articulately connected rows of joints, which however never belong to the perisome and always go out from the ventral side of the disc, and indeed from the mouth.

The third class, that of the *Scytodermata* Brmstr., whose members are distinguished preferably by the position and arrangement of the sexual organs and inner gills, as well as by the stretched cylindrical form of the leathery body and the considerable development of the tentacle-like head appendages (1), comprises as orders the *Holothuriae* and *Sipunculida*. The latter show partly a longer, worm-shaped figure, partly also a divergent arrangement of the nervous system, in which one distinguishes a pharyngeal ring and a middle ventral cord (2). A more distinct **[p. 44]** emergence of lateral symmetry, which in the following divisions attains its mightiest development, can no longer be mistaken.

**Vermes.**

As is known, Linné (as earlier already Aristotle) set up in the series of animals a particular class of the worms. However, this by no means answers in its extent to the group of the Vermes as we here set it up as a typical main division of animal forms. The Linnaean worms comprise, with the exception of the arthropods (Insecta Lin.), all invertebrate creatures.

A greater agreement with our division **[p. 45]** is shown by the order of the Intestina set up by Linné in his class of the Vermes, yet this too contains single forms foreign to the type of the worms (such as Myxine and Teredo), while others, which we believe we must by right claim for it, are incorporated apart from it partly in the order of the Testacea (Serpula), partly in that of the Mollusca (Nereis and Aphrodite). How isolated, moreover, these latter stand here was already felt by Pallas (1), who first, on an exact investigation of the whole structure, recognised the inner connection of these forms and made the proposal to assign them another place. United with the intestinal worms they are by O. Fr. Müller in the order of the Helminthica, where they (always still without the Serpulacea, which remained with the Testacea) form with the Lumbricina, which Linné had already counted among the Intestina, a suborder of their own, the Setosa, which stand over against the remaining worms (from which, however, Fasciola and Planaria were excluded as belonging to the molluscs). The latter are designated, for want of bristles, as Mutica.

In the same extent we meet the group of the worms (Intestins) still in Bruguières (2). Cuvier was the first (3) who believed he recognised in the creatures united here a twofold typical structure. The one set, the annelids, which were distinguished partly by a red colouring of the blood, partly by a segmentation of the body and the presence of a simple ventral ganglionic chain, he brought as the representatives of a class of their own to the articulated animals, while he reckoned the others, the Entozoa (with the forms of the Nemertina, Turbellaria and also the Lernaeada), to the circle of the zoophytes. This arrangement has not lacked applause. Lamarck, Latreille, Goldfuss and others have adopted it. **[p. 46]** On the other side, however, it has also met with much contradiction. In Germany it was especially v. Baer (1) and at the same time my uncle Fr. S. Leuckart (2) who attempted the proof that a class of the Entozoa of its own is very unnatural, that the assemblage of the helminths can be regarded only as a fauna of the interior of the animal body, whose members have their relatives and representatives in various other orders and classes of the animal kingdom. One emphasised the great agreement of the Nematoidea and bristle-worms, of the Trematoda and Hirudinea, and sought to distribute the remaining forms of the helminths elsewhere too. So e.g. my uncle believed he recognised a close kinship of the Acanthocephala with the Sipunculida, of the Cestoda with the polyps and acalephs — in which Meckel (3) also agreed with him — and therefore proposed a union of the animal forms concerned.

At the moment the arrangement of Cuvier may find few more defenders (4). Quite generally helminths and annelids are again united in a common division of the worms. Only on the relation of this division to the remaining groups of the invertebrate animals does there still prevail a great difference of views. Some regard the worms as a particular typical main division of the animal kingdom (Gravenhorst (5), Berthold (6), v. Siebold (7)), while the others see in them a group subordinate to the type of the articulated animals **[p. 47]** and mostly even merely set it equal to the remaining individual classes of these. The latter procedure is certainly the least natural. Even if one wishes to hold the worms to be articulated animals (although by this, in my opinion, what is characteristic of this group of animals wholly falls away), the differences between them and the other forms belonging there must be strictly kept in view. We must then, as Milne Edwards especially has done, assume in this great division two main groups, worms and arthropods, both of which in turn fall severally into classes.

However, I must confess that the differences between worms and arthropods seem to me so considerable that I cannot resolve to unite the two (1). Both rather seem to me built each on a particular plan, different in essence. The articulation of the body, as well as the presence of a ventral ganglionic chain — the only relations which bring the higher worms near the proper articulated animals — are in my view not founded with necessity in the type of the worms, but indeed in that of the arthropods. There they are merely called forth by a further development of the type and always without that definite significance as in the true articulated animals.

Most worms lack both articulation and the ventral ganglionic chain. Pervasive, by contrast, and conditioned by the type of the worms, is the presence of a nuchal ganglion, from which the nerves radiate to the various sides. It is in agreement with the stretched body form and the lateral symmetry of the body that usually among these nerves two longitudinal trunks stand out, descending backward into the so-called tail end. These very two lateral nerves now **[p. 48]** are also those which, in the more highly developed forms of the worms, according to the law of the median fusion of corresponding lateral parts (a law which makes itself felt so extraordinarily often in animals with laterally symmetrical type), unite in the median line of the belly beneath the intestinal canal into a single unpaired trunk. That in the course of this ventral cord, answering to the individual segments, particular ganglionic swellings also show themselves can surprise us the less, since already in the separate lateral nerves of other worms, in Malacobdella (1) and in some larger species of Planaria (2), quite analogous structures occur.

As to the segmentation, this too, I believe, cannot of itself yet entitle us to a union of the worms with the articulated animals. In other animal circles too we find it brought into use. The arms of the ophiurans, the vertebral columns of the bony animals show quite the same repetition of like morphological sections in a simple series one behind another. Everywhere it seems able to arise very easily where the longitudinal dimension prevails, especially when lateral symmetry is not disturbed in any way.

And then, how very different is the segment formation in the worms from that in the arthropods. While in the latter the individual body rings group themselves together in turn according to a quite definite norm, while in this their appendages are reshaped in a corresponding way, we meet in the articulated worms essentially a quite uniform development of segments and their appendages. There a heteronomy prevails in the arrangement of the rings, here a homonomy. A head especially, furnished with feeding instruments which have arisen from the metamorphosis of segmental appendages, **[p. 49]** is lacking to all worms. What one calls a head in these animals is as little a morphological equivalent of the head of the arthropods as the so-called mandibles (1) are an equivalent of the feeding instruments just mentioned.

Having thus recognised the common peculiarities of worms and arthropods as rather outer likenesses, which in themselves by no means allow one to infer the inner agreement of the building style, the other differences between these two groups of animals must on the other side gain still more in weight. While the arthropods everywhere possess a more or less widely closed vascular system, everywhere cross-striations on the muscle bundles (even where, as e.g. in the vertebrates, smooth fibres occur, on the viscera, the glandular tubes etc.) and a total want of all vibratile cilia, we meet by contrast in the worms a completely closed circulatory apparatus (at least in the annelids), smooth muscle fibrils and a ciliated epithelium in great extent (2). Moreover there is lacking in worms that peculiar substance, chitin, which is found throughout in the integuments of the arthropods. In the coverings at least it cannot be found (3).

Under such circumstances, I believe, we are fully entitled to the assumption that the worms are built on a plan of their own, divergent from the type of the arthropods, and therefore also by right **[p. 50]** are to be regarded as an independent great main division of the animal kingdom.

To this division, however, we must, as it seems to me, add, besides the Cuvierian intestinal worms and the annelids, some other isolated groups which at any other place would be without all connection, all link. I mean the Rotifera and Bryozoa.

The former, as is known, were counted by Ehrenberg (after the example of O. Fr. Müller) among the Infusoria (1), but have been separated from them by other zoologists on the ground of their whole organisation, certainly with the greatest right. Burmeister believed he saw in them lower crustaceans and reckoned them to his Entomostraca with retrograde metamorphosis (?). To such a view, however, I cannot accede. The want of a ventral cord and of cross-striated muscle fibres — Euchlanis triquetra, to be sure, makes an exception in the latter respect, since here real cross-striations occur on the primitive muscle bundles, as however also in Pentastomum in the dermal muscle tube and in Aphrodite in the pharynx —, the absence of chitin in the coverings, the lack of proper feeding instruments and jointed legs, as well as the widespread occurrence of a ciliated epithelium, forbid such a grouping. All these relations, by contrast, are quite usual in the division of the worms. To this comes the articulation of the body, which is found in many Rotatoria just as in the annelids. Without further hesitation, **[p. 51]** therefore, I share the assumption of Wiegmann, R. Wagner, Milne Edwards, Rymer Jones, Berthold, v. Siebold and others, who reckon the Rotatoria to the worms.

As to the Bryozoa, it will, I believe, have emerged sufficiently from what was stated above on the organisation of the Coelenterata that they can no longer be associated with the polyps. However, the view of Milne Edwards, that the Bryozoa are to be ranged with the tunicates, also seems to me, as I have already stated in another place (1), not acceptable, because the absence of a branchial sac in the former founds a fundamental difference between the two. By contrast the Bryozoa show a striking kinship with the Rotifera, as especially Arth. Farre (2) emphasises and as was not unknown even to Ehrenberg. The ciliary wreath on the head disc of the Rotifera (the so-called wheel apparatus) answers, as already v. Baer (3) remarks, to the ciliated arms of the Bryozoa. Especially probative for this agreement is the gen. Stephanoceros, where the edge of the head disc, quite as in the Bryozoa (4), is drawn out into arm-shaped processes covered with cilia. I should like here to recall an analogous relation in the Cephalopoda, the formation of the head appendages in Nautilus, where, as we know (through the investigations (5) of Valenciennes), the proper arms have vanished and only their appendages persist as cylindrical tentacles (answering to the suckers of the Acetabulifera, as the gen. Cirroteuthis especially lets one recognise very distinctly). Quite **[p. 52]** so in the Rotifera, in which (with the exception of that genus named above) the prolongations analogous to the arms of the Bryozoa are lacking and only their long cilia have remained. That many Rotifera, like the Bryozoa, live in cases I will not emphasise here, because in the former this case seems everywhere to be only a secretion of the outer coverings, not however, as is the case (1) in the Bryozoa, to consist of the hardened and calcified integuments themselves. Interesting, however, it is that in the case-dwelling Rotifera the intestinal canal, just as in the Bryozoa, makes a quite considerable loop-shaped bend in order to open far upward, near the head end, instead of in the tip of the hind body.

The Bryozoa are, moreover, the only worms which possess a skeleton. Quite generally otherwise the outer coverings remain soft and pliant. The calcareous and leathery tubes, often also built up of foreign bodies, of sand and the like, in which many worms, especially from the group of the so-called Capitibranchiata, live, are never integral parts of the body. As in the Rotifera just mentioned, these arise here too by the hardening of a peculiar secretion furnished by the inhabitants themselves.

With the echinoderms the development of the radial type is extinguished in the series of animal forms. In the division of the worms there begins another manner of building, characterised by the lateral symmetry in the form of the body and the arrangement of the viscera. The central longitudinal axis, which there behaved quite uniformly to all parts of the periphery and acted determiningly on the constitution and grouping of the surrounding parts, has here transferred its morphological significance to an ideal longitudinal plane which stretches down through the middle of the body. It parts the body into two lateral halves, **[p. 53]** of which one is the perfect mirror image of the other. Where it cuts the periphery of the body, in two opposite longitudinal lines (which naturally must hold exactly the middle between the outermost limits of the two side halves), a definite opposing difference has formed itself. The surface passing perpendicularly through one of these longitudinal lines has become the ventral surface, the opposite one the dorsal surface.

Belly and back show in arrangement, form and constitution of their parts manifold lesser or greater differences. So e.g. the locomotor instruments are fastened to the former. Dependent on this it is that the belly appears as the lower, the back as the upper body surface, while, as we have seen before, in animals with radial type the upper and lower surface are determined by the poles of the central axis and so answer to those regions which in the worms etc. we designate as front and hind end of the body (1).

An anatomical difference between back and belly is lacking in all animals with ray-shaped body, at least in all to which such a form belongs in its whole characteristic peculiarity. However, after the combinations of the radial type with the laterally symmetrical sometimes brought into use, it cannot be mistaken that also in the Radiata an analogous opposition in the development of the peripheral parts is already possible. It can be determined which regions in the Coelenterata and echinoderms answer to the belly and back of the other animals.

**[p. 54]** Most distinct is this among the echinoderms in those remarkable forms of the holothurians in which already a ventral disc of their own, comparable to the foot of the gastropods, has developed, in the Psolinae. Here, as I see in a Cuvieria of the physiological institute here, there runs in the median line of the ventral surface one of those five longitudinal muscles radiating from the bony pharyngeal ring, which, as e.g. Pentacta shows distinctly, stretch along beneath the blunt longitudinal edges of the body. — These latter answer in morphological respect to the five arms of the starfishes. The median line of the ventral surface would accordingly here too coincide with one of these five arms. The four other arms would be distributed as paired elements over the two lateral halves of the body, in such a way that the two outermost of them, which lie farthest from the unpaired arm, belong to the dorsal surface of the body.

The determination, moreover, of which of the five peripheral rays of the echinoderms is the ventral ray is not everywhere quite easy. In the holothurians one will here best go by the position of the inner gills. They are real laterally symmetrical structures, as I see in Cuvieria. That ray, then, which runs in the middle between them is the unpaired ventral ray.

More difficult is this determination in the Actinozoa and the Pelmatozoa. Perhaps the not seldom more or less eccentric position of the anus gives information here, although the radius passing through it often does not directly touch a ray but answers to the interspace of two rays. The differences expressing themselves in this evidently point to a disturbance of lateral symmetry, as it expresses itself precisely in the position of the anus so often elsewhere — a relation which here, however, considerably hampers the decision of the present question.

**[p. 55]** So much, however, seems certain, that in the five-rayed echinoderms one ray is constantly to be regarded as the ventral ray. Whether an analogous relation is also found in the Coelenterata cannot be determined from the outset. Impossible it is not, although the difference in the prevailing number of the peripheral articulation must call us to a cautious examination. If, moreover, we found here too that the ventral surface is likewise determined by the course of a single ray, we should have to regard the opposite ray as dorsal ray, the two between as symmetrical lateral rays.

For the decision of this relation we hold here too to those cases in which the radial type is combined with a laterally symmetrical arrangement. We consider the comb-jellies. In these we see that the fishing threads, which, as already stated above, appear as paired elements, are placed in the interspaces between each two opposite pairs of ribs. If now we infer by analogy that the fishing threads, because of their symmetrical development, belong to the side surfaces, we find a relation divergent from the relation in the echinoderms (1). The four ribs of the Coelenterata are all four paired elements, of which two bound the ventral surface, the two others the dorsal surface.

So much on the relation of the radial type to the laterally symmetrical. Let us now turn again to the worms. First of the typical arrangement of these animals.

That for the moment a satisfactory, sufficiently wide and yet sharp bounding of the type of the worms is not yet possible emerges already from this, that the Sipunculida are so often associated with it. Perhaps this points to the division of the worms **[p. 56]** in its present extent being by no means a wholly natural one. The morphological conception does not, so far at least, find everywhere a sure point of attack in the uniformity of body structure which, despite all diversity of forms, prevails in the worms.

What may perhaps be adduced as most characteristic for the worms is the usually very evident predominance of the longitudinal dimension with an almost everywhere quite perfect lateral symmetry. In the direction from back to belly the body of the worms is almost constantly depressed, yet in varying degree and usually without the ventral surface being outwardly different in a striking way from the dorsal. Jointed locomotor instruments are constantly lacking.

As to the arrangement of the inner organs, this too shows with very few exceptions a strict lateral symmetry, such as is found among the invertebrate animals only in the arthropods besides, from which the worms are sufficiently distinguished by the characters already mentioned above. The intestinal canal, which is lacking only in a few groups, as we shall adduce further below, and is then represented by the body cavity (as in many Coelenterata), runs as a rule quite straight and always without a particular mesentery from the front end down to the hind, where it opens. Only rarely is the anal opening moved further forward (as also among the echinoderms in Sipunculus, among the crustaceans in Lepas and some others). Sometimes an anus is also lacking. In the latter case, moreover, the intestine usually shows a more or less considerable lateral branching, as especially in the Trematoda. A parenchymatous liver is found in no worm. The vascular system consists either of various mutually communicating and closed longitudinal trunks, or is represented by the body cavity. As respiratory organs function partly the outer skin, partly however also its various **[p. 57]** appendages (1). The genital apparatus shows a great diversity, but is, where it occurs (in some cases it is lacking, and then spermatozoa as well as eggs form freely (2) in the body cavity), constantly distributed in like development over both lateral halves of the body. On the arrangement of the nervous system the needful has already been brought forward above.

A consequence of the mostly so very evident predominance of the longitudinal dimension in the form of the body in the worms is, as already stated, the tendency to the formation of more or less numerous morphologically corresponding sections, which, as so-called segments, lie in simple series one behind another and in a manner repeat the whole body many times. Especially distinctly pronounced is such a tendency in the more highly developed worms, the annelids, yet not restricted to these alone. This behaviour shows itself, moreover, not merely in the outer form. Very generally, often even more evidently, it expresses itself also in the individual systems and organ parts, while here it is almost completely extinguished in the arthropods, which outwardly show an analogous articulation. This phenomenon stands in the most intimate connection with the whole mutual relation of the body segments, which is so very different in worms and arthropods. In the latter there shows itself among the individual segments a quite constant, unalterable relation of number and dependence, governed by the unitary principle of the body; in the former, by contrast, there is found merely a multitude of sections repeating itself in fluctuating number, which essentially also anatomically present a like development and therefore also possess a much greater independence **[p. 58]** and autonomy. In some cases, in the Cestoda, the latter grows to such a degree that one is quite entitled to conceive the individual segments as so many independent animals joined with one another into a common colony.

Where now under such relations definite appendages are found outwardly on the body of the worms, as is not seldom the case, these too are spread uniformly and completely symmetrically over all segments. Such appendages, moreover, are of different kinds. Partly they are mere epidermoidal structures, local developments of the integuments of hook- or bristle-shaped form, whose occurrence is made possible solely by the peculiar constitution of the latter, and which are found again in similar development on the chitin membranes of the arthropods (which the outer coverings of the worms also resemble in structure). Partly, too, those appendages are formal, variously shaped evaginations of the body envelope, into whose formation, besides the outer coverings, the muscular tube of the body with its excitatory and nutritive elements, the nerves and vessels, also enters. Already in the Coelenterata and likewise, though less generally, in the echinoderms, appendages like these latter are widely spread (as so-called tentacles), especially in the circuit of the mouth opening. In the worms too they are found preferably, in some, like the Bryozoa and many Capitibranchiata, even exclusively at this place, while otherwise they usually repeat themselves on the individual body rings, and indeed completely symmetrically.

In form and arrangement these structures show manifold differences. In the circuit of the mouth they are usually simple, long threads of cylindrical shape (cirri tentaculares), while on the segments they appear as single lateral appendages (pinnae, oars), and here as a **[p. 59]** rule consist of two parts each, of a thread-shaped cirrus and a blunt prominence lying beside it (a so-called foot-tubercle), which latter then bears on its tip a bundle of epidermoidal bristles. In many cases, moreover, such foot-tubercles are lacking, while in others particular lancet- or leaf-shaped evaginations have developed beside them. These latter usually function as gills. Where they are missed, the business of respiration is transferred to other appendages, now to the cirri or tentacles, now also to particular morphologically quite independent parts, which stand in paired arrangement on the dorsal surface, mostly near the front body edge.

Very interesting, however, it is that those outer appendages on the body of the worms (with the exception of the gills last mentioned) do not, as in the arthropods, occur merely in single number on each side, but either, as e.g. the hooks at the front body end of the Cestoda or the tentacles of the Bryozoa, uniformly and in the form of a wreath beset the whole periphery, or at least repeat themselves in corresponding manner on back and belly. So the bristle bundles and oar plates.

Quite evidently this circumstance recalls the relations occurring in animals with radial type. Ventral and dorsal surface have evidently not yet entered into that sharp opposition as we find it in the arthropods and also in the molluscs. Beside the longitudinal plane running through the middle line of back and belly, which acts determiningly and decisively on the formation and development of the individual body parts, we have in the latter case morphologically still to attend to a second longitudinal plane, which crosses that one at a right angle and is laid through the outermost limits of the two lateral halves of the body (1). Both **[p. 60]** planes have a like morphological relation to the constituent components of the body. The structures arising in them themselves are single, those lying beside them laterally double, yet such double structures can also later grow toward one another and fuse (1). Of the real existence of such a behaviour in the lateral parts of the body the numerous differences in the arrangement of the oar plates furnish the clearest proof. However, it cannot be mistaken that for the whole typical structure of the worms the second longitudinal plane, laid perpendicularly through the lateral halves, has far from so pervasive a significance as the first.

A great number of worms lack the manifold appendages hitherto mentioned. Instead, however, there is found at times, in the class of the Trematoda, another, morphologically quite peculiar appendage, a sucking disc, which serves as locomotor organ and is usually placed in the middle line of the belly at the hind end of the body, but from there also sometimes moves a lesser or greater stretch forward. On its lower free surface it bears, for easier attachment, a varying number of roundish depressions, so-called sucking pits, now, as in the leeches, only a single one, now also a greater number, six (Polystomum), eight (Octobothrium) and so on. In the latter case the sucking pits usually stand in the circuit of the disc, which then also has mostly lost its roundish shape and is more or less deeply notched at the periphery between the pits.

A similar appendage, perhaps answering to the sucking disc, on the dorsal surface of the front body end above the mouth opening is the so-called head in the **[p. 61]** bristle-worms. With the head of the arthropods it is by no means identical. The latter has constantly arisen from the fusion and metamorphosis of a greater number of segments. One will hardly be able to prove this, however, for the so-called head of those worms, not even there (1) where it, as is the case in some animals belonging here, appears ringed or is furnished with thread-shaped appendages of its own. In the Lumbricina this structure is known as the so-called proboscis or upper lip. In many Hirudinea too there is found an indication of the head (2), and precisely here it is that its analogy with a sucking disc comes forth very evidently.

Of a divergent morphological significance again is the so-called head of the Cestoda and Acanthocephala, which, as it seems to me, has arisen quite simply from a transformation of the front body end, as we meet such already among the Scytodermata in Sipunculus and especially in Phascolosoma.

Of interest, moreover, it is that already in some articulated worms, in which, as stated above, the development of the individual segments is in general uniform (over against the relations occurring in the arthropods), a definite heteronomy forms itself, though for the whole type only little characteristic and significant. In two different ways this seems able to happen. For one, at times the front body segments are more or less stunted and often even completely vanished down to their appendages **[p. 62]** (1), while in other cases, sometimes even at the same time, it is the hind segments which suffer a transformation and appear in the form of a particular body appendage (which I have designated as postabdomen (2) in Hermella and others, where it occurs). Very evident is the latter kind of reshaping especially in many Rotifera, in which Burmeister (3) believed he found in part a ground for his assertion on the position of these animals. However, this case, as said, does not stand alone in the great division of the worms. That the tail-shaped appendage concerned has, however, really arisen from a metamorphosis of segments is proved by the circumstance that in many cases it is partly still distinctly ringed, partly also — since that alone would not be decisive enough, because at times, as in the Lumbrici, the head appendage too appears ringed — is traversed by the intestinal canal.

The development of the worms is still just as little completely known as that of the echinoderms. Even if we know perhaps a greater number of particulars of it, we still always lack the understanding of the connection of these individual phenomena. For systematics, as for morphology, there grows out of it so far only in few cases a sure point of support.

The first changes which the yolk undergoes after fertilisation are the same which are found in general in the whole animal world. The yolk cleaves and by help of this process is gradually transformed into a multitude of cellular elements, which serve for the building up of the embryo. But already here some difference shows itself. In the one case, namely, as it **[p. 63]** seems in the majority of the worms, especially the less highly developed, the whole mass of these cells takes part in this from the beginning in a uniform way. The embryo arises under such circumstances with its whole body surface at once, as is also found in the Coelenterata and echinoderms. In the other case, by contrast (1), there forms at first only a so-called primitive part, which only in the course of development, while it increases in size and extent, grows round the whole yolk and so only gradually gives the embryo origin and form. That primordium answers to that body surface which morphologically has the greatest dignity, the belly, quite as in the insects, where a completely agreeing formation takes place. That, moreover, this circumstance should suffice (as Kölliker thinks) to separate those worms in which such a process occurs (the gill-worms and Hirudinea, so far as we know up to now) from the rest and unite them again with the arthropods, I should doubt the more, as we can hardly yet even surmise in what extent such a process is found in the worms. Moreover we see also in other typical main divisions of the animal kingdom (in the molluscs) quite the same difference; now a uniform transformation of the whole yolk into the body of the embryo, now the formation of a primitive part.

In many cases the embryo already in its first shape resembles the developed animal almost quite completely (Ascarides, Planariae etc.). Where, however, an articulation occurs, this is constantly lacking at first. Likewise the manifold appendages of the individual segments. The body is originally only short and clumsily built and without opposition of dorsal and ventral surface. Only gradually does it stretch, according to the length of the finished worm to be sure **[p. 64]** in varying measure, and flatten. The articulation sets in either uniformly in its whole extent, or forms itself only by and by. The former is found (besides the Rotatoria) preferably in the Hirudinea (1). Pretty much at the same time, yet only after the animal has already left its egg envelopes, there form here over the whole length of the body the individual segments, each with a nerve knot and a blind sac of the intestine, since indeed, as we have already stated above, the anatomical systems of the body, like the outer skin, usually incline in like manner to the formation of particular morphological sections. If, moreover, as often happens (especially in the Hirudinea), these parts later fall once more by transverse furrowing into a greater number of ringlets, this is morphologically of no great significance. The ringlets so arisen are not to be set equal to the segments.

In another way the articulation arises in the Chaetopoda (at least in the gill-worms, since the development of the Lumbricina is still unknown) and Cestoda. In both the body attains its final completion only by a successive addition of joints. At first their number is only small, later often very considerable. In both groups, moreover, the place at which the formation of the joints happens shows a difference. While in the former the hind body end is the seat of this new formation, in the others the front end of the body appears as such. In the Chaetopoda the hind segments (with the exception of the anal segment) are the youngest, in the tapeworms the front ones. Answering to this relation the appendages of the body also develop in the gill-worms from front to back, first the head with the adjoining cirri and eyes (which latter **[p. 65]** also belong quite generally in the first phases of development to the Capitibranchiata, which in the developed state are mostly blind), later the oar plates, which only gradually separate into foot-tubercles and joint-threads, last the gills.

Beside such a metamorphosis there is found in the division of the worms also again the remarkable process of the alternation of generations, especially in the Trematoda, where it was first recognised by Steenstrup, yet also, according to Miescher and van Beneden (1), in some Cestoda. The nurses, which in the first generation possess an infusorium-like, in the later already a worm-like shape, produce by a budding in their interior (in Distomum) a numerous brood of so-called cercariae, which formally pupate and only after loss of the hind, tail-like appendage become like the mother animals. To the group of the Bryozoa too such a propagation through alternating generations seems not foreign. To this at least point the statements of Meyen (2) and v. Siebold (3), that in Alcyonella and Cristatella in the interior of the infusorium-like embryos, even before these have left the egg shell, two animals each like the parental individuals form, which however strikingly remain constantly enveloped by the skin of their nurse animal, since this enters into the formation of the outer shell. However, it is very doubtful to me that this alternation of generations extends over all Bryozoa. In the embryos of a Tubulipora at least, which I observed on Heligoland, I found no trace of it. These had a short, cylindrical shape. In the middle of the flattened head disc they showed a roundish opening, in whose circuit single tubercle-shaped prominences (the **[p. 66]** first traces of the arms?) were found. Outwardly they were clothed, like the embryos of most worms, with a ciliated epithelium (1).

After these remarks on the morphological relations belonging in general to the type of the worms, we turn to the consideration of the chief modifications of which these are capable, preferably in order thereby to establish the individual larger and smaller natural groups of the present division and to assess them according to their mutual value.

The two Cuvierian classes of the Entozoa and Annelides, united by me with the Bryozoa and Rotifera in the circle of the worms, are, as already mentioned above, anything but natural. They are by no means strictly and completely bounded against one another. I cannot therefore agree with the view of those zoologists who divide the worms quite simply into these two groups and at most add to them, as a third group of equal value, the class of the Rotifera (Grant, Milne Edwards (2) and others).

Even if the latter, as I believe, do after all form a particular class together with the Bryozoa, yet on the other side annelids and entozoa cannot be kept completely apart. Both show in many forms so intimate a kinship that natural systematics may not neglect it.

The division of the Entozoa into Nematoidea and Parenchymatosa deriving from Cuvier is certainly a very unhappy one and probably wholly to be given up. Much more noteworthy (1) **[p. 67]** is the classification of (Zeder and) Rudolphi, according to which the helminths fall into the orders of the Nematodes, Acanthocephali, Trematodes, Cestodes and Cystici. All the groups, with the exception of the Cystici, which are not different from the Cestoda and certainly contain mere degenerate and stunted forms (2) of the latter worms, are all very natural, although they in part belong together among themselves in turn to a common larger division, in part however must also be united with various forms set by Cuvier among the red-blooded worms. As to the latter, Savigny first drew attention to how unsuitably the Hirudinea were set together by Cuvier with the Lumbricina (out of which two Cuvier had made the common order of the Abranchia) and the other annelids. Later Blainville (3) separated them wholly from these and united them with various helminths in a class of the Apodes, which he opposed to the remaining red-blooded worms (the Chétopodes), while out of the rest of the Entozoa he formed a third class of equal value, the Subannélidaires. Even if the tendency expressed in such a procedure must be fully approved, yet the classes of the Apodes and Subannélidaires can no longer stand as natural groups. In the former we meet beside the Hirudinea and some Trematoda also the Nematoda and Acanthocephala (with the Sipunculida), in the other the Turbellaria, Cestoda and Trematoda — forms whose difference **[p. 68]** will certainly easily strike one on any closer consideration.

Of all the various attempts to resolve entozoa and annelids into their natural divisions, I hold the attempt of Burmeister (1) by far the happiest. The groups of the Helminthes, Trematodes and Annulati distinguished among the worms by this very excellent zoologist, which according to their systematic significance may appear as so many classes, are in my view essentially quite correct and held together by definite, very noteworthy characters in form and structure. Only in single subordinate relations did it seem to me here and there necessary to depart from the views of Burmeister. The Bryozoa and Rotifera are, moreover, excluded from the worms in the system of the latter. In what has been remarked before, however, one will find it justified if I not merely reckon them to the type of these animals, but also gather them in a common class, as *Ciliati* (from the very developed ciliary clothing of the tentacular apparatus and the corresponding wheel organ).

The first of these classes, for which I propose here the name of the *Anenterati* (2), since the designation Helminthes chosen by Burmeister is usually used for the whole group of the Entozoa, comprises the two orders of the *Cestodes* and *Acanthocephali*. In the structure of the nervous system (3) and of the nutritive apparatus both show in general a complete agreement. Even the body structure is quite similar in both groups. Everywhere the front part of the body is reshaped in a uniform way into a peculiar section, into the so-called head, and furnished with holdfast instruments, now with sucking pits, now with hooks, now also with both kinds **[p. 69]** of structure at once. To be sure, the Acanthocephala lack the flat form of the body and an articulation such as belongs to the tapeworms, yet this can the less condition a fundamental difference, since a cylindrical form of the body with simultaneous absence of an articulation is at times found already among the Cestoda (in Tetrarhynchus). The kinship of these two orders also shows itself in the very agreeing form of the embryos e.g. of Echinorhynchus (1) and Taenia (2). An intestinal canal is lacking in the whole class of the Anenterata. Instead one finds in the tapeworms a double system of vessels which spread in the body, one deeply embedded in the parenchyma of the body, with wide longitudinal canals on each side, which anastomose with one another, the other lying under the skin, a delicate mesh network (3). The place of the intestine in the Acanthocephala, by contrast, so one supposes, is taken by only a single subcutaneous vascular network (without particular walls). In this now I should like to see an analogue of the second vascular network of the Cestoda just mentioned.

A difference between the two orders, however, rests, according to the mode of view at present quite generally usual, in the circumstance that the hook-worms possess a very roomy body cavity, which is lacking to the tapeworms. However, are we so quite unconditionally entitled to the latter assumption? Would it be impossible that the central vascular system of the Cestoda possessed the morphological significance of the body cavity? Could this not, through an excessive development of the body parenchyma, in an analogous **[p. 70]** way as in the developed acalephs, be pushed back to an inner vessel-like apparatus? I must openly confess, such an interpretation seems to me very natural. At least in my judgement no essential hindrance stands against it. The lining of the body cavity could then in the Cestoda have after all reached a greater development and independence, so that it would even be enabled to contractions of its own, in order to mediate the movement of the nutritive fluid contained in it. At the same time the genital apparatus, as is indeed already the case in the acalephs, would thereby have lost its position in the interior of the body cavity and could be enclosed by the body parenchyma. Under such circumstances there would then remain between the Acanthocephala and the Cestoda, if we leave aside the unequal development of the body cavity, only a difference in the arrangement of the generative instruments. But this cannot hinder us from a union of the corresponding creatures in a common class. We see such differences, after all, not seldom elsewhere among quite closely related groups in the animal series.

A second class of the worms comprises the orders of the *Nemertini*, *Turbellarii* (1), *Trematodes* and *Hirudinei*. I will designate it as the class of the *Apodes*, with a name, then, which has already been given by Blainville to a larger division among the worms, not indeed wholly conformable to the present class, and after the precedent of Milne Edwards (2) is several times transferred to the group of the Hirudinea separated from the proper annelids. **[p. 71]** How close the kinship of the forms united in this class is, one has long felt and often also pronounced more or less definitely. So Linné already set the gen. Fasciola (in which he — as likewise O. Fr. Müller — united besides proper Trematoda also single flatworms, like Planaria lactea, which he held identical with Distomum hepaticum) close beside Hirudo. But after these animals had later been so widely separated from one another by the Cuvierian classification, first my uncle Leuckart (1), v. Baer (2), Dugès (3) and others again drew attention to the close kinship of the leeches and Trematoda (to which Cuvier had also set the flatworms and Nemertina). Likewise Burmeister, who in his class of the Trematodes gathered the Planariae, the Rudolphian Trematoda and the Hirudinea. Certainly with the greatest right, as the typical agreement in form and structure proves. The Nemertina are, moreover, excluded by Burmeister from the present class. They stand, united with the Nematoda into a common group (Gymnodermi), in the class of the Annulati. However, I cannot agree with Burmeister in this. The Nemertina are, despite many differences, still much more closely related to the Planariae than to the Nematoda. The arrangement of the outer coverings and of the nervous system, the branching of the intestine and even the peculiar, very divergent development of the proboscis (4) secure them a place near these worms. I must, however, hold it very perverse when Oersted (5) wishes to unite with the Trematoda and Hirudinea also the Gordiacea and Sipunculida, **[p. 72]** but on the other hand separates from them not merely the Nemertina (Cestoidei Oerst.), but also the Planariae (1).

The four orders of the Apodes adduced above seem to me very natural (2) and just as definitely bounded against one another as they are bound among themselves by common more general characters. Mostly the animals belonging to them are oblong worms, strongly depressed from above downward, which, like the Anenterata, completely lack proper foot-like locomotor organs, if at least one leaves out of account the sucking discs pretty generally spread among the Trematoda and Hirudinea. The Turbellaria are usually distinguished by a very flat, often completely leaf-shaped, the Nemertina by a very long, almost tapeworm-like body form. In both the outer coverings are beset for life with a ciliated epithelium, which otherwise is present only during the earlier embryonic periods. An articulation is either lacking or, when it is found, as in the Hirudinea and some Nemertina, is everywhere only little distinct and also more irregular than in the so-called annelids. Almost still **[p. 73]** greater than in the outer form of the body is the agreement between the various orders of the Apodes in the inner structure. So the nervous system in them consists quite throughout of a large (paired) nuchal ganglion lying above the mouth opening and two strong lateral nerves, which only in the Hirudinea fuse with one another in the median line of the belly and, answering to the individual segments, form a chain-shaped series of ganglionic swellings, as in most annelids and the arthropods. Such ganglia, however, are already found also (as mentioned above) in some other Apodes with separate lateral nerves. What besides distinguishes the class of the Apodes is the striking tendency to the formation of numerous lateral evaginations of the intestinal canal, which mostly branch in turn and in many Turbellaria and Trematoda ramify tree-like through the whole body. Noteworthy in this is that in the latter groups the proper stem of the intestinal canal does not, as usual, descend in the median line of the body cavity, but has mostly fallen through a middle longitudinal cleft into two lateral limbs, which communicate with one another only in their front part. Evidently the same tendency to the formation of lateral ramifications on the intestine shows itself already in this; a behaviour which also seems to stand in a certain connection with the want of a particular anal opening (1) in the Turbellaria and Trematoda. In the Hirudinea, moreover, these ramifications are quite evidently under the influence of segment formation. Yet precisely here they are relatively the most considerable, although in many cases (e.g. in Clepsine), especially toward the **[p. 74]** hind body end, they still reach a very mighty development. In the interior of the mouth cavity there is often found in the Apodes also a muscular protrusible tube, a proboscis, which in most Turbellaria shows a considerable development and in the Nemertina is even placed above the intestinal canal as a completely independent, very mighty structure, which opens in front of the mouth opening at the tip of the head end. The vascular system consists, when it occurs, throughout of wide, only little branched longitudinal trunks, of which (divergently from the arrangement of the circulatory apparatus in the articulated worms) especially the lateral trunks present a strong development. In the structure of the generative apparatus the Nemertina differ from the other Apodes. Male and female organs are in them distributed over different individuals and consist in a simple series of oval little sacs lying on each side beside the intestinal canal. The other Apodes are hermaphrodites, whose genital system is distinguished by the presence and the development of the germ-conducting canals and in all three orders very distinctly lets a like typical structure be recognised (1).

After the Apodes now I should like to insert the class of the *Ciliati* set up before, which comprises the orders of the *Bryozoa* and *Rotiferi*. The analogies and differences found between the two in the form and structure of the outer body have already been stated. Besides, however, let it still be mentioned that likewise the nervous system, as well as the genital apparatus and the intestinal canal, present in both essentially a like arrangement and grouping, although in this too single differences occur, which however may hardly be considerable enough to hinder a union of them in a common **[p. 75]** class. So the nervous system in both groups consists preferably only of a nuchal ganglion with various larger and smaller trunks radiating from it. Likewise in both (whether throughout in the order of the Rotifera?) the genital apparatus lacks particular efferent canals. Testes and ovaries are reduced to a little heap of small cells in the interior of the body cavity, so that one perhaps cannot even speak of the existence of particular germ-preparing organs, quite as is the case in the gill-worms. In general the Ciliata approach these latter, especially through the Bryozoa, in such a way that a union of them, as I formerly (1) proposed it, would appear very natural, if on the other side the Nematoda and Lumbricina did not also form a very suitable transition to those worms. It almost seems as if the typical form of the Branchiata had formed itself on these two different paths. As the Lumbricina join preferably the so-called Dorsibranchiata, so the Ciliata especially the so-called Capitibranchiata. Many of these latter even resemble, in the earlier stages of their development (2), if one leaves the bristles out of account, completely a bryozoon. Possible, therefore, that in the sequel the Ciliata may really be set together with the gill-worms, although then, as it would seem to me, the Lumbricina and Nematoda could hardly remain united with them.

For now, however, I believe, the class of the Burmeisterian *Annulati* or of the *Annelides*, as I should like to name it (naturally with exclusion of the Nemertini), can still be maintained. Habit and structure of the worms reckoned there justify this, in my judgement, **[p. 76]** completely. To be sure, we may here regard neither the presence of segments and outer body appendages (of bristles, foot-tubercles, cirri etc.), nor that of a middle ventral ganglionic chain as the character of our class. The occurrence of these structures, after all, shows manifold differences even within the individual orders of it. So among the gill-worms e.g. the tentacles and head cirri are lacking to the Arenicolae, the foot-tubercles and cirri of the segments to many so-called Capitibranchiata, the bristles to the remarkable gen. Peripatus. Likewise the segmentation of the body is missed in Sagitta, an animal which according to its structure (1) must be set in the order of the Lumbricina and not among the molluscs, as, after the precedent of Milne Edwards, v. Siebold and I myself (2) formerly assumed. On the other side, by contrast, formal bristles planted in the skin are found among the Nematoda in Hemipsilus (3), though only at the front end of the body and even here not in so regular an arrangement as in the so-called Chaetopoda — a relation which, moreover, easily finds its explanation in the absence of a segmentation. But even the latter is not wholly lacking to the Nematoda. Very characteristic is it in Pentastoma, a genus which can hardly be wholly removed from this order.

What holds the class of the Annelides together is the morphological agreement in the outer form of the body, in the arrangement of the intestinal canal and of the blood-vascular system (which, moreover, in the Nematoda is perhaps for the most part (4) lacking), as in general in the mutual **[p. 77]** relation of the individual anatomical systems. According to the differences which make themselves known preferably partly in the structure of the nervous system and the genital apparatus, partly also in the occurrence of the outer body appendages, they fall into the orders of the *Nematodes*, *Lumbricini* (s. Terricolae) and *Branchiati*. A separation of the latter into two groups of equal value with the Lumbricina etc., the Capitibranchiati and Dorsibranchiati, as it is accepted after Cuvier almost everywhere to the present day — only Milne Edwards (1) unites them (as Annelides) over against the Lumbricina (Scoleidis M. Edw.) into a common order —, is hardly admissible. Not even as suborders would these two groups commend themselves, because the transitions between them are so many and so manifold. — The setting together of the Nematoda with the Chétopodes Blainv. needs, after what has been said above, no special justification any more. As little as the absence of the fleshy body processes hinders us from uniting the Lumbricina (Abranches sétigères Cuv.) with the Branchiata (2), just as little does the absence of the bristle-shaped epidermoidal appendages entitle us to a separation of the Nematoda.

**Arthropoda.**

The great division of the Arthropoda or arthropods (Condylopoda Latr., Loricata Nitzsch) answers in its essential content quite completely to the class of the Insecta set up by Linné. With acuteness this great naturalist already recognised the numerous and so **[p. 78]** very manifold forms of this circle in their inner connection. Separated from the remaining invertebrate animals (which were gathered in a single class of the Vermes, first dissolved by Cuvier), they form in the Systema naturae, certainly with the best right, a quite independent division. That single few groups, like the Lernaeada and Cirripedia, remained wrongly excluded from the insects and found a place in various orders of the Vermes (the former with the Mollusca, the latter beside Chiton among the Testacea), one will be able to excuse the more easily, since the paradoxical form of these creatures had to remain completely unintelligible without knowledge of developmental history and therefore, as experience has shown, has given occasion down to our time to the most erroneous (1) opinions.

What may be objected against the setting together of the arthropods with the worms in a common great circle of the Animalia articulata or Arthrozoa (Polymeria) has already been subjected to an examination above. In what follows it will emerge ever more how much the arthropods deserve to be regarded as an independent division and set beside the worms, as beside the remaining main groups of animal formations. The likeness of the arthropods and worms is, in my view, without all deeper connection. It rests on a mere crossing of the form-determining laws and shows only that here and there, even before the arising of a definite typical plan, a single formative process answering to this plan is also elsewhere — I might almost say, by way of trial — **[p. 79]** brought into use by Nature, yet without then possessing a like pervasive significance and dominion.

Nevertheless the union of the worms and arthropods rests on a definite likeness in the outer form, on a likeness which is conditioned by the — according to the typical significance, to be sure, somewhat differing — employment of a like morphogenetic process. For this reason that procedure may also still be defended up to a certain point. But when Lamarck, Latreille and Carus add to the arthropods and annelids also the molluscs and form out of these three groups a single common division (Animaux sensibles Lam., Cephalidia Latr., Corpozoa Car.), there results from it a mere irrational heap of forms, whose inner emptiness of content lies too openly in the day for a word more to be wasted on it. —

The likeness of the arthropods to the annelids, especially to the gill-worms, rests preferably in the like long-stretched and symmetrical form, as well as in the segmentation of the body. What however already in the latter respect distinguishes the arthropods is a striking, most characteristic heteronomy of the segments, with a peculiar development (completely foreign to the type of the worms) of the paired appendages on the segments. These appendages are not merely everywhere fastened movably beside the median line of the belly — not, as in the gill-worms, on the side surfaces of the body or at once on belly and back — and jointed when they appear as locomotor instruments, but also participate just as completely as the individual segments in the heteronomy of the body.

Already from the arrangement of these appendages it may be inferred how in the arthropods the opposition between belly and back is so very much more strongly marked than **[p. 80]** in the worms, how the former here possesses before the latter a very distinguished morphological dignity. In agreement with this, then, the structure of the nervous system in the arthropods is also in morphological respect another than in the worms. A nuchal ganglion such as the latter animals possess is lacking to the arthropods. The central parts of the nervous system, which, answering to the segments, appear as a series of chain-like connected, often also more or less mutually fused ganglia, belong solely to the ventral surface of the body. Even the so-called brain of the arthropods, which differs from the remaining ganglia only by its position in front of the mouth opening. With the nuchal ganglion of the worms it cannot be held together, since this constantly belongs to the dorsal surface. That, moreover, in the arthropods too the so-called brain very often has a similar position cannot surprise us given the small room of the head. Easily can it be pushed up from front and below to behind and above as far as the initial part of the oesophagus. The pharyngeal ring of the arthropods is under such circumstances without all morphological significance. It becomes necessary solely through the arrangement of the intestine and the position of the mouth opening in the hind part of the head. Like the oesophagus, quite so are sometimes also other structures lying in the median line of the belly between two ganglia embraced by the lateral commissures. So e.g. in many grasshoppers in the thoracic cavity particular strong inward-directed processes of the outer skeleton, in many caterpillars the insertion points of muscles etc. In this relation it finds its explanation why in many cases the commissures of the pharyngeal collar can shorten so quite extraordinarily strongly. Even the absence of a proper brain really observed sometimes (in Acanthocercus (1), Dichelestium? (2)) **[p. 81]** may with such an arrangement be much more easily comprehensible than if the ganglion concerned, a structure of independent kind, stood in a certain opposition to the remaining parts of the central nervous system, as one must assume where one compares it with the nuchal ganglion of the worms.

Besides these, as it seems to me, quite fundamental differences of the arthropods and worms there are found between the two divisions also many other anatomical differences. So the circulatory system of the former is everywhere in greater or lesser extent incomplete, never perfectly closed, as in the annelids. The motor of the blood movement is formed by a vessel-like tube which, always unpaired, lies in the median line of the back above the intestine and only in few cases shortens into a heart-shaped sac. The generative organs are restricted in their position everywhere to a quite definite section of the body, the so-called abdomen. Never do they extend uniformly, as in the worms, through the whole length of the body. — Instead of the smooth muscle fibres there are found in the whole division of the arthropods throughout cross-striated fibres, even on the viscera. Characteristic for the arthropods is also the circumstance that vibratile cilia, even in the embryonic periods, are constantly lacking. Indisputably such a phenomenon stands in a quite definite relation to the constitution of the outer coverings and the epithelial lining of the inner organs. These consist in the division of the arthropods of a very peculiar substance, of chitin, which thickens extraordinarily easily and presents on the surface of the body a protecting armour, an outer skeleton, whose firmness is still strengthened by a quantity of embedded lime salts.

In order now, despite this arrangement, to secure to the body of the **[p. 82]** arthropods a certain degree of mobility, which was the more necessary as the animals concerned, at least the most highly developed forms, are destined for a life on land, a quite particular provision had to be made. The outer coverings might not cover the whole body in a continuous layer, like a rigid armour or a shell. They had to fall into a number of firm horny rings lying one behind another, which are connected with one another by a more delicate skin and are thereby enabled to change their mutual position.

In this circumstance alone the teleological significance of segmentation seems to me to be founded. In the annelids it is therefore also more of subordinate dignity both for the whole economy and in morphological respect. It offers here the beginning of a formation which only later, under other relations, attains a pervasive typical significance and is left to the play of the formative forces for the production of the most manifold forms.

The means of which the creative power of Nature here preferably makes use is a definite, very peculiar heteronomous development of the individual rings. In the annelids these showed essentially a like formation at all parts of the body. Otherwise in the arthropods. Here they present manifold differences in form and connection and group themselves together everywhere in the developed individuals (1) into a number of larger sections, which one is accustomed to designate according to position and arrangement as head, thorax, belly (abdomen) and hind body (postabdomen). To these four morphological sections, however, one must, as I believe, add **[p. 83]** a fifth, which I shall mention henceforth as fore-head. — Only in rare cases, however, are all these sections completely developed side by side. Now they fuse in greater or lesser extent, now also one or another of them is lacking, as especially the hind body, which of all those parts has the most restricted spread and outside the group of the crustaceans is found only in the Scorpionida (1).

All individual segments of these sections are, even if not in like measure in the different groups, enabled to the development of lateral appendages, as in the gill-worms. However, these extremities differ from the corresponding structures of the latter creatures partly by their anatomical arrangement, partly also by this, that they take part in the heteronomy of the body. So they appear, although morphologically completely agreeing among themselves (2), now as antennae (on the fore-head), now as feeding instruments (on the head), now as locomotor apparatus or holdfast organs (on thorax and abdomen), now as so-called false legs — which are often in turn reshaped into gills, accessory sexual parts etc. — (on the postabdomen). Constantly, however, such appendages are present only on the fore body of the arthropods, on fore-head, head and thorax. Only in the crustaceans and myriapods are they found uniformly also on the sections lying behind.

Given the very intricate morphological relations of the arthropods it is explicable if one is still today of the most divergent view on the interpretation **[p. 84]** and the connection of the skeleton in these animals. It took, after all, a long time before one recognised at all the morphological agreement of the individual body segments and their appendages. Of imperishable value in this respect are the careful investigations of Savigny (1) on the feeding instruments of the arthropods and their relation to the legs in the Hexapoda, Arachnida, Crustacea and Myriapoda; investigations which later were further carried out in several respects by Dugès (2) for the Arachnida, as by Brandt (3) for the crabs, and which have been completely confirmed in their most essential content. Equally significant for the morphological conception of structure in the arthropods is the comprehensive ingenious attempt of Erichson (4). Unfortunately here too, as in Savigny, merely the comparative-anatomical standpoint is quite simply emphasised, and developmental history, despite the numerous brilliant discoveries of Rathke (5) in this field, has remained completely unregarded. Certainly, however, it is a sure guarantee for the correctness of Erichson's assumptions that these, as it seems to me, find their confirmation in all essential points precisely in developmental history. To be sure, Zaddach (6), who first drew the results of the latter into the range of these investigations, has several times reached divergent results, yet I cannot everywhere agree with him, as shall at once be more closely stated and motivated, however surprising and acceptable **[p. 85]** many of his statements may appear at first sight.

The development of the arthropods proceeds, like that of the Branchiata and Hirudinea, from a primitive part which answers to the later ventral surface. Thereby, however, the arthropods already distinguish themselves in this first stage of development from those worms, that the traces of articulation appear much earlier, mostly already at a time in which the yolk is by no means yet overgrown by the germinal membrane (answering to the serous layer in the vertebrates). Only few groups (the Arthrostraca (1) and Entomostraca) are those in which the articulation makes itself known only later, after the overgrowth mentioned has already completely taken place. But here too this does not arise, as in the annelids, from the very first by the formation of complete segments on which only afterward the appendages sprout, but by this, that directly beside the median line of the primitive streak a series of paired wart-shaped elevations forms, which gradually grow out into the extremities, while the ringing advances from the belly toward the back and only thereby conditions the arising of particular segments. In this early mode of formation of the extremities, then, the reason is indisputably **[p. 86]** to be sought why in the arthropods they are not placed on the side surfaces of the body segments but on the belly, close beside the median line.

Very distinctly it may be gathered from this relation that in the arthropods the extremities possess a much greater morphological dignity than in the Branchiata. While in the latter they arise as mere appendages of the segments, in the former they precede the conformation of these sections. They have, over against the segments, gained in independence. Not everywhere, however, is this relation marked with like sharpness. In some cases even the segments arise first, as in the Branchiata, while the extremities, though perhaps not all, sprout on them only later. So especially in many Hexapoda (e.g. the Diptera, where the legs of the thorax form only in the course of the metamorphosis, after the ringing is already completely present), in the mites, which at first lack the last pair of legs, etc. The most striking exception in this respect is made by the postabdomen, which develops after the usual scheme only in the Arthrostraca (1). In all other cases it arises as an independent appendage at the end of the primitive streak, at first as a wart-like prominence, which only gradually draws itself out into a tube-like, cylindrical structure (2). Only after the formation of the segments **[p. 87]** does the postabdomen furnish itself with its extremities. Whether, moreover, here too the articulation gradually ascends from the ventral surface to the back is still uncertain, yet by analogy not improbable. Otherwise at least we meet everywhere this mode of formation, even where the segments lack extremities, as on the abdomen of the spiders (1) and Hexapoda.

Not always, however, does the articulation rise to the production of complete segments. In many cases, as on the thorax and abdomen of many crustaceans (e.g. the Decapoda), as especially everywhere on the head, the individual segments cannot be proved as isolated formations, although the corresponding extremities are completely developed. On back and sides they are then joined with one another into an unarticulated mass. Such a relation, however, does not entitle us, as Erichson wishes for the head, to the assumption that now this mass too presents only a single segment. Already Rathke (2) has very aptly furnished the proof that the number of extremity pairs present everywhere allows one to infer the existence of a like number of segments, even where these cannot be anatomically proved. Into the formation of the head, then, according to this, just as many rings have entered as paired appendages are found.

The first rudiment of all paired appendages present on the ventral surface of the individual segments, however much they may differ anatomically and functionally from one another, is the same. Only gradually do all those numerous forms develop, through manifold differences in the development of these parts, which we perceive in the developed **[p. 88]** arthropods. Some, like the feeding instruments and false feet, simply remain behind in their development, while others, like the thoracic feet of most Decapoda and Entomostraca, attain their final shape through the process of retrogression. In manifold employment appear here all the morphogenetic processes mentioned above, of arrested formation and of further formation in the embryonic sense, of progressive and retrograde metamorphosis. The more special proof we leave to developmental history. Here it would remove us too far from our purpose. As a peculiarity of the crustacean type let only the tendency indwelling in the individual extremities to split lengthwise be mentioned — a tendency which we have met in analogous manner in the Branchiata. In the crustaceans too the two parts thereby arisen usually diverge very strikingly from one another in their later development. Both follow, independently of one another, the laws of shape formation mentioned, each in a different way.

As already stated, however, not all rings of the body are equally fitted for the production of extremities. In the Hexapoda and spiders this restricts itself solely to the girdles of the fore body, to head and thorax. Where in these arthropods besides movable appendages still occur, as especially in the six-footed insects at the tip of the abdomen, such have everywhere arisen from a metamorphosis of the individual segments themselves and are never to be compared with the proper extremities (1). Otherwise, however, is the relation in the **[p. 89]** Myriapoda and Crustacea, in which, in the norm at least, all segments of the body, also those of the abdomen and hind body, take part in the formation of extremities (1).

Besides the structures hitherto mentioned there are found, moreover, in the arthropods still various other appendages on the individual segments, though far from so generally spread as the extremities of the ventral surface. Known before all are the wings of the Hexapoda, which belong to the second and third thoracic ring and in form and development not seldom present very considerable differences. They are articulated on the dorsal surface, as the legs on the ventral. With the latter they show in general so manifold analogies (2), even in their development (which agrees essentially completely with the development of the false feet), that the pronouncement does not seem to me too bold that the wings are the morphological equivalents of the legs and mere repetitions of these structures on the dorsal surface. That the wings everywhere form much later than the extremities of the ventral surface, that they never, like these, take part in the formation of the segments, can condition no essential difference. We find the same relation, after all, also in the limbs of the postabdomen and in some cases (see above) even in those of the thorax, which nevertheless belong with the feeding instruments etc. to one and the same organ group. That difference in the time of development of wings and legs is now, to be sure, the more striking as it concerns two corresponding parts of a common segment. **[p. 90]** However, this circumstance too loses weight when we consider how the development of the individual segments proceeds from the ventral surface, and therefore the formation of the ventral appendages also becomes possible much sooner. Where the segments arise without such extremities, where the latter, as e.g. in the Diptera, are only formed afterward, this process coincides in time with the sprouting of the wings.

The simultaneous appearance of dorsal and ventral limbs in the Hexapoda is a repetition of that arrangement which we have proved above as characteristic for the Branchiata. Evidently, however, it has for the type of the arthropods far from so pervasive, so important a significance. The opposing difference of belly and back is here much too great for a definite direction in the formative activity on the one surface to have to call forth with necessity a corresponding process on the other. The development of the ventral surface is by far the prevailing one. On it the extremities are never completely lacking, while the formation of such appendages on the back is to be regarded as exception. Hence the restricted and fluctuating occurrence of these structures. Interesting, moreover, it is that in many cases already in the Branchiata (in the Phyllodoceae and Aphroditeae) the cirri of the dorsal surface differ in their development from the corresponding structures of the belly and then in shape recall the wings of the Hexapoda.

Already some time ago Oken (1), to whom morphology in general owes so many very valuable and acute disclosures, paralleled the wings of the Hexapoda with the gills of the crabs. Naturally in such a comparison, of the individual morphologically different appendages of the crustacean body which function as respiratory organs, only those structures can be drawn upon **[p. 91]** which do not belong in the type of the usual extremity formation, namely then the gills of the Decapoda. Given the arrangement of these parts, which, like the wings of the insects, are placed in pairs on the (abdominal) segments and indeed mostly above the individual ventral limbs, such a supposition gains the greatest probability. It also receives a new support in this, that we see how in the crabs the reshaping of the extremities into gills is so extraordinarily frequent. Even the circumstance seems to furnish no convincing counter-proof, that these gills in many Decapoda give up their anatomical independence, that they move down on the side parts of the body until they find their insertion point on the basal joints of the adjoining legs. This would, after all, be a mere modification of that process which we have likewise found in the Branchiata. Here too the upper and lower extremities (belonging to back and belly) not seldom fuse on the side surfaces of the individual segments. Still more inessential is it that the gills of the Decapoda are covered over by the lateral prolongations of the dorsal shield and lie hidden in the cavities thereby formed. According to their development the gills are outer appendages of the body. With the same right as the gills of the Decapoda, the leaf-like appendages found by Rathke (1) in the embryos of Asellus aquaticus on the dorsal surface of a middle body ring may also claim to be the morphological equivalents of the wings in the Hexapoda.

The Okenian view discussed above, that the gills of the Decapoda answer to the wings of the six-footed insects, and so, like these latter, are to be referred to the type of extremity formation, has, moreover, found no **[p. 92]** general applause. Other very weighty authorities have rather pronounced that the morphological equivalents of the wings are to be sought in the so-called dorsal shield of the crabs. So especially Rathke (1) believed he saw already in the grown-together wings of many Coleoptera an indication toward the formation of the dorsal shield. Zaddach (2) too pronounces for the analogy of the parts concerned. However, in my view such an assumption can hardly be held. To understand the formation of the dorsal shield one must remember that the individual segments on the body of the arthropods are not mere solid rings, but are themselves in turn (in most cases at least) composed of several elements, which belong partly to back and belly, partly however also to the side surfaces. The latter are known under the designations of the epimera and episterna. Of them the former lie at the sides of the dorsal plate, the others at the sides of the ventral plate. Such lateral elements can be proved both in the Crustacea and in the Hexapoda (where Stein has recently (3) designated them as parapleurae and pleurae). While, moreover, in the Hexapoda of these pieces especially the latter are distinguished by their development, in the Crustacea it is precisely the former. They form here usually, e.g. in the Arthrostraca, very considerable plates projecting more or less far at the sides, which not seldom even curve round toward the ventral surface. In analogous arrangement these pieces appear **[p. 93]** at times also where they hang together continuously with the intervening dorsal plates, where they do not appear as isolated, independent skeletal parts. If then at the same time the segments at any region of the body are not completely separated, if the dorsal plates there are fused with one another, there must arise a shield-shaped covering whose lateral edges project in greater or lesser extent and cover the adjoining parts as soon as they bend downward. Such a shield now is the dorsal shield of the Decapoda. That it has arisen in the way just stated from the development of the upper side pieces and the dorsal plates of the segments connected with them is very distinct in Hippolyte, Peneus, Palaemon and others. Here the segments of the postabdomen too show a like conformation. Only in this do they differ, that the individual dorsal pieces of them do not hang together continuously among themselves.

In some cases the dorsal shield of the Crustacea attains a still more considerable independence. It lifts itself off in greater extent from the underlying segment and grows even to a degree that it encloses the whole body. In the median line of the back it articulates itself by a longitudinal fold. So it forms the bivalve shell of the Daphniae, Cyprides, Cirripedia. In Pandarus, where a similar reshaping of the dorsal pieces sets in, the structures concerned remain constantly separated from one another on the individual segments. They are not grown together into a connected shield, as is otherwise so often the case. Especially here it is that the outer appearance can easily occasion the supposition that the appendages concerned answer to the wings of the Hexapoda.

The articulation of the body in the arthropods, as well as the formation of the extremities, happens, as in the annelids, in general gradually advancing from front to back. However, this norm holds less for **[p. 94]** the whole body than rather properly only for its individual sections, which up to a certain degree, as we have already stated above for the postabdomen, form independently and separately from one another. Only where the development of these sections happens quickly one after another, without perceptible break, without pause and from the very first with the lawful number of segments, only there does it have the appearance that the rings of the whole body form in continuous connection and uniformly from front to back. In this case, then, the embryo also offers from the very beginning in its conformation a great likeness to the developed animal, even if its appendages have perhaps not yet reached their development. Such is found, as it seems, preferably in the six-footed insects (1) and Arachnida, but also in many Crustacea (as especially in the Amphipoda).

In other cases, however, the embryo, when it leaves the egg envelopes — even apart from the constitution of the extremities — has still far from the shape as it is found in the developed individuals. The number of its body segments is often smaller than in the developed state. As a rule under such circumstances the last segments of the abdomen are lacking (as e.g. in the Isopoda), or even the whole body section concerned (as in many Decapoda, in the Entomostraca and Myriapoda), so that besides fore-head and hind body then merely head and thorax compose the body.

The latter relation is the more striking as it shows us that not even the individual sections of the body need arise in continuous sequence answering to their position. The end part of the body, the postabdomen, is here formed earlier than the abdomen lying before it. However, this is to be regarded only as exception. **[p. 95]** It finds its explanation in this, that the postabdomen is enabled precisely by its peculiar mode of development to appear quite independently already at a time in which hardly the rudiments of the rest of the body are laid down. The preceding sections (if at least we leave the fore-head aside for the time being) are always laid down in series one after another, first the head, last the abdomen, and, independently of one another, severally developed gradually from front to back. The hind segments of each section always appear before the front ones of the same section. Likewise the extremities. The mandibles precede the maxillae in their formation, the front feet of the thorax the hind ones, even if the latter again in many cases (in many insects e.g.) form earlier than the hind extremities of the head. Another, though only apparent, exception in this respect is made by the antennae, which very often are laid down not before but after the mandibles, not merely in the crabs (very evidently e.g. in the Lernaeada), but also in the insects, in which they not seldom are even lacking during the whole larval state (in the Diptera). This, however, may well be interpreted to mean that the antennae do not, as one has hitherto quite generally assumed after the anatomical arrangement, belong with the feeding instruments to the same body section, but form a system of segments of their own, different from it, of which mention has already been made above under the designation of the fore-head. A confirmation of this supposition I find in this, that in the Stomatopoda such a section is really found as an independent part consisting of several segments. In all other cases, by contrast, it is without demonstrable articulation and also always fused with the proper head, which bears the mouth opening; a relation which we meet in analogous manner so very often also in the remaining sections.

**[p. 96]** In its highest development, in the so-called Podophthalma, the fore-head consists of three girdles, which then always, even where they are not present as separate segments, can be proved by three corresponding pairs of appendages. The first pair of these bears under such circumstances at its tip the visual instruments, while the two hind ones, which (in accordance with the type of extremity formation in the Crustacea) are split and developed in a different way in their individual parts thereby arisen, appear as antennae. The connection of the eyes with the foremost pair of these three extremities I can hold only for an accidental one, not however for essential. The so-called eye-stalk is in my opinion quite the same structure as the antennae lying behind it, only more rudimentary than these — a circumstance which perhaps depends precisely on the peculiar employment of it and the connection with those sense organs. That these never appear in another shape than in that of eye-stalks can hardly speak against this view, and much less prove (as Zaddach (1) supposes) that the arising of the visual instruments is to be referred to a limb formation. How e.g. would it be possible to vindicate a particular segment for the eyes of the Arachnida? With the view pronounced above, by contrast, that that connection with the first pair of the limbs fastened on the fore-head is only an accidental one, every other position of these sense instruments may easily be reconciled. Everywhere where those extremities are not formed, among the Crustacea in the so-called Edriophthalma, as in the insects, they retain their original position on the fore-head or also, when this is completely lacking and the head is fused with the thorax, on the so-called cephalothorax. Such a relation finds **[p. 97]** a very fitting analogy in the different position of the presumed organ of hearing in the Orthoptera, discovered by v. Siebold (1). This too, as we have learned through this excellent anatomist, is sometimes (in the Locustinae) seized on by the extremities of the thorax, while in the Acridia it presents a divergent position on the fore part of the abdomen. Moreover, finally, the development of the eye-stalks, as Rathke e.g. found it in the crayfish, speaks fully in favour of our opinion. At the beginning of development, before the appearance of the eyes, the stalk-shaped bearers of these organs resemble quite the first rudiments of the antennae.

To judge by the development of the whole three pairs of feelers in the arthropod just named, the segments of the fore-head with their respective limbs form, moreover, in continuous sequence not from front to back, like the girdles of the proper body sections, but from back to front. The whole fore-head thus appears, quite like the postabdomen, far rather as an appendage of the body or rather of the primitive part than as an integral section of it. Only for these holds the law stated above of development from front to back. In agreement with this behaviour it is that the fore-head, when it begins to become rudimentary, loses not its hind but its front joints. In the Arthrostraca it consists on each side only of two segments more, which are represented by the appended antennae (which evidently answer to the two pairs of feelers of the Podophthalma), in the six-footed insects and the Myriapoda even only of a single ring, whose appendages one may certainly rightly parallel with the last antennal pair of those first-named crabs. In most Entomostraca finally (with the exception of the **[p. 98]** Siphonostoma and Lernaeada (1)), as in the spiders, every trace of the fore-head has vanished. The antennae are lacking to these arthropods, for what one regards in the Entomostraca as such has in the majority of cases (in Daphnia e.g.), as we shall see at once, a quite different significance.

With the exception of the Stomatopoda, moreover, as already mentioned, the fore-head has everywhere given up its anatomical independence. In all other arthropods it is fused with the following section, with the proper head. This now consists, where it is completely developed, of four pairs of limbs with their respective girdles, which latter, to be sure, always hang together more or less completely into a common capsule-like structure. The limbs of the head are the upper lip, the two mandibles and two pairs of so-called maxillae, of which the hind ones in the six-footed insects are more or less fused with one another in the median line into an unpaired part, the lower lip.

The upper lip one must, after its whole origin, reckon to the organ group formed by the ventral appendages of the body segments. That it is constantly unpaired (2) can hardly justify the inconsistency of which one, on the contrary, makes oneself guilty. We find, after all, so often that definite laterally symmetrical structures in animals with lateral type are replaced by an unpaired middle element. So here too. Instead of two lateral appendages only a single one develops in the median line. That this however is the case here seems on the other side again to speak for the independence of the **[p. 99]** head from the fore-head. By far in the greater majority of cases we find such a relation not in the midst of a running series of homologous paired structures, but at one or the other end of it. — Of all the various appendages of the segments the upper lip seems (also in the insects?) to arise first. It appears as a wart-shaped prominence at the front end of the primitive streak, which mostly develops into a transverse plate, in other cases (in the Entomostraca and also the Pycnogonida) however into a short tube, which then bears the mouth opening on its tip and functions as sucking tube. In this form it persists not seldom (in the Siphonostoma and Pycnogonida (1)), while otherwise (e.g. in Cyclops (2), in the Lernaeada etc.) it later likewise, as the hind wall gradually vanishes, becomes a plate-shaped structure. Behind it now arise the remaining appendages of the head, first the mandibles, later the maxillae, the front and hind, now (in the higher crabs, as in most insects) in quick sequence one after another, now however also only after a considerable interval of time, after the legs of the thorax, at least the front ones, are already laid down. The latter is especially the case in the Entomostraca furnished with a sucking proboscis (where through this late arising of the jaws moreover many other interesting metamorphoses become possible), but also, after the discovery of Kröyer (3), in the Pycnogonida and even in many insects (especially Diptera), where then the maxillae, and indeed mostly only the lower ones, are lacking during the whole larval state.

**[p. 100]** The relation just adduced in the development of the feeding instruments seems to me well worth considering in judging the question of the relation of these structures. Variously, namely, the assertion has been set up, as especially by Burmeister and recently again by Zaddach (1), that the feeding instruments, at least the two pairs of lower jaws, belong not to the head but properly to the thorax. Their connection with the former would accordingly not be essential and by no means have its foundation in the typical arrangement of the head. A priori this assertion can the less be rejected, as we see that with a fusion of the head with the thorax lying behind it, now (in the spiders) these appendages really — but completely only the hind pair of maxillae — become quite conformable to the proper legs and function as walking instruments, now also (in the higher crabs) the proper thoracic appendages all (Stomatopoda and Decapoda, with the exception of the so-called Schizopoda, of Hippolyte and others) or only with their front pair (Arthrostraca) are reshaped into auxiliary jaws and join the feeding instruments.

However, it seems to me as if such an assumption had arisen merely through a too one-sided consideration of such a mode of development occurring in the spiders and higher crabs. Precisely in these arthropods, however, the relations are less clear because of the peculiar connection of head and thorax. Easily can a conception like that of Burmeister gain here a great probability when one sees how the hind jaws and the legs of the thorax not only arise in continuous sequence one behind **[p. 101]** another, but also present a uniform form (the latter, however, as we shall yet see, with some exceptions which speak very evidently for the view of Erichson). In the Entomostraca, however, and the insects the relations are otherwise. Even if we do not wish to adduce the former here as probative, because in them the morphological arrangement might perhaps admit manifold doubts and various interpretations, yet the insects seem to prove the correctness of the usual assumption. In them maxillae and legs form independently of one another, and so little generally in continuous sequence that the latter not seldom precede the former in the time of their arising. It would, however, be a very striking violation of the law already discussed above if in these cases the hind appendages of the thorax should sprout sooner than the front ones. Where otherwise such a relation takes place, on the fore-head, as perhaps on the postabdomen, definite grounds for it, standing in agreement with the whole mode of development of the sections concerned, can always be stated — which however here would hardly be possible.

Besides, we see, where the head is separated from the thorax, everywhere on the former the three jaw pairs adduced. Even in Galeodes, where only a trace of this separation still appears and the palps of the maxillae (1), especially of the hind ones, already almost quite resemble the walking feet, even here these still find their insertion on the head, not on the thorax.

What likewise still seems to speak in favour of the usual assumption is the quite constant fusion in the insects of the hind pair of maxillae into the so-called lower lip. As already mentioned above, such **[p. 102]** fusion is to be met preferably only at the front and hind end of a running series of homologous paired structures. Another behaviour is always to be regarded as exception and very rare. In case now the maxillae were really to be reckoned to the thorax, we should have here such an exception, since then not the first pair of the thoracic appendages but the second would present a fusion in the middle line.

Under such circumstances now I believe I can for the maxillae without great hesitation assent to the opinion of Erichson, according to which the structures concerned are regarded as extremities of the head and only — if I may so say — exceptionally, in the spiders, are transformed into walking instruments agreeing with the legs of the thorax.

The variations in the development of the appendages on the head are manifold. In many Entomostraca, e.g. in Acanthocerus (1) and in various parasites (2), the hind pair of the maxillae seems to be lacking, likewise in the spiders the upper lip. As to the former of these relations, it probably reduces itself quite simply to this, that here the hind maxillary pair, which otherwise too develops latest, is not formed (3) at all, **[p. 103]** while in the second case the lack of the upper lip seems to stand in some connection with the simultaneous absence of the whole fore-head. The laying down of the upper lip, namely, happens, to judge by the Rathkean observations, pretty much at the same level as the hind antennae — an arrangement which, moreover, is also otherwise observed in the grouping of the appendages of two adjoining, incompletely bounded and fused segments. Only later, so it seems, do upper lip and antennae move further apart, so that the latter come to lie quite distinctly before the former. Under such relations now the absence of the antennae, as we meet it in the spiders, can certainly also easily bring about the want of the upper lip.

As now, moreover, to the transformation of the hind jaws into walking instruments occurring in the spiders (1), this cannot strike us given the morphological identity (2) of the individual body rings and their appendages, the less so as we perceive at the same time how the head thereby ceases to appear as a particular, anatomically bounded body part, and fuses with the thorax lying behind it into a common mass. On the same morphological identity rests also the possibility of the transformation of the thoracic appendages into so-called accessory jaws occurring in the higher crabs, in the Malacostraca, into structures which according to their anatomical structure resemble the maxillae far more than the legs, although they, not the walking instruments lying behind them (which must be interpreted as **[p. 104]** appendages of the abdomen), answer to the proper legs of the insects, as Savigny has already most convincingly shown. In agreement with this it is, then, that in these crabs the segments of the thorax (all, or only in part) are fused with the head, not, as in the spiders, the head with the thorax.

The thorax itself consists, as it seems, everywhere of three segments and of a corresponding number of paired appendages. In the six-footed insects these latter form the three pairs of walking instruments, in the Arachnida only the three hind pairs, since, as stated, the front pair occurring in them has arisen from a metamorphosis of the hind jaws. In the Malacostraca, by contrast, these thoracic feet pass as accessory jaws to the head, now (in the Podophthalma) all, now also only (in the Arthrostraca, with the exception of the Pranizidae, where the same relations recur as in the Podophthalma, as of the Laemodipoda, where two thoracic feet are transformed, though both not equally completely) with their front pair. In the latter case, then, there remain only two thoracic legs, which, as in the Myriapoda, join backward the appendages of the abdomen and agree with these also in their form. Moreover such a relation finds an indication already in some Decapoda. Here too that metamorphosis sometimes (in the Schizopoda, Hippolyte etc.) extends only to the two front appendages, so that then the hind pair persists in its proper form and is used as walking instrument.

The development of the appendages of the thorax happens, quite as stated above also of those of the head, from front to back, and likewise so that the last of them sometimes, as especially in the mites, but also in some Entomostraca, are laid down only much later than the front ones, only at a time when the young have already **[p. 105]** long left the egg envelopes. From the formation of the feeding instruments, moreover, that of the thoracic appendages is quite independent, as the Entomostraca, but also many insects, quite distinctly prove, where these, even if only in their front pairs, arise already at a time in which the jaws are by no means yet present in lawful number. That however in other cases the appendages of head and thorax, like those of a common section, come forth quickly one behind another in continuous series, is quite natural when we only consider that such is precisely only the case where head and thorax are fused with one another and the appendages concerned of both parts are shaped in a like way.

Of the highest interest, moreover, is it that even under such relations in some Decapoda the metamorphosis of the thoracic legs into accessory jaws is really an actual one. In the undeveloped individuals of Hyas, Galathea, Pagurus (1), of Caridina (2), Palaemon and certainly in a greater number (3) of related crabs, when the abdomen with its appendages is still little developed, the three pairs of thoracic feet are distinguished before the jaws of the head by a wholly divergent shape. They are of a very considerable length, of similar form as at first the abdominal legs of Astacus marinus, and serve, like these, as locomotor instruments, as swimming feet. Here, then, the appendages concerned appear under relations which recall too strikingly the arrangement occurring in the insects and spiders for one to be able to mistake the complete analogy with the thoracic legs of these arthropods. But as hereby on the one side the opinion of Burmeister, **[p. 106]** Milne Edwards and others is refuted, that the abdominal feet of the Decapoda and other crabs are to be paralleled with the thoracic feet of the insects, and the greater number of the jaws in the crabs would proceed simply from a multiplication of the appendages of the head already occurring in the insects, so the present relation furnishes also on the other side, in my view, a new proof that the three hind accessory jaws of the Podophthalma belong to a section of their own, different from the corresponding front structures, that therefore not, as Burmeister and Zaddach wish, the two pairs of lower jaws of the insects answering to the latter are likewise to be vindicated for the thorax.

In Astacus marinus during the foetal period the shape and function of the thoracic feet, at least of the two hind pairs, is quite similar. The front pair, by contrast, is completely like the lower jaws lying before it. Its ground, moreover, this latter circumstance may perhaps find in the relatively early appearing formation of the abdominal limbs, which makes such a representation by the proper thoracic legs, at least in the whole extent, unnecessary. Where the abdomen with its extremities develops still earlier, in A. fluviatilis, all three thoracic legs appear from the very beginning in a shape quite analogous to the maxillae.

In the Entomostraca the paired appendages of the thorax likewise appear at the beginning as mighty swimming feet. Only the Daphniidae make an exception, which however probably reduces itself to this, that these hatch much later than the other related crustaceans and run through the earlier stages of development, precisely those in which otherwise the thoracic appendages present that form, still in the interior of the egg envelopes. According to the observations of Jurine (1) at least, in general **[p. 107]** the processes of development seem to differ only inconsiderably. The thoracic legs arise, moreover, in the Entomostraca much earlier than otherwise usual. At the time of their formation, of the appendages of the head only the tube-shaped upper lip is yet laid down. In this, then, it probably also has its ground that the thoracic feet mostly sprout either close behind that upper lip (e.g. in Lernaeopoda, Balanus), or even (in Cyclops (1)) at the sides (2) of it. Only in rare cases are upper lip and front thoracic appendages separated from one another by a greater interval. So it seemed to me e.g. in the embryos of Caligus.

When now, however, later the feeding instruments arise close behind the upper lip, the two front pairs of the thoracic legs move up to before the mouth opening or at least up to its level, if they did not already from the very beginning hold this position, while the last pair removes itself ever more backward from it. So it becomes possible that the jaws — a relation which at first sight seems so very paradoxical — have their position behind the front appendages of the thorax. Many Entomostraca possess, moreover, at the beginning only the two first pairs of thoracic legs, like many mites. So e.g. Achtheres, Tracheliastes, Anchorella. However, it is still doubtful whether in such cases the last pair is also really formed afterward, and not rather constantly lacking.

Very divergently from the interpretation just given of the swimming feet in the embryos of the Entomostraca, which was first set up with all its consequences by Erichson, Zaddach has recently declared the structures concerned to be the appendages of the head. He parallels them — in accordance with his view of the structure of this section **[p. 108]** — with the two antennal pairs and the mandibles of the Malacostraca. However little I can agree with the mode of view of this zoologist on the composition of the head, just as little does this interpretation of the swimming feet also seem to me in agreement with the morphological arrangement. Precisely developmental history, to which Zaddach refers, prevents in my view such a conception and justifies the older assumption. Never, so far as we know up to now, do the hind pairs of the oar-feet become the mandibles. On the contrary these form, as Rathke has proved for Cyclops, quite independently with the maxillae lying behind them in the gap between the mouth opening and the last pair of thoracic legs, which by its position, as it seems, constantly, also in the developed state, makes itself known as a pair of limbs of the thorax. But that the front oar-feet of the Entomostraca belong with this latter to the same section can hardly be drawn into doubt. They also differ, even after the later metamorphosis, from the antennae of the Malacostraca in several respects so strikingly that already Straus-Dürckheim and Rathke believed they had to separate them from these and regarded them as altered pairs of feet — an interpretation which stands completely in harmony with the development. That the appendages concerned at their arising agree in shape with the head limbs of many Malacostraca, which Zaddach emphasises, can only show that both kinds of structure morphologically belong to a like plan and type, not however that they are also to be paralleled in their relation to the sections of the body. As necessary for the assumption of Erichson, Zaddach designates the proof that the so-called antennae lie at some time of life behind the mandibles. To be sure, the dispute could easily be settled by this. Unfortunately, however, such a proof cannot be furnished — yet only for the reason **[p. 109]** that, so far as we know up to now, the mandibles always appear only at a time in which the front thoracic appendages have already taken up their position before the mouth opening or at its sides.

As, moreover, the thoracic limbs of the Decapoda, which at first function as swimming feet, later suffer a change of their original form, so also those of the Entomostraca. The metamorphoses of these are in the latter crustaceans even still much more striking and manifold. They change not merely in the individual groups and species, but here and there, in the Lernaeada (1), even in the individual sexes. Never, as it seems, do all three pairs remain what they were at first, swimming feet. Now only the middle pair (as in Daphnia, Lynceus etc.), now only (in Apus) the hind pair keeps this significance. It then shows, in the developed state, a branching and considerable size. The front pair, sometimes also (in the Cyclopidae) at the same time the middle one, transforms itself in the majority of the Entomostraca into a simple antenna-shaped structure, while the last in its shape usually holds the middle between the jaws and the abdominal appendages, or even quite resembles the ventral feet, as in the Cirripedia, Lophyropoda and many Copepoda (e.g. Pontia) (2). In the group of the Siphonostoma and Lernaeada the first pair of legs of the thorax becomes clasping feet, likewise mostly the remaining pairs, although these often present a somewhat different shape. In the female Lernaeada the third pair of clawed feet later grows together in the median line either merely at the tip or also down to the base into a quite peculiar holdfast apparatus. Likewise the front thoracic legs **[p. 110]** in the Cirripedia into the so-called stalk (Lepas) or the case (Balanus). In many cases the thoracic feet also vanish in the course of development. So in Apus (1) the middle pair, in the males of many Lernaeada the front, in Ergasilus the hind. In Chondracanthus, Epachthes, Penella, Lernaeocera even all three pairs are lost again by a retrograde metamorphosis (2).

The same anatomical connection which in the division of the arthropods is so often met between fore-head and head, between head and thorax, is found sometimes even between the thorax and the following abdomen. These two sections too are not always separated from one another and distinguished according to the development of their segments and extremities. Often they fuse with one another, either in whole extent (3), or only in part, when namely the thorax, as e.g. in the Arthrostraca, presents a different development in its front and hind half. In such cases, then, the appendages of the divisions concerned also show a like shape and development, if indeed the rings of the abdomen are furnished with them at all. As already mentioned, they are lacking in the six-footed insects and spiders.

The number of segments on the abdomen is not the same in all **[p. 111]** groups of the arthropods. It is rather subject to many fluctuations, and greater ones than the number of segments on the preceding sections of the body. In the Malacostraca it is in general five, in the six-footed insects twice five. Already here, however, manifold divergences set in. While in the Malacostraca only rarely (e.g. in Bopyrus) the last girdle remains undeveloped, there are found in the insects in the majority (yet not (1) everywhere) only nine abdominal segments, here and there even (e.g. in the Hydrocantharidae) only eight, of which then in addition the last are metamorphosed in the developed state in a remarkable way into the copulatory organs. Between five and ten the number of the rings concerned seems also to fluctuate in the Arachnida. Four abdominal segments, as in Bopyrus, are found likewise in a great multitude of Entomostraca, yet here too exceptions are not lacking. Most striking are those cases in which the number has increased by a very considerable amount. So especially in Apus, so also in the Myriapoda.

Where limbs occur on the abdomen, they are preferably organs of movement, now (in the Podophthalma, with the exception of the Schizopoda and some others) alone, now also in community with some (Arthrostraca, many Entomostraca) or all (Myriapoda) appendages of the thorax (2). So at least is the relation in the fully developed **[p. 112]** animals, while, as we have seen, in the earlier stages of development not seldom the legs of the thorax too are used in analogous manner. The number of the abdominal feet agrees everywhere with that of the corresponding segments, although these latter are not always quite completely separated from one another and here and there abdominal segments also occur which lack limbs, even in the crabs, in which otherwise, after all, such ventral limbs are met quite constantly. The most interesting example of the former behaviour is given us by the Julidae, in which, as is known, on each developed abdominal ring two pairs of walking legs are found — a relation which points to a very early fusion of each two segments, of which at first one really still meets some traces (1). In many Laemodipoda the two first pairs of feet of the abdomen are lacking, although the girdles concerned are quite completely developed. Probably, however, here too the legs were at first developed, only they did not reach their full formation and were lost again (2). For this speaks at least the circumstance that sometimes a distinct rudiment of them still occurs, and that the gills present on the same segments too have probably arisen only from the metamorphosis of one leaf of the extremities. Moreover we know through v. Nordmann's fine investigations that the Lernaeada too, which in the developed state mostly completely lack ventral limbs (only in Penella, Peniculus and some others are some remnants of them still found), at first really possess such appendages.

The abdomen develops of all sections on the **[p. 113]** body of the arthropods latest, even later than the postabdomen. Only the Arthrostraca are excepted in the latter respect. In them the development of the body parts goes uniformly from front to back. In many cases the abdomen is still completely undeveloped even when the embryos have already left the egg envelopes. So in the Decapoda (with the exception of the Astacina), Entomostraca (with the exception of the Daphniidae) and Myriapoda, in which the joints of the abdomen are formed only later, in lawful sequence one after another (1). In other cases, in the Isopoda, at the hatching of the embryos merely the hind joint of the abdomen is lacking (as in many Entomostraca constantly). The small group of the Bopyridae belonging to the Isopoda possesses at first even only three abdominal limbs. Later merely a fourth is added.

On the occurrence and the development of the postabdomen, the last section on the body of the arthropods, the needful has already been stated above. Everywhere it arises already very early, with the exception of the Arthrostraca already at a time when the abdominal part is not even laid down. Its full formation it receives only later. Sometimes it even remains quite rudimentary for life, as especially in the Laemodipoda (and Pycnogonida?). Without articulation is the postabdomen also in the Xiphosura, where it appears in the shape of a long, straight spine. Otherwise, however, one almost always finds a division into segments, whose number to be sure changes considerably and can rise from very few (in many Entomostraca) **[p. 114]** to very many (e.g. in the Cirripedia). After the mode of development of the postabdomen one should almost suppose that here the addition of segments happened not, as usual, at the hind, but at the front end, that therefore the segments adjoining the abdomen are the youngest. Direct observations, however, do not yet exist for this. Only so much may well be certain, that the last segment of the postabdomen forms very early and in the time of its arising precedes the remaining rings — indisputably for the same reason which, in the absence of the hind body, makes the like necessary on the abdomen for the last abdominal segment. In the development of the limbs the postabdomen, moreover, constantly remains behind the preceding sections of the body. Very often they are not laid down at all, as in the scorpions and a great number of Entomostraca. In other Entomostraca they remain constantly only very rudimentary. In the long-tailed Decapoda their development is relatively still greatest, although here too much simpler than on abdomen or thorax. Nowhere in the developed state do the limbs of the postabdomen serve for locomotion, at least nowhere exclusively. Only in the larvae of Bopyrus and Phryxus do they appear at first (after the interesting discovery of Rathke) as swimming organs, and indeed they alone (1), since the limbs of thorax and abdomen receive their permanent shape already early. Later they become gills, whose structure and function they also otherwise so very often possess.

So much of the morphological relations of the arthropods, of the arrangement of the sections and appendages of the body in general. Already from the multitude of the elements composing the body, these bearers of the most **[p. 115]** various form-building processes, the wealth of shapes may be inferred which owe their origin to the manifold combinations of these individual factors. No other division of the animal kingdom offers so infinite a fullness of the most different forms.

Variously has systematics attempted to bring order and connection into this mass. Linné divided his class of the insects according to the presence and constitution of the wings into a series of orders, which in part still exist today in their original extent and really also, as especially the later investigations on the arrangement of the feeding instruments etc. have proved, appear with few exceptions as very natural groups. Least, however, does this pronouncement hold of the last order of Linné, of the Aptera, which are distinguished before the rest by the absence of wings and contain the most different forms. Wingless six-footed insects stand here united with Crustacea, Arachnida and Myriapoda in an irrational heap. Cuvier recognised very well the insufficiency of such a classification. He separated the Crustacea and Arachnida (after the precedent of Latreille) from the insects and formed out of them two particular classes standing equal to the insects. The Myriapoda remained with the latter, at first also the Arachnida (with a part of the Crustacea, the Isopoda, which in their body form are apparently so closely related to the Myriapoda). The principle of division he took partly from the anatomical structure of the respiratory and vascular system, partly also from the arrangement of the head and the appendages of the body. Yet another division of the arthropods is by Savigny, who divided them according to the number of the locomotor instruments into Hexapoda and Apiropoda and emphasised as a common distinguishing character of the latter group the number of the extremities (always exceeding the number six). According to this there belong to it both the Crustacea and the Arachnida and Myriapoda. **[p. 116]** However, the union of such different forms has certainly rightly never found a general applause, although Oken, Goldfuss, Nitzsch and recently again Streubel have recommended and accepted it as natural. Almost everywhere, by contrast, one has followed the Cuvierian classification, yet mostly with some modifications, which preferably concern the group of the Myriapoda. While Lamarck (at first), Wiegmann and others reckon it with Cuvier as a particular order to the class of the insects, Schweigger, Burmeister, Stein and others to the Arachnida, Erichson to the Crustacea, finally Leach, Latreille, Grant, Rymer Jones etc. form out of it a class of its own standing equal to the Crustacea, Arachnida and Hexapoda.

Erichson has the great merit of having first recognised the Cuvierian classes belonging to the arthropods in their inner connection and distinguished them from one another in a very ingenious way according to definite morphological characters. In the six-footed insects, so he has proved, head, thorax and abdomen are always distinctly separated from one another, and the two former sections each furnished with three pairs of extremities, the head with the feeding instruments, the thorax with the legs. As here, so also in the Arachnida the abdomen is without appendages. Head and thorax, however, are fused with one another, in which at the same time the hind pair of the feeding instruments has been transformed into the front pair of legs. Hence instead of three pairs of locomotor organs four of them. In the Crustacea, by contrast, the occurrence of the limbs is not merely restricted to the fore body. The abdomen too bears jointed appendages, and these especially it is which are here developed as locomotor instruments. The thoracic legs, by contrast, have passed as accessory mouth parts to the head, either all — and in this case head and thorax are completely fused — or only in part. So at least in the higher crabs, the **[p. 117]** Malacostraca, while in the Entomostraca such a transformation has not set in. In general, so Erichson (1) thinks, one could separate these arthropods from the Crustacea almost with the same right as the Arachnida from the insects. The characters of the Entomostraca are properly those of the remaining three classes taken together: the mouth, namely, contains exactly three jaw pairs, as in the insects (at least one can regard this number as the normal number), the head is fused with the thorax, as in the Arachnida, the hind body has organs answering to the legs, as in the Crustacea. To this comes still a particular character common to the Entomostraca in this, that the first pair of feet of the thorax (mostly also the second) is situated before the mouth opening.

Such is according to the view of Erichson the morphological relation of the three Cuvierian classes of the arthropods. In essentials I must fully agree with Erichson, but nevertheless cannot approve that he partly rejects the anatomical characters as principle of division, partly also emphasises unduly single moments which in my opinion are not so very weighty. To these I reckon above all the circumstance that he sets up as an exclusive mark of the Crustacea the presence of the legs on the abdomen and accordingly then associates the Myriapoda with the Crustacea, although they in their whole anatomical structure agree much more with the insects and spiders and also, as we shall see at once, depart in the arrangement of the feeding instruments from the crabs. To be sure, the presence of abdominal legs is a peculiarity of the crustacean type, yet it needs nevertheless just as little to be restricted to the group of these animals as e.g. the occurrence of a postabdomen, which we likewise find quite generally in the **[p. 118]** Crustacea. If we wished to assume this latter relation as decisive for the class of the Crustacea — and we certainly can with the same right with which Erichson holds the presence of the abdominal legs for such — we should also have to reckon the scorpions to the crabs.

We may never forget, however, as already mentioned above, that certain morphological relations which have perhaps a typical significance for one or another group are sometimes found again in other groups too, even if perhaps without being so directly demanded by the plan of organisation. Under such circumstances now we see not a single compelling reason to range the Myriapoda among the crabs, although we gladly grant that they can just as little be connected with the Arachnida or the Hexapoda and differ from both, as shall yet be further discussed, by definite morphological characters. We regard them as a separate group standing equal to the remaining classes.

Thus we should have in the division of the arthropods four, or, if one, as seems to me quite consistent, also separates the Entomostraca from the remaining Crustacea, five individual great groups sharply separated from one another, which after the usual mode of view one could regard as so many classes. If, however, we look more closely at the relation in which they stand to one another, we shall soon notice that they are by no means quite of equal value, that rather some are more closely related among themselves than with the others. The six-footed insects (among which, as Nitzsch (1) has so acutely shown, the Aptera hexapoda, which still today are at times regarded, as e.g. by Walckenaer (2), as a particular group — standing together with the spiders and Myriapoda in the **[p. 119]** class of the Aptera —, can be very well distributed), the Arachnida and Myriapoda present among themselves a greater agreement than the higher Crustacea (Malacostraca) and Entomostraca, which in turn on their side hang together much more intimately. In the former the thoracic legs always keep their significance as walking instruments (also in the Myriapoda) and their position behind the head. The respiratory instruments are here everywhere tracheae, which (with the exception of Pteronarcys (1), a neuropteran) in the developed state constantly open outward and in manifold form now form merely leaf-like sacs (2), now also tube-shaped, branched or unbranched canals, in which the atmospheric air is contained. That such organs, as has been asserted, are lacking in some smaller species belonging here, especially in mites and wingless insects, still very much needs confirmation and may until then well be rightly doubted.

Otherwise, by contrast, is the relation in the Entomostraca and Malacostraca. Never are tracheae found in them (3). They breathe by help of particular gills, or, where these are lacking, by help of the appendages of the postabdomen expressly reshaped for it. Sometimes too the outer coverings quite simply take the place of the respiratory organs. At the same time the relation of the thoracic legs is a very divergent one. Never do they persist in their lawful **[p. 120]** number as locomotor instruments. They are rather, when they keep their original position, either all or in part transformed into accessory feeding instruments. As organs of movement function preferably the appendages of the abdomen, which are constantly present, although here and there they are in part lost again in the course of the metamorphosis. Beside this there is also found quite constantly in the two groups concerned a postabdomen (1), as a rule likewise furnished with foot-like appendages.

Under such relations now, I believe, one divides the division of the arthropods best and most naturally into the two classes of the *Crustacea* and *Insecta*, the same which Duméril, as at first Cuvier, set up. To the former belong as main orders the *Entomostraca* (2) Latr. (Neusticopoda Car.) and *Malacostraca* Leach, to the latter the *Myriapoda* Latr., *Arachnida* Leach (Acera Latr.) and *Hexapoda* Sav. On the morphological characterisation of these groups I have only little to add. It results quite simply from a putting together of the more general relations mentioned above. Hardly does it seem to me necessary to mention here once more what the relation of the Entomostraca to the Malacostraca, of the Arachnida (3) to the Hexapoda is, that **[p. 121]** the Malacostraca are distinguished by the relatively mighty development of the fore-head (1), the Arachnida by the complete absence of this section, etc. Only on the group of the Myriapoda I must still add something, the more so as Savigny (2) and after him Erichson (3) have here, in my opinion, not quite correctly conceived the relation of the head to the thorax and have thereby also been misled into false views on the position of these animals. According to the interpretation of these two zoologists, namely, the so-called lower lip following next behind the mandibles consists of the two pairs of maxillae, which are said to stand in one row beside one another and to be fused among themselves. To such an interpretation, however, I cannot assent. I see in it rather only a single pair of maxillae (4), and indeed **[p. 122]** the front one, which quite as in the Hexapoda the second pair of maxillae hangs together in the median line into a common mass. The inner lobes are in my opinion (1) the coxae, the outer the palps. The following pair of feet then belongs not to the thorax, but is the lower pair of maxillae, which here then — quite just as in the Arachnida, only not everywhere so completely, since often the claw is still lacking — is transformed into a pair of locomotor organs agreeing essentially with the remaining legs. That this my interpretation is correct results especially convincingly in the Chilognatha, partly from the anatomical arrangement, partly also from the development. When the latter leave the egg envelopes, they (Julus) are, as De Geer already knew, but as has recently been confirmed by the very exact investigations of Newport (2), furnished with three pairs of feet, which are spread over the four front segments in such a way that the third lacks all appendages. On this segment, however, are situated the outlet openings of the genitalia, which after the discovery of v. Siebold (3) are covered over with a small scale in which the analogues of the extremities cannot be mistaken. If we reckon these to those three pairs of legs, we get a number of locomotor instruments quite agreeing with that of the Arachnida and also indisputably of the same morphological significance, as emerges both from the whole development and from this, that the segments of the body growing afterward (because they very early grew together each two with one another) **[p. 123]** are distinguished by the presence of two pairs of feet each and so prove their inner connection. They are the segments of the abdomen. Of the appendages of the preceding body rings, however, the front one lies under the lower lip mentioned above; it is therefore, since the number of thoracic segments in the insects never amounts to more than three, the metamorphosed third jaw pair, for which I have also given it out before. In the Chilopoda the relations of the appendages on head and thorax are quite similar (1). Here too the third jaw pair transforms itself into the front pair of feet, yet it hardly ever functions as such, since it is covered from below by the peculiarly metamorphosed front (not middle, as Erichson wishes) pair of legs of the thorax, as by a strong lip. The metamorphosis of these appendages into a powerful grasping apparatus is, moreover, only of small morphological dignity. I should not like to compare it with the transformation of the thoracic appendages into accessory feeding instruments, such as occurs in the Crustacea. It is rather, as it seems to me, quite simply conditioned by the outlet place, lying in these feet, of the poison gland occurring in the Chilopoda, and may very fittingly be compared with the transformation of the last postabdominal joint in the scorpions, only that here not the paired appendages but directly the corresponding segment is the seat of the transformation concerned.

**Mollusca.**

Cuvier was the first who recognised the forms belonging to this great division of the animal kingdom in their connection and gathered them under the above name. **[p. 124]** Linné, and with him his contemporaries and successors, had distributed them among the various orders of the worms. The shelled molluscs formed (with the other shell-bearing animals) the order of the Testacea, while the naked creatures belonging here, united with a great part of the worms, with the echinoderms and caseless polyps, composed the Linnaean order of the Mollusca. Single apparently very divergent forms had yet another position. Teredo was reckoned by Linné to the worms, the compound ascidians to the much-embracing gen. Alcyonium. The former animal stood in the order of the Intestina, the latter among the zoophytes. — However, the Cuvierian division of the molluscs too has undergone some change, since the Cirripedia, which in Cuvier form beside the Cephalopoda, Pteropoda, Gasteropoda, Acephala and Brachiopoda a sixth class of their own of the molluscs, have been excluded from it and recognised as crustaceans. The group of the tunicates too, whose closer knowledge we owe especially to the fine investigations of Savigny (1), one has several times wished to remove from the division of the molluscs. Lamarck raised them to a particular class, which he set between the Radiata and white-blooded worms (Entozoa Cuv.). Likewise Latreille connected them with the Holothurida (2) and Echinoderma into the division of his Actinozoa. To be sure, I believe, the whole organisation of the tunicates gives us ground enough to regard them as a particular group, as has also already been done by Goldfuss, Grant, Burmeister and others, and to separate them from the Acephala, to which they belong in Cuvier (as Acéphales sans coquilles in opposition to the Acéphales testacés, the Conchifères Lam.). **[p. 125]** That however they are to be separated wholly from the molluscs seems to me doubtful, although one cannot mistake that they are very strikingly distinguished before the remaining groups of these animals in several respects. If, moreover, one really wishes to remove them from the molluscs, they must, in my opinion, form a division of their own in the animal series, in which then perhaps the Bryozoa too are to be incorporated. Most naturally such would then find its place between the echinoderms and worms. As class or order the tunicates can be ranged nowhere else than with the molluscs.

If now, however, we set the tunicates (Perigymna Burmstr.) aside for the time being, there remains to us in the division of the molluscs (Palliata Nitzsch) still a series of animal forms which are distinguished before all the rest by a common, very definite architectural plan. From the typical arrangement of the arthropods or worms, as from that of the echinoderms or Coelenterata, it is very strikingly different. Mistaken therefore seems it to me to connect the molluscs with one or another of these circles, as has been attempted several times. The Carusian division of the Corpozoa (Oken's skin-animals), which besides the molluscs also comprises the arthropods and worms, like the Burmeisterian division of the Gastrozoa (Cormozoa Streub.), which (identical with the Ganglioneura Rud.) unites the molluscs with the Radiata, are no natural groups, are not held together by an inner kinship, by a common and agreeing building style.

Instead of the radial mode of formation we meet quite unmistakably (despite manifold disturbances) a laterally symmetrical type, instead of the stretched form of the body a short compact body, without repetition of segments and paired lateral appendages. Other morphological elements it is which, quite peculiar to the type of the molluscs, condition the shaping and **[p. 126]** serve the formative forces as chief point of attack.

Nowhere does the soft outer covering envelop the body in the form of a simple sac or tube. On the back it everywhere forms a so-called mantle, a shield-shaped thickening or duplicature, whose form and arrangement change manifoldly. Likewise the ventral surface is furnished with an appendage of its own of muscular texture, with the so-called foot, while finally in the more highly developed forms at the same time the front end of the body makes itself known as a particular section, as head, which is distinguished before the trunk lying behind it in several respects. The formation of this part is very remarkable and divergent from the mode of development of the head in the worms, arthropods and vertebrates, as we shall see further below.

Where a head is present, it bears the entrance into the digestive canal, which otherwise lies quite simply at the front end of the body. In it the central parts of the nervous system also concentrate. In the headless molluscs these are scattered in the body at various places, at the sides of the gullet, in the base of the foot and between the gills. They appear as paired ganglia, connected among themselves by commissures into a common system. Most constant in position and occurrence are the pharyngeal ganglia. They lie on the front section of the digestive canal, on the oesophagus, and indeed symmetrically at its sides. Above the gullet they are connected by a transverse commissure. Not seldom they also fuse in the median line of the back. A proper pharyngeal ring, such as occurs in the more highly developed forms of this division, is not originally founded in the typical plan of organisation of the molluscs (1), but rather called forth by a mere modification **[p. 127]** in the development of this system. The lower oesophageal commissure, namely, is found (as one can see very distinctly in the arrangement of the nervous system in the Heteropoda (1)) only when the pedal ganglia — which, as said, stand in connection among themselves and with the lateral pharyngeal ganglia — move up forward to beneath the oesophagus. Where these, as in the Conchifera, have another position or are wholly lacking (in the Tunicata), a complete pharyngeal ring never comes about. Sometimes, however, pedal and pharyngeal ganglia are also so much approached to one another that they hang together. If now in such cases the pedal ganglia beneath the oesophagus are fused according to the law of the median symphysis, then (in the Gymnobranchiata) all these ganglia together form a single mass, which embraces the alimentary canal only with an upper commissure lacking any swelling (2).

The digestive instruments of the molluscs show a very mighty development. Likewise the sexual organs, which moreover, like those, are very often (especially in the Gasteropoda) quite strikingly disturbed in their symmetrical development. Only rarely, quite generally only in the Conchifera, is the anus (3) placed at the hind body end in the middle line, never perhaps the genital opening, **[p. 128]** which rather always (also in Dentalium?) opens laterally on the body, usually (with the exception of the Conchifera) asymmetrically on one side only. The more special arrangement of these parts, often very characteristic for a single group, offers a great diversity (1) and cannot be considered more closely here. Only so much let still be mentioned, that the intestine constantly exceeds the body in length, even if often only a little, that it, in agreement with the short, compact form of the body, forms a sac-shaped, simple or also compound stomach, and is everywhere furnished with a particular bile-preparing apparatus of considerable size, with a liver.

The circulation of the molluscs goes on, after the important discovery of Milne Edwards, nowhere in a completely closed vascular system, as in the echinoderms and annelids. A lesser or greater stretch of it is constantly lacunar. So in particular the body cavity forms a wide venous blood reservoir. In general it is preferably the venous system which appears deficient. In many cases, however, even all vessels vanish, with the exception of the heart, as is known to be likewise the case in the Hexapoda, yet this never forms, as here and otherwise in almost all arthropods, a tube-like canal divided into a series of chambers lying one behind another, but always (with the exception of the Tunicata) a short muscular sac (2), a ventricle (3) with single or double auricle.

**[p. 129]** The respiratory organs of the molluscs are almost everywhere particular appendages of the outer coverings expressly developed for this purpose, which now lie free, now however also are embedded and hidden in cavernous spaces. Not all molluscs, however, breathe by means of such gills. A number of them is directed by residence on land to direct air-breathing. For such a destination serve the so-called lung sacs (in the Pulmonata), which moreover, as can be proved, do not stand completely isolated by the plan of their formation, but morphogenetically join directly the organs destined for the mediation of indirect air-breathing (so-called water respiration). Still other molluscs likewise lack particular respiratory structures. They then breathe either with the whole skin surface of the body (as among the Gymnobranchiata the Phlebenterata Quatref. — Apneusta Köllik. —) or through the mediation of the lamellae of the mantle (as the Brachiopoda).

For the zoological consideration of the molluscs the arrangement of the gills is of the greatest interest, especially since it has been set up by Cuvier as the most excellent character of the larger and smaller groups created by him. Here, however, we must above all put to ourselves the question whether the so-called gills really have in every case the same morphological significance, whether under them perhaps, despite the agreeing function, manifold structures of different kinds have not been gathered? We see, after all, in other divisions too, in the gill-worms and Crustacea, a like behaviour. In them too it is appendages of very different kind which function as gills.

As to the grouping of the appendages paralleled with one another under the common name of gills on the body of the molluscs, such follows in general quite uniformly the laws of lateral **[p. 130]** symmetry. As however we have otherwise already seen in the present division several disturbances of such an arrangement, so especially here. In many Gasteropoda the gills are found only on one side of the body, the right or the left. The corresponding structures of the other side are then either completely lacking (Aplysia, Pleurobranchus, Doridium etc.), or are likewise moved over to the opposite side (e.g. in Haliotis, Cassis, Buccinum, Murex), so that here then the number of gills is doubled (1).

All these differences, however, cannot yet entitle us to a judgement on the nature and the morphological relation of the gills. Far more important is it in this respect that in the relation of the structures concerned to the individual sections of the body we strike on another quite definite difference. The so-called Gymnobranchiata — the same to which the gill-less Phlebenterata mentioned above belong — are those which here distinguish themselves before all other molluscs. The structures which one designates in them as gills are quite evidently mere processes and appendages of the mantle, from whose outer surface they rise. In all other cases, by contrast, the gills appear not as appendages of the mantle, but as independent productions of the outer body envelope, which are fastened in the furrow-shaped depression in the circuit of the mantle and are more or less far covered and overtopped by its free edge. Very distinct is this arrangement where the gills still lie free to the light of day, in the Cyclobranchiata, Hypobranchiata and Heteropoda, as also in the Lamellibranchiata. In other cases that furrow at the insertion place of the gills deepens into a formal **[p. 131]** cavity completely covered over by the mantle, which then contains the gills in its interior and leads outward beneath the mantle edge by a slit-shaped opening. So in the Ctenobranchiata and many Pteropoda, so also in the Cephalopoda (1). In position and extent, moreover, both the branchial cavity and the respiratory opening show manifold differences. Of greatest roominess, surrounding almost the whole visceral sac, is it in the Cephalopoda and some shelled Pteropoda (e.g. Hyalaea), very narrow by contrast e.g. in Haliotis. In the order of the Ctenobranchiata the upper lip of the respiratory opening, formed by the mantle edge, very often lengthens into a canal-shaped, more or less long process, into the so-called respiratory tube. The Pomatobranchiata show essentially a quite analogous arrangement of the branchial cavity, only in them the respiratory opening does not coincide with the mantle furrow. It arises rather by this, that the branchial cavity, which stretches along beneath the mantle, is slit lengthwise in greater or lesser extent. In Bullaea, where this slit is only short, its outer end still flows together with the mantle furrow. Aplysia already lacks this connection between respiratory opening and mantle furrow. Both are completely separated from one another. Quite evidently the respiratory opening has here lengthened more inward, into the mantle. The whole branchial cavity is open and is covered and closed solely by the lateral lips of the respiratory opening belonging to the mantle, which overlap one another.

What we can already gather from the agreement just mentioned in **[p. 132]** position and arrangement of the gills in the greatest majority of the molluscs, in opposition to the behaviour of the parts of the same name in the Gymnobranchiata, namely that the gills of the latter are other, morphologically differing structures, becomes certainty when we see how in the former the manifold variations in the shaping of the respiratory organs can be derived from a common primitive form, from the form of a feather with middle shaft and two-rowed vane. In vain, however, is the attempt to draw the gills of the Gymnobranchiata into the range of this scheme. They are of a ruleless diversity, like all the numerous accessory formations on the animal body which lack a planned, typical foundation. In the form of simple leaves or cylinders, of branched trees etc., they usually stand in considerable number and in varying grouping spread over the surface of the mantle.

Otherwise, however, is it with the gills of the remaining molluscs. Without prejudice to the disturbances of a lateral symmetry already mentioned above, there is found here in the norm on each side of the body only a single gill. Nautilus alone possesses two of them. That form, moreover, which I should like to regard as the ground form is preserved in complete integrity only rarely. So e.g. in Pleurobranchus, Valvata and others. Most often it is modified by this, that the shaft of the feather is fused in its whole length with the outer coverings. Then the gills appear on each side in doubly comb-shaped form, as two longitudinal rows of lamellae lying beside one another (e.g. Diphyllidia, Turbo etc.), still oftener as simply comb-shaped formations, when namely one row of these lamellae has vanished (1). Where the gills in such cases have **[p. 133]** preserved the symmetry of their arrangement on both sides, they sometimes (as in Hyalea, in Patella etc.) flow together in an arc at their end. In other cases the original feather-shaped form of the gills also becomes more complicated by this, that the lamellae of the vane furnish themselves in turn with secondary lamellae of second etc. order. So especially in the Cephalopoda, but also already in Aplysia etc. Interesting, moreover, it is that the two modifying moments just adduced can also occur combined. So in Chiton, where the gill leaves, still quite simple in Patella, have been transformed by the development of lateral secondary lamellae into the form of single small feathers or feathered pyramids. That likewise from a mere variation of that ground form of the gills the apparently so very divergent shape of these organs in the Conchifera derives is proved especially distinctly by the gen. Solemya (1), in which the primary feather form with shaft and two-rowed vane is still completely preserved. Very simply the usual arrangement of the gills in the Conchifera may be derived from this. The individual cylindrical lamellae or rays of the vane, namely, grow very considerably and then appear on each side either as two longitudinal rows standing beside one another of thin and free threads (e.g. Pectunculus, Arca) or still more often, when namely the threads of each row connect among themselves, as two quite considerably developed membranous leaves, which, answering to the two lateral rows of the vane, lie on each side in the mantle fold and take the body between them. According now as the shaft from which the leaves go out hangs together more or less far lengthwise with the body, the inner edge of the gills is also free in varying extent. In some **[p. 134]** cases, e.g. in Ostrea, these are fastened in whole length (like the gill leaves of many Gasteropoda) on the body. Not seldom too the gill lamellae of both sides are fused in the median line at their hind end, where they project beyond the body (Unio, Mactra etc.). In Teredo this union extends even over the whole length of the gills; a relation which, moreover, becomes possible only by this, that these take their beginning only behind the visceral sac.

Through a peculiar metamorphosis of the mantle, which needs a closer discussion further below, the gills in the Conchifera are at times enclosed in a respiratory cavity which, despite the different mode of origin, offers many analogies with the branchial cavity of the Gasteropoda and Cephalopoda. The mantle, namely, which is distinguished by the mighty lamellar development of its lateral edge lobes, not seldom fuses in the median line of the belly and then, when this growing together has taken place as completely as possible, forms an outer sac-shaped envelope round the body, which however mostly does not lie directly on the latter (only in Teredo (1), where the branchial cavity begins only behind the proper body, is this the case), but stands off from it and so then forms a cavity of its own completely surrounding the body up to the back. This latter cavity now is the branchial cavity, in which the respiratory organs lie. Backward it remains, moreover, constantly open a stretch in the median line.

The two lateral gill lamellae, which in the manner mentioned proceed from a transformation of the vane on the shaft of a feather-shaped gill, can also on their side in turn undergo manifold changes of form. Of these I will here only emphasise that sometimes (in some Tellina species) on each side the outer lamella is lacking, and **[p. 135]** then the number of gill leaves is single (1), as in the simply combed gills of many Ctenobranchiata.

Just as single is the number of the lateral gill lamellae in the Tunicata. However, these appear here in a wholly different arrangement, so that at first sight a reduction of the parts concerned to the gills of the Acephala is hardly possible.

In the ascidians they present a simple sac, which lies in the fore part of the body. As the mantle lobes of the Conchifera for the formation of the branchial cavity, quite so the gill lamellae here too seem to be fused among themselves in the median line of the body. That they present a divergent position is little essential. As the gills in Teredo could recede from the side parts of the body to its hind end, quite as possible is the opposite change of position in the ascidians. Dependent on this position and this arrangement of the gills in the last-mentioned animals it is, moreover, that the intestinal canal with its front section does not open directly outward, but inserts itself into the hind part of the branchial sac. The respiratory apparatus has pushed itself in between mouth opening and oesophagus (2).

Quite the same position has the branchial apparatus in the salps. In these, however, the relation becomes different by this, that the gills do not, as in the ascidians, form a **[p. 136]** closed sac. Only in the median line of the back are the two gill leaves fused in their whole length, never however at the same time in the median line of the belly. They are only little developed in breadth and form both together (1) only a single narrow body of band-like shape, whose lateral edges are at most somewhat rolled up tube-like. Explicable it becomes under such relations that the oesophagus possesses its own opening, and indeed before the branchial apparatus. However, this too does not here coincide directly with the outer mouth (or inlet) opening, because the so-called respiratory cavity, in which the gill is stretched, presents a very excessive development and takes up the whole considerable space between the two outer openings of the body. Quite similar, only less considerable, is the respiratory cavity already in the ascidians. The lesser roominess in the latter is brought about by the different position of the mouth and cloacal opening. Both lie, namely, pretty close beside one another, while in the salps they stand diametrically opposite one another, yet not so that they were found above and below on the body, but front and back (on belly and back). Quite evidently the longitudinal diameter of the salps answers to the line drawn in the ascidians through the two body openings, which crosses with the perpendicular diameter through brain and visceral sac. The nuclear surface (2) of the salps is morphologically to be set equal to the lower, fixed end of the ascidians, the brain surface to the upper. Completely agreeing with such a view is the position of the branchial apparatus, which becomes paradoxical and inexplicable as soon as one **[p. 137]** [takes the nuclear surface for the dorsal]. In the ascidians the branchial sac extends from above downward, from the brain surface to the nuclear surface. The same position is found also in the salps. Here too the one insertion point of the band-shaped branchial apparatus is on the brain surface, the other on the nuclear surface. The gill is stretched obliquely from above downward in the respiratory cavity.

Having now followed the individual modifications which the gills of the molluscs present in arrangement, form and position in the various groups, there still remains to us the consideration of the so-called lung sacs, which are found in the Pulmonata and function with their respiratory vascular network as respiratory instruments. If we ask after the morphological significance of these parts, if we compare them with the respiratory apparatus of the related molluscs, the answer must, in my judgement, turn out that they are to be paralleled with the branchial cavities of the other Gasteropoda. From these they differ only by this, that in the interior no particular respiratory organs are enclosed (1). Otherwise they agree with them in every respect (2). What at first sight might perhaps contend against the assumption of such a morphological identity is only the circumstance that in some, though rare, cases a lung sac occurs at the same time beside a branchial apparatus. In Onchidium, where Ehrenberg first discovered such a behaviour, it can still be explained most easily. The gills of this animal are, as in the Gymnobranchiata, mere outgrowths or prolongations of the **[p. 138]** mantle, beside which a particular lung cavity can after all still form. Otherwise, however, is it in the Ampullariae, which (1) besides the latter still possess a complete branchial cavity with gills. In these, however, according to the anatomical relation, the lung sac appears as an appendage of the branchial cavity, as a side cavity, arisen by an evagination from the latter. Far, then, from seeing in this union of lung sac and branchial cavity in the same animal a ground for the morphological difference between the two, I should like rather, under the present relations, to take from it a proof for the correctness of the opposite view.

From all these manifoldly changing relations one may recognise the importance of the branchial apparatus for the architectonics of the molluscan body. Nevertheless a closer consideration very soon shows that that significance is more a subordinate one, that other factors appear as the chief bearers of the characteristic structure and by their capacity for modification determine the limits within which the difference of shape formation moves.

For the morphological conception of body structure in the molluscs only little has hitherto been done. An attempt at the reduction of all those manifold shapes is still lacking today, although our knowledge of the outer form and the development of the molluscs might after all already make such a presentation possible. The simplest and most regular relations in the typical division of the molluscs are offered us by the naked Gasteropoda. They form the starting point of our investigations. The shape of the body here recalls the Trematoda among the worms or those peculiar creeping holothurians like Psolus, Cuvieria etc. The lower surface of the body is flattened and forms the foot. It appears as a strongly muscular, sharp-edged **[p. 139]** disc, which reaches from the front body end to the hind body tip and bears the egg-shaped or cylindrical trunk with the visceral mass. The front section of this trunk, the head, is a small globular structure, which bounds itself off behind by a shallow ring-shaped constriction and at the lower end, close before the foot, is furnished with the slit-shaped, longitudinally set mouth opening. The lateral boundaries of the mouth are formed by two pad-shaped prominences, the lips, which very often (in the Gymnobranchiata) draw themselves out into a small lappet-shaped process, into the labial feeler. Above the mouth, on the crown, stand the antennae, two (1) symmetrical processes of cylindrical shape. The dorsal surface of the body is covered by the mantle, by a shield-shaped duplicature of the outer coverings, which extends from the nape variously far backward, in Limax only about to the middle third of the trunk, in the Gymnobranchiata to its hind end. From the skin coverings of the trunk the mantle is parted by a ring-shaped furrow, by the so-called mantle furrow. Where mantle and foot are distinguished by size and development, this fold lies in the circumference of the body. The edges of foot and mantle in this case abut directly on one another, so that the proper simple skin coverings become visible only in the bottom of that fold. The whole trunk is then, in a manner as in the tortoises by the dorsal and ventral shield, so here enclosed by the **[p. 140]** mantle and the foot, between which in the middle line of the fore body only the head projects outward.

On the morphological relation and the significance of the parts mentioned developmental history gives us information. Where the formative processes of the embryo in the group of the Gasteropoda are, as it seems, simplest and could also be observed most exactly, in the Pulmonata e.g. in Limax (1), there arise on the yolk globe, after this has been enabled by the process of cleavage to the separating out of the first embryonic structures, at the beginning two pads lying beside or rather above one another, of which the lower, the ventral pad, gradually draws itself out into a quite considerable tongue-shaped appendage of the yolk bladder and metamorphoses into the foot (2). Even before the further development of this ventral pad has set in, there rise at its front boundary, where it lies against the lower surface of the yolk globe, the two labial pads, two lateral prominences, which in the Limacinae remain very far behind the foot in size. Before them, more turned toward the crown surface, later sprout the two antennal pairs (3), while the labial pads themselves gradually grow ever more toward one another in the median line of the belly. The dorsal pad lying above the foot **[p. 141]** gradually overarches the yolk globe toward the head and becomes the mantle. For a time yet the rest of the yolk globe, enclosed by the skin coverings of the embryo, projects like a hernial sac between head and dorsal shield. Only gradually is it pushed back inward into the body cavity by the further development of those coverings (1), where it is then used up for the formation of the viscera, especially of the liver.

The Gymnobranchiata, as the Pomatobranchiata etc., show in their development single divergences from the processes just depicted. However, such rest not perhaps on a real difference of the morphogenetic processes brought into application, but rather merely on a different relation in the development and formation of the shape-conditioning elements. Labial pads, foot and mantle are in them too, quite as in the Limacinae, the most important morphological factors, the chief bearers of development and form. That however these in their first shaping differ so strikingly from the corresponding parts of those Pulmonata could hardly be inferred from their later agreement. Only the manifold greater fluctuations in the formal appearance of these parts in the Gymnobranchiata etc. might perhaps already a priori let us suppose such a difference, if indeed we were entitled from the outset to the assumption that these differences resulted directly, according to the laws of arrested formation etc., from a cyclic series of embryonic developmental processes. Observation has now indeed proved the admissibility of such a supposition. We know that the finished form of the **[p. 142]** Gasteropoda concerned does not develop directly and by a straight path, but is the product of a metamorphosis.

On the ventral surface of the yolk globe enabled after fertilisation to the separating out of the embryo there form in the Gymnobranchiata too (1) the labial pads and the foot, placed quite so toward one another as is the case in Limax. In this however they differ from the corresponding parts of this latter gastropod, that not the foot is distinguished by growth and development while the labial pads remain only rudimentary (2), but conversely the latter attain a very mighty size, and the foot stands considerably behind them (3). The labial pads, namely, develop into two large wing-shaped lobes (into the so-called velum), on whose free, rounded edge a row of long and powerful cilia grows out. So long as these labial or head pads keep their initial size and development, the foot remains a short, tongue-shaped appendage on the ventral surface of the yolk, which meanwhile **[p. 143]** has furnished itself with a shell-bearing (1) mantle and thereby become the trunk. This latter forms the more considerable part of the body, the whole upper and hind mass of the body. It covers not merely, as in the Limacinae and the other Pulmonata, which develop after the same scheme, the dorsal side of the foot, but also projects backward beyond it and so helps at the same time to form the ventral surface of the body.

In the course of development, however, this relation changes. The delicate shell is lost in the naked snails. The lobes of the labial pads gradually vanish, and the foot increases in size. It grows backward and to the sides, and becomes a considerable muscular disc. In like proportion the trunk is gradually lifted up from the ventral surface toward the back. It ceases to take further part in the formation of that section and gets a position as in Limax etc. The whole ventral surface of the body is now formed by the foot, while the trunk covered by the mantle presents the dorsal surface of the body.

In quite analogous manner goes on the development and gradual formation of the body in the Ctenobranchiata and in general, as it seems, in most other Gasteropoda (also in the Cyclobranchiata?). These too are distinguished during the earlier embryonic states by the existence of two considerable head lobes (of the velum). In this, however, they differ from the naked snails, that in them partly the foot constantly has a relation to the trunk as in the Gymnobranchiata only in the earlier stages of development, partly also the shell is not lost, but remains and increases in firmness and size.

**[p. 144]** The mother soil of the shell is the mantle. As it at first arises by this, that the outer layer of the embryonic cells on the trunk runs through a peculiar, independent development, so it grows later by apposition of a lime-rich secretion furnished by the mantle. Where this apposition, as in most cases, does not happen uniformly at all places, but preferably on one side, there that peculiar, spirally wound form must gradually arise which the case of the Gasteropoda is known to present. Naturally such growth has the greatest influence on the formation of the trunk enclosed by the mantle in the interior of the shell. However, these relations have for our mode of consideration a lesser significance. Even in zoological systematics they certainly deserve far from that exclusive or preferential attention which one has so variously attributed to them.

As a law of pretty general validity it seems to emerge that the mantle on the body of the Gasteropoda is the more considerably developed and covers the greater part of the trunk, the more foot and labial pads recede in significance and formation. Already in this a definite opposition between the structures concerned is unmistakably pronounced. On the one side stands the trunk with its covering, on the other foot and labial pads. These latter parts form a system belonging together, up to a certain point independent, which stands to the trunk, at whose cost it increases in formation, according to its most general significance in the same relation as an appendage to its chief organ. Foot and labial pads form in their connection the *fore body* of the molluscs, the trunk with the mantle the *hind body*.

That foot and velum really belong together results partly from the anatomical relation in which they stand to one another (as we shall see further below, **[p. 145]** they even sometimes grow together), partly also from the manner of their development. They proceed from a common central point which lies at the front end of the ventral surface of the trunk. From here the vela grow outward and upward, while the foot lengthens backward (1).

With this mode of development it is in harmony that the foot everywhere, where it reaches only a lesser length, becomes rudimentary and is lacking in its hind part, never in its front. In the same measure, however, as the foot recedes forward, the ventral surface of the hind body becomes free and overarched by the mantle, as during the embryonic period it is also found in the naked-gilled. Very distinct is this relation e.g. in Bullaea. The mantle here covers, like a bell-shaped envelope, the whole hind half of the body, since from the tip of the hind body it extends forward on all sides, though on the dorsal surface further than on the belly. In general too the mantle lies otherwise almost everywhere far more at the hind body end than at the front. In Limax the relation is to be sure another, yet perhaps only because nowhere else is the formation of the hind body half so much called forth by the excessive development of the foot as here. Also by this Limax is distinguished, that in it not, as usual, the whole trunk is covered by the mantle, but only a relatively small section of it (which is also the case in Firola etc.).

To Bullaea join, through the arrangement of the mantle, Clio, Pneumodermon and other naked Pteropoda. In these, however, the mantle covers a still far greater **[p. 146]** part (1) of the body. The simple skin coverings of the trunk are nowhere visible any more. Mantle, foot and velum envelop the whole body. As a rule there remains, moreover, between mantle and foot still a part of that simple skin covering. So especially at the bottom of the ring-shaped furrow in the circuit of the mantle edge, in the so-called mantle furrow.

Already above it has been shown how the formation of the branchial cavity in the Gasteropoda rests on a mere further development of this furrow into a cavity which stretches along between the mantle and the visceral sac. It arises as the edge of the mantle (2) by its excessive growth overarches the original envelopes of the body without fusing with them into a common mass. In connection with the usual position of the mantle at the hind body end and its gradual growth toward the periphery, but especially forward, it seems to stand that the branchial cavity is mostly found beneath its front edge, on the nape.

Despite all differences in the development of the mantle, however, we must everywhere regard the dorsal surface of the trunk as the mother place of this structure. Never does it lie on the ventral side alone, but often on the back alone, though preferably, as said, turned toward the hind body end.

Otherwise, by contrast, is it with the foot. Not merely that it is constantly placed on the ventral surface (3); it also never **[p. 147]** extends up over the side parts of the body to the back, however considerable its size, however varied its arrangement may be. It cannot, moreover, lie in our purpose here to name all the numerous modifications in the form of this structure. Let the remark suffice that it can be now broad and disc-shaped, now narrow and cylindrical, now long, now short, that it is now fused in its whole length with the ventral surface of the hind body, now fastened only with its front base close behind the head (as e.g. in the Ctenobranchiata). — A particular consideration, moreover, the foot still needs in those cases where it is distinguished by a very unusual development and has become unfit for creeping. So in the Heteropoda. Here it is transformed by lateral compression into a keel-shaped fin, which fastens itself in the median line of the belly behind the head end. So at least in its front larger part. On the hind edge, by contrast, a small part of the foot has separated itself off by a transverse furrow and independently metamorphosed into a formal sucker. To some degree a similar arrangement the foot already possesses in Dentalium, where it forms a short, cylindrical or proboscis-shaped process projecting forward beneath the head.

Completely different from this form is the arrangement of the foot in the Pteropoda. Precisely the development toward the sides it is here which distinguishes it and transforms it into two lobe-like fins, whose morphological significance was first correctly recognised (1) by Blainville. The reshaping of the foot in the foregoing way begins, **[p. 148]** moreover, already in the Pomatobranchiata, which in general in their whole structure stand so near the Pteropoda that it is hard in many cases to fix the boundary between the two. Already in Bullaea we see how the hind half of the foot spreads out laterally and projects outward over the mantle in a narrow fin-shaped fold. In Gasteropteron this arrangement goes still further. The foot metamorphoses into a roundish, flat disc, which hangs together with the body only at the front end in the median line, where it bears the mouth opening, but otherwise is completely free and especially laterally overtops the trunk far. Quite similar appears the arrangement of the foot in Tiedemannia, as also in Cymbulia, only that in the latter the visceral sac is fastened in its whole length on the foot disc, and this therefore appears under the shape of two broad longitudinal fins, which hang together in the middle line of the belly among themselves and with the proper body. In other Pteropoda the middle part of the foot gradually dwindles from behind, so that preferably only the two lateral fins remain, between which then the body projects. Already in Bullaea such a relation is indicated. Still more striking does it become in Hyalaea, where the hind edge of the foot is cut out far in the middle line, while the lateral parts lie like a pair of wings beside the mouth opening. Always, however, even here a plate, though only a narrow one, can still be distinguished behind the mouth opening, which forms a middle commissure between the two side lobes and is bent forward at its hind free edge. Quite similar in Limacina, only here both the lateral wings and the ventral part of the foot have a divergent form. The same three parts (1) form the foot in Clio. The middle **[p. 149]** unpaired part, which here to be sure possesses a more independent development, Eschricht (1) has described in this animal as an isolated structure, as neck collar. By its position, however, as by its connection with the fins, it proves itself quite evidently as middle foot lobe.

The peculiar form and development of the lateral fins would, moreover, in all these cases perhaps still admit another interpretation. Perhaps — so one might suppose — they are not real parts of the foot, arisen by a lateral spreading of this structure, but the persisting head vela of the embryos, especially as they here and there, as especially in Limacina and Hyalaea, quite strikingly resemble such. Then the foot in the Pteropoda would be only very rudimentary and consist solely in the middle appendage between those two fins. That this in Hyalaea etc. hangs together with the fins might not contradict such a view. After all, such a connection, which by position and relation of the parts concerned already in itself becomes very easily possible, could be conditioned and called forth by the persistence of the vela.

A complete decision of this question will be able to be brought only by the direct path, by an observation of development. So long, however, as such is lacking, I believe I must declare myself unconditionally against the interpretation of the fins as head lobes. Partly the manifold transitions from the usual form of the foot to this extreme formation seem to me to speak against it, partly also the positional relations of the parts concerned in Clio. Here the interpretation of the fins as head lobes might hardly be possible, since they do not, as otherwise, take the mouth opening between them, but lie backward pretty far removed from it. Moreover **[p. 150]** I believe finally I must claim yet another structure of the Pteropoda and of some related Pomatobranchiata, different from the fins, as rudiment of the velum.

In Gasteropteron this appears as a free lappet-shaped process (velum capitis) of triangular shape, which lies above the mouth opening before the visceral sac (hind body) and with the side parts of its base forms the lips. I should not know how else one would wish to interpret this appendage. At most it could still have proceeded from the growing together of the tentacles, which otherwise are lacking to our animal (1), only the arrangement and especially the behaviour of it in other species, namely in Cymbulia, where besides quite distinctly two feelers still occur, might make such an assumption appear untenable. — That the structure concerned is unpaired makes in no respect great difficulties. Either it can only in the course of development have become unpaired from the fusion of two lateral parts, or also have existed from the beginning under this shape. We see, after all, in animals with symmetrical type quite often instead of two corresponding lateral elements only an unpaired middle one (2).

Evidently the same structure it is which in Bullaea and the other Akera by its growing together with the outer skin coverings forms the so-called nuchal plate (bouclier). Cuvier, as is known, derived the origin of this **[p. 151]** structure from a growing together of four tentacles. However, the four edge lobes of this plate occurring in Bulla ampulla seem to me the less fitting and sufficient for the due foundation of this interpretation, as the number four of the tentacles in the animals concerned would be against all analogy. Much more probable, by contrast, is to me the supposition just pronounced. A quite analogous nuchal plate, only smaller and less marked, I see also in Hyalaea. In Limacina, by contrast, I should like to hold the two lip-shaped folds, which run down from the crown on the side parts of the mouth opening to the front base of the middle tongue-shaped foot lobe, for corresponding formations. Again divergent is the arrangement of this part in Cymbulia. Most does it here still resemble the corresponding head lobe of Gasteropteron, yet it is partly grown together a greater stretch on the nape with the mantle, partly it also hangs together laterally with the front edges of the fins and so helps to complete the disc of the foot. Hereby it becomes possible that this extends forward beyond the mouth opening and encloses the latter. The boundary between head lobe and foot is, however, still very distinctly marked by a fold which draws from the corners of the mouth in an arc upward and outward. Close above the mouth opening on the surface of the head lobe stand two small awl-shaped prominences, the feelers. In Clio I can likewise hold the two cap-shaped folds of the head, which lie laterally between the fins and the mouth opening and enclose the head cones, only for the metamorphosed lobes of the velum. In form and arrangement they afford sufficient points of support for such an interpretation.

The metamorphoses of the head lobes in the other Gasteropoda Lovén (1) has made the object of a very careful **[p. 152]** investigation. In most cases, in the Ctenobranchiata (1), they seem to be completely lost in the course of development. Most often their rudiments are still found in the Gymnobranchiata, where they are known under the designation of the velum (whose parts e.g. in Eolidia form the front so-called tentacles). Most strikingly they appear in Thetys, in the shape of a mighty semicircular veil, which covers and surrounds the mouth and repeats in colossal form the unpaired head lobe of Gasteropteron.

Its most complete formation the typical structure of the Gasteropoda reaches in the Cephalopoda. All the individual morphological elements which in those enter into the formation of the body and condition its various shapings we find, though in several ways divergent arrangement and development, here too. The reduction of the parts concerned is, moreover, because of these divergences subject to many difficulties, although through Kölliker's masterly presentation of the developmental history of these animals an abundant material for such an undertaking lies before us. How little, however, the morphological conception of these so very interesting creatures has yet come to a conclusion emerges from this, that even Kölliker (2) could still remain in uncertainty which part in the Cephalopoda is to be interpreted as back, which as belly.

On an outward consideration one distinguishes on the body of the Cephalopoda chiefly two parts very sharply set off from one another, a front, relatively very considerable head and a trunk lying behind it. The latter is clothed by a bell-shaped, strongly vaulted mantle, which in every respect, even by its relation to the branchial cavity, as already **[p. 153]** mentioned above, answers to the structure of the same name of the Gasteropoda. Not in the same measure is such the case with the head. The so-called head of the Gasteropoda is properly hardly more than the front part of the trunk, distinguished by the appendages of the fore body found on it. At most Clio might in this respect make an exception among the Gasteropoda by the development of a formal head — which seems to be conditioned preferably by the persistence and the peculiar formation of the head lobes. In general this creature joins by its outer shaping more than any other snail the Cephalopoda. Even the arms of the latter find in the head cones quite evidently their morphological equivalents. Likewise the mantle shows a like mighty development. Only the lateral spreadings of the foot seem to be lacking in the Cephalopoda. The so-called fins of the latter, which have a similar structure, prove on closer investigation completely different. They are mere radiations or duplicatures of the mantle, which lack every typical significance and therefore can also very often, as in the Octopoda, be lacking. By contrast the so-called neck collar of Clio, which I have interpreted above as the middle lobe of the foot, recalls in its more general relations by position and even by shape so strikingly the funnel of the Cephalopoda that we from the outset gladly concede the possibility of a morphological agreement between the two.

Far greater and surer points of support and disclosures for the morphological interpretation of the cephalopod body (1) developmental history gives us.

**[p. 154]** What already in the earliest stages of development distinguishes the Cephalopoda from the Gasteropoda, at least from the species hitherto observed (whether really from all — e.g. from Clio — I should almost doubt), is the relation in which the embryos stand to the yolk. Never, namely, do these enclose the whole yolk globe; they rather constrict themselves off from it in the course of development, so that the rest forms a formal outer appendage (like an umbilical vesicle), which is lost only later. Morphologically, however, this difference is of small interest. Likewise the circumstance that the cleavage extends only over a small part of the yolk, over that part, namely, which is next destined for the formation of the embryo.

In this, however, these two groups of the molluscs agree with one another, that by no means the whole surface of the body arises at the same time. First forms in the Cephalopoda the mantle, at first a flat shield-shaped structure, as in Limax. It marks by the place of its origin the back or hind body. In the circumference of the shield arise the remaining morphological elements of the body, all paired, next to the mantle (if we leave the gills out of account) the lateral limbs of the funnel, outward from them the head lobes, on each side a front and a hind. All these structures are at first spread out surface-wise, so that the front head lobes take the lowest, the dorsal shield the highest place. If we compare in this state the embryo of a cephalopod, e.g. of Sepia, as Kölliker has figured it Tab. 1. Fig. XIX or XX (from above), Fig. XXV (from the side), with Cymbulia or Hyalaea, **[p. 155]** a very striking analogy in the form of the body cannot possibly escape us. The surface lying on the yolk, which consists of the two pairs of head lobes (1), resembles almost quite completely the peculiarly metamorphosed foot of these Gasteropoda. The front head lobes, which outwardly bear the eyes and in the median line of their front edge are furnished with the entrance into the digestive canal, with the mouth opening, answer in every respect to the mouth pads or velar halves, the head lobes lying behind them to the lateral fins of the foot. As in Cymbulia, both are here fused into a common disc, yet so that their boundary still remains distinct for a considerable time. This disc forms the fore body. In the furrow between mantle and it lie the lateral funnel limbs, which in the middle line of the body above the mouth opening are grown together among themselves and with the shield of the fore body, embrace ring-like the base of the hind body or back (of the mantle) and backward, where they take the anal opening between them, project beyond the hind head lobes. If now, as the arrangement quite distinctly proves, these latter answer to the lateral fins of the foot in Hyalaea, the funnel limbs can only still be analogous to the middle foot lobe, which in Clio forms the neck collar. Position and arrangement, as the connection with the fore body, are completely in accordance with this interpretation. That they are paired does not speak against it; we have in this behaviour only a new instance of that morphogenetic law according to which an unpaired middle element can be replaced by two corresponding lateral ones.

In this stage the embryo of the Cephalopoda is thus completely built after the type of the Gasteropoda. The divergences from it are hardly greater than they also otherwise occur in the various individual species of this group. **[p. 156]** Mantle, foot and velum are quite distinctly present and without all essential differences from the usual behaviour. The head lobes (velum and side parts of the foot) form the fore body, which takes up the ventral surface of the body. The hind body, covered by the mantle, lies above it on the dorsal surface. The presence of the mouth marks the front end, that of the anus the hind end of the body. On the morphological interpretation there can hardly be a doubt.

Gradually, however, the relation changes. Fore body and hind body grow very considerably, yet not lengthwise, but in the height diameter. The lateral lobes of the velum and foot, at first spread out surface-wise, transform themselves into the globular head, in whose lower pole the mouth then lies; the mantle, at first only a flat shield, becomes a high bell, whose lower edges by their excessive growth gradually cover over the lateral funnel limbs. These latter finally grow together with their hind ends above the anus into an unpaired cylindrical structure, which later alone projects from the mantle slit (the respiratory opening).

In the morphological conception of the cephalopod body, moreover, such transformations cannot impair the interpretation of the individual elements. What we in the developed state call the head in the Cephalopoda, this peculiar structure, arises then from a growing together and metamorphosis of the velum and the side parts of the foot. The funnel answers to the middle lobe of the foot in the Pteropoda and the bell-shaped mantle to the coverings of the hind body. The proper body end coincides with the anal opening, not with the end of the hind body, which morphologically is to be interpreted as the highest tip of the back. The so-called dorsal surface of the hind body results accordingly as the front ascending surface of the back, the so-called **[p. 157]** ventral surface as the hind descending surface. The head marks the lower body end.

The so-called longitudinal diameter of the Cephalopoda from the tip of the head to the end of the hind body is therefore not, as one usually assumes, congruent with the longitudinal diameter of the other animals with lateral type. It rather cuts this at a right angle; it is the height diameter. The proper longitudinal diameter, by contrast, runs in the median line of the body from the so-called back to the so-called belly, which moreover, as said, have this designation only falsely.

Under such circumstances we can then also see in the position of the heart, as of the genital apparatus in the Cephalopoda, no divergence from the usual arrangement in the Gasteropoda. They lie not, as one hitherto assumed, on the ventral surface of the body, but, as everywhere, on the back, only on its hind descending surface. Even the anus offers no different grouping. It is found, as in general in most creatures, at the end of the longitudinal diameter. The mouth opening was at first situated exactly opposite it. That it has left this position and moved further back in the median line of the fore body, as far as the middle (into the centre of the so-called head), finds its explanation in the peculiar reshaping of the front head lobes (of the velar halves). Already in Cymbulia we see through a similar fusion of the two velar lobes an analogous change of position. Already here the entrance into the intestinal canal has moved backward, though much less far than in the Cephalopoda.

The morphological relations of the Cephalopoda are on their side very suited to further our insight into the structure of the Gasteropoda. Above all they offer our assumption of the relation of velum and foot a weighty point of support. They show most distinctly how these belong to a common system, how they **[p. 158]** together form the fore body of the Gasteropoda, whose middle longitudinal line coincides with the longitudinal diameter of the body. The hind end of the foot it is, then, which at the same time determines the hind end of the body. — Almost everywhere, moreover, as in the Cephalopoda, so also in the Gasteropoda, the height diameter of the body is distinguished before the longitudinal diameter. The highest point of the back is the tip of the hind body (1), which in the case-bearing species lies hidden in the innermost whorls of the shell. In most cases such is easily evident. But where the longitudinal diameter of the fore body is distinguished by its shortness, it is less clear. Here (e.g. in Bullaea, Clio etc.) the tip of the hind body seems, namely, to be congruent with the end of the longitudinal diameter. In the same proportion, namely, in which the foot recedes forward, the hind body sinks, until finally its hind descending surface comes to lie with the fore body in a common plane, and so then its proper height diameter appears as a direct continuation of the longitudinal diameter on the fore body.

The position of the anus, as of the genital opening, is, moreover, in the Gasteropoda by no means so regularly at the end of the longitudinal diameter as in the Cephalopoda. It is found almost always further forward and thereby offers a divergence from the usual arrangement, as it is also not seldom perceived in other creatures. **[p. 159]** That it, moreover, could not under such relations persist in the median line of the ventral surface is quite explicable given the arrangement of the foot. The development of the latter had to push it from there toward the side surfaces of the body.

In our presentation of the development and the morphological relations of the Cephalopoda the arms have hitherto not been thought of. These structures are quite peculiar to the molluscs concerned. In the Gasteropoda they are completely lacking with the exception of Clio. And even in the latter they are only very rudimentary. The time of their formation in the Cephalopoda falls in that period in which the fore body is still spread out surface-wise. They arise quite independently in the periphery of the fore body on the surface of the yolk. At first they appear as oval pads, which only later stretch and then enter into connection with the front head lobes, with the parts of the velum (1), then.

The manifold differences in the development of the suckers (2) fastened on these arms I pass over. Only so much let still be mentioned, that for morphological reasons the interpretation defended by Valenciennes (3) against Owen of the numerous tentacles found on the head of Nautilus, according to which these are regarded not as arms but as structures analogous to those suckers, seems to me very acceptable. The proper arms have here vanished or are at least very rudimentary; no longer free cylindrical appendages, but mere fold-shaped lobes between the bases of the tentacles. The whole relation recalls in several respects those transformations of the **[p. 160]** gill feather in which the shaft has vanished while the leaves of the vane persist.

A new, very peculiar modification in the development of the molluscan type we meet in the Acephala (with exclusion of the Tunicata). Here the body has in general a cylindrical, more or less stretched (1) shape with the mouth opening at the one, with the anus at the other end. So at least in the Lamellibranchiata. As a rule the body is thereby compressed from the sides, though in varying degree. A proper head is lacking everywhere, indisputably because in the Acephala the elements of the fore body never present so independent a development as in the Cephalopoda and even only in the Gasteropoda. The foot is everywhere fastened with its whole length in the median line of the belly on the trunk. It begins at the front body end, as in the Gasteropoda, but never forms, as usually here, a sole or disc fit for creeping. Its arrangement recalls still most the form of the corresponding structure in the Heteropoda and Dentalia. Now it is hatchet-shaped, compressed from the sides, now tongue-shaped, now club-shaped. In many species it appears very rudimentary, as in Teredo, where it resembles a sucker, in others (in the so-called Apoda) it is even wholly lacking.

At the front body end, laterally from the mouth opening, lie the labial palps, two pairs of leaf- or tentacle-shaped structures (2), which I should like to regard as the morphological equivalents of the labial pads or velar halves; an interpretation which also, as we shall see, seems to be justified (1) by their **[p. 161]** development. The trunk of the Acephala is covered by the mantle; however, this is here not, as in the Gasteropoda, grown together with it in its whole extent, but for the greatest part free. Only in the median line of the back is it fastened. From there it hangs down at the sides under the shape of two considerable skin lobes, which take the body between them, as the cover a book. At the place of connection of these lobes with the body, between the two, in the mantle furrow, lie (2) the gills, whose peculiar form was already explained above. Also there already the peculiar transformation not seldom affecting the mantle was pointed to. Not everywhere, namely, do its two lateral leaves remain free lobe-shaped appendages. Very often rather a fusion begins at the hind end of their edges, which in the individual species advances variously far forward in the median line of the belly. Where this fusion takes place in great extent, the mantle forms a formal sac-like envelope of the body, which however only in very rare cases (Teredo) fuses with the outer covering **[p. 162]** of the body. Otherwise there remains everywhere a wide cavernous space, the branchial cavity, between the two. Nowhere, moreover, is this fusion quite complete. Always there persist in the mantle cavity, as the remnants of the original arrangement, single openings, a front one, for the passage of the foot, and two hind ones, of which the opening lying next to the back leads into the cloaca, the other into the branchial cavity.

Very usually, moreover, the edges of the two hind openings draw themselves out into two more or less long tube-shaped canals, which are to be regarded as appendages or prolongations of the mantle. Now these tubes (siphones) remain separate, now however they also grow together outwardly with the adjoining surfaces into a common structure. In Teredo, where they almost equal the trunk in circumference, it is they preferably which condition the peculiar long-stretched and worm-shaped body form.

Outwardly the body of the Acephala is covered by two mostly quite uniformly developed shells, which, answering to the two mantle lobes, have a lateral position. Naked animals belonging here, as there are e.g. naked Cephalophora, one does not know (1). By contrast in some cases, in Teredo and Aspergillum (2), the size of the **[p. 163]** shells in comparison with the body is only extremely small, so that merely a very small part of the body is covered by them.

So constituted, however, is the structure of the body only in the Lamellibranchiata. The Brachiopoda show in several respects a divergence. This rests chiefly on this, that the body is not compressed from the sides, but in the direction of the height diameter, from the dorsal surface to the belly (1). Agreeing with this there is found a dorsal and ventral shell, a dorsal and ventral mantle lobe. Gills are lacking. The mouth opening lies on the front edge of the short, tongue-shaped body in the median line, the anus on the one side. Beside the mouth are found two paired rolled-up arms of peculiar structure, which are usually regarded as analogues of the labial palps in the Lamellibranchiata, but which perhaps might also answer to the foot. That this appears here in the shape of two lateral elements cannot surprise us, the less so as we meet already in the Cephalophora the indication of such a formation, although here besides the two paired parts there is always at the same time the rudiment of a middle unpaired piece present.

The development of the Acephala is only very insufficiently known and therefore also offers a morphological conception of these animals so far only few sure points of support. However, it may the more find here a short consideration, as especially the investigations **[p. 164]** of Quatrefages (1) on the formative history of the Najades, the only Acephala which have hitherto been made the object of such an investigation, have spread various erroneous views. After the cleavage of the yolk the embryo at first forms, as in the Gasteropoda, an irregular, globular mass (2). Soon, however, one notices at the one place, there where later the mouth opening lies, at the front end then, a short and blunt prominence, which is distinguished by a clothing of very long, delicate vibratile hairs and later probably metamorphoses by a division into the two pairs of labial palps. The opposite hind end shows already now in the middle line of the belly a small notch, while on the dorsal surface the later outer coverings seem to be distinguished by a more considerable thickness. On the surface of the body, which likewise, like that front prominence, is clothed with cilia, though only with very short ones, stand, especially on the front half, at the sides of the labial pad, some glass-clear, long, bristle-like points. Later begins the formation of the shells (3) on the dorsal surface. Not always, however, do both lateral shells arise at the same time. Often one sees that one of them already possesses a very considerable size while the other is still very small or even still wholly lacking. Gradually, moreover, this difference is evened out, although not seldom even at far advanced development still always one shell appears somewhat smaller than the other. Both shells now overarch by their gradual growth the side surfaces of the **[p. 165]** embryo. They consist already early of two parts, of a larger basal piece of triangular shape lying against the back, and of an end piece which has the form of a lance point, is beset on the outer surface with tooth-like thorns and articulates at an angle on the ventral end tip of the basal piece. Merely the former pieces form the shells of the grown animal, the end pieces are lost in the course of later development (1). When now gradually the points of these end pieces strike against one another, then begins in the median line of the belly a formal splitting of the embryonic body, as whose first indication perhaps the notch mentioned above at the hind end of the embryo is to be regarded. The more this advances inward, the further gradually do the two lateral shells with the embryonic body masses enclosed by their vault diverge from one another. At the end both lie flat beside one another in the same plane. The stiff, bristle-shaped spines, which at first were found on the surface of the body, have meanwhile been displaced by the growth of the shells and lost. By contrast new ones form later, and indeed on the inner cleft surfaces of the body. The deeper the splitting reaches, the deeper also moves the formation place of these bristles (2).

Quite complete, moreover, the division of the embryo into two lateral halves never becomes. Always there remain in the middle between the two, as the remnants of the original connection, single masses which mediate their connection. In the hind half of the body these have transformed themselves during the gradual advance of the splitting into a strong muscle, **[p. 166]** whose transversely running fibres attach to the inner surface of the gaping shells and close these completely when they contract, as happens from time to time. Before this muscle, which reaches forward to the middle of the body, lies another pad-like yolk mass, which I should like to regard as the first rudiment of the proper trunk with the foot. It forms a transverse body of cylindrical or rather of short and broad, heart-shaped form. Its front lateral ends offer a peculiar appearance. They seem to contain in the interior a pretty large oval hollow. Quatrefages, who overlooked (1) the middle body mass in whose front ends these excavations lie, held the latter for two lateral stomachs and even described on each of them an intestine of its own, running arc-wise backward. Each half was accordingly, like an independent animal, to be furnished with a separate digestive apparatus (and heart), and the later completely divergent arrangement to proceed from a secondary growing together of the two body halves. Such an assumption, however, I must flatly contradict as a wholly perverse and erroneous one. To be sure, I too have not succeeded in recognising the remarkable embryonic structure with complete certainty. So much, however, I believe I can definitely assert, that an organisation such as Quatrefages ascribes to the lateral embryonic halves does not exist in reality. What he took for a stomach certainly has this significance just as little as the arc-shaped longitudinal pad described by him as intestine. What, moreover, these parts signify in reality I cannot say, since the later phases of development have remained just as unknown to me as to the earlier investigators. Not wholly **[p. 167]** improbable, however, is to me the supposition that the so-called stomach cavities in the projecting lateral corners of the trunk are the auditory vesicles, in which the otolith has not yet developed. As a rule (in the Gasteropoda (1), also, according to v. Siebold (2), in Cyclas) the formation of these concretions takes place, to be sure, already at an early time, soon after the laying down of the auditory vesicle itself has taken place, yet such is alone probably not yet sufficient to refute a supposition which seems to agree completely with the later arrangement of these parts.

At the hind end of the foot pad in the median line there has meanwhile developed an extremely long byssus thread, which is unbranched and outside the body can mostly be followed a very considerable stretch. Its greatest thickness this thread has in the lower part, with which it sits on a short cylindrical elevation, the so-called byssus organ (3). Whether this, moreover, really contains a gland, like the byssus organ of Mytilus, Pinna etc., whether it is at all to be set equal to this structure, seems to me by no means decided yet. I believe at least to have perceived, to be sure only a single time, yet here very distinctly, that the byssus thread takes its origin only from the immense lengthening of one of those glass-clear spines mentioned above. The appearance and constitution of the structures concerned also speak fully in favour of this observation. The trunk is, moreover, that part of the body which is longest furnished with such spines. Two of them stand at the sides of the byssus thread, two others further forward.

The yolk parts enveloped by the lateral shells of the young mussels **[p. 168]** themselves, which are separated from one another by the splitting of the original body, hardly ever unite again, as Quatrefages supposes. The middle unpaired body mass arises rather, as I should like to suppose, only through the gradual growth of the trunk, in whose interior only later the digestive and generative apparatus develops. Possible it is, to be sure, that for this also a part of the lateral yolk masses, in which distinctly a quantity of very characteristic embryonic cells is found, is used up. A point of support for this supposition is afforded at least by the circumstance that in some Lamellibranchiata the viscera of the body (the ovaries) extend laterally into the leaves of the mantle (so in Mytilus etc.). Perhaps this relation arises only from the persistence and further development of an arrangement common at the beginning to all Lamellibranchiata. In the other species it would then form only a transitory state. In the main, however, probably only the mantle lobes and gill leaves proceed from these lateral embryonic halves. As I suppose, the pad-like edge of the former has been held by Quatrefages for the lateral aortae, the outer boundary of the gill masses, still undivided as it seems on each side, however for the intestinal tubes. What he has interpreted as venous vessels, as well as arteriae meseraicae, I can only hold for interspaces between the embryonic cells of the lateral yolk masses.

Directly at the front end of the body before the foot one sees under favourable relations the same long and delicate cilia whose early arising I have mentioned above. Indisputably they cover the labial palps, whose metamorphosis from that hump-shaped, unpaired prominence may now already have begun.

So far of the morphological relations and the development of the Acephala, which despite manifold divergence **[p. 169]** nevertheless show in essentials a like typical arrangement with the Gasteropoda. Not in the same measure can such be said of the Tunicata.

The chief difference of these latter rests in this, that every trace of the fore body is lacking, that the morphological elements of it, labial pads and foot, are completely missed. The body of these animals is solely to be compared to the hind body of the other molluscs. The outer coverings are considerably developed, cartilaginous or leathery and offer chemically as histologically quite peculiar relations occurring nowhere else in the animal world. After the discovery of C. Schmidt (1), which later was extended and confirmed by Kölliker and Löwig (2), they consist of cellulose. An outer shell is lacking to the Tunicata. Instead, however, the coverings themselves sometimes show a considerable wealth of lime salts. In single cases, as I see especially in a new ascidian of the physiological institute here from Chile, these thereby even stiffen into a formal outer skeleton, as in the echinoderms. An interesting peculiarity of the Tunicata is it also that they very often, like polyps and Bryozoa, are united with one another in colonies.

Despite these relations, however, the Tunicata do not lack all relations of kinship to the other molluscs. The Acephala especially it is to which they join, with which they agree anatomically and also morphologically most. A community of the architectural plan is not to be mistaken, although it is realised in both in a several times divergent way.

The mantle of the Tunicata appears nowhere any more under the form of a free, bell-shaped or lobed appendage on the body. As in many Lamellibranchiata, **[p. 170]** it indeed forms a sac-like envelope, yet this is nowhere any more separated by an interspace from the proper trunk, but in whole extent directly grown together with it, as in Teredo. In this it finds its explanation that the body cavity with the viscera lies close beneath the mantle coverings.

Most distinct is the analogy of this arrangement with the formation of the outer mantle envelope usual in the Lamellibranchiata in the order of the ascidians, which by the form of their body in general still join the Acephala most nearly. The actual development of the mantle is, however, different. Its sac-shaped envelope forms in the ascidians not, as in the Acephala, through the fusion of two lateral lobes, but by this, that the outer cell layer on the body of the embryos lifts itself off in the whole circumference from the underlying masses. We find here a process which, as it seems, is also otherwise brought into application in morphogenesis. After in a typical group of animals a shaping has once been attained on a definite path, suddenly for the same purpose quite other means and forces are set in motion. Usually, moreover, with this at the same time a change in the relative value of the result is connected. While this was formerly attained more incidentally, it now becomes chief purpose. While formerly the shaping concerned was subordinate to the remaining morphogenetic phenomena, it now steps into the centre of the whole organisation. It acts determiningly and modifyingly on the whole body and even appears itself as seat and scene of a new series of shaping processes.

Just so now behaves the mantle of the Tunicata. In many cases it offers an arrangement of quite peculiar kind and completely divergent from the behaviour of the mantle in the Acephala. In other cases, by contrast, and so especially in the ascidians, the analogy is quite unmistakable. **[p. 171]** The mantle forms here, as said, a simple sac-like envelope, like the mantle of Mya, Pholas and other Lamellibranchiata with closed branchial cavity. At the one end this sac is fastened on stones, plants etc. At the other end two openings are found beside one another, not seldom on the tips of a short cone-shaped prominence. A longitudinal plane laid through both openings divides the body into a like right and left half.

The gills of the ascidians form, as already adduced above, a sac-like container, which lies in the outermost free end of the body and leads outward through one of the openings found there. The other opening is the cloacal opening. In the bottom of the branchial sac the intestinal canal takes its origin, whose windings fill the hind part of the body.

If one compares the organisation of the ascidians with that of the Acephala, there must above all arise the question of the significance of those two body openings. The one of them, so much may be said at once, is the cloacal opening. But the other? Does it answer to the respiratory opening of the Lamellibranchiata, as the neighbouring position of the cloacal opening lets one suppose, or perhaps to the mouth opening? Is — on this turns the decision — the free body end of the ascidians the hind or the front? At first sight the answering of these questions seems not hard. Already Cuvier declared the opening in question to be the respiratory opening, the free body end the hind. The analogy with the Lamellibranchiata it was which was to justify this. In both orders then the position of the cloacal and respiratory openings, as also that of the gills, would be quite uniform. The latter would then not have moved forward to before the visceral sac, but, as in Teredo, lie behind it. Only the mouth would then have a divergent arrangement, for which the reason might perhaps be found in the peculiar manner of **[p. 172]** attachment. The ganglion lying between cloacal and respiratory opening must here naturally be interpreted as branchial ganglion, not however as oesophageal ganglion, as we assumed above.

However, it is very questionable whether such interpretation is correct, whether it especially stands in harmony with developmental history. Of this further below. It suffices here to remark that the above-mentioned conception of the structure in the ascidians is not the only possible one. The free end of the body may also be interpreted as the front, the respiratory opening as mouth. The gills are then, so one must assume, placed before the visceral sac. Likewise the cloacal opening — perhaps because of the fastening of the body at the hind part — has moved up on the dorsal side of the body forward to near the mouth. A respiratory opening of its own would accordingly be lacking and also, given the divergent grouping of the gills, be just as unnecessary as the opening for the passage of the foot in the absence of the latter.

Before we now, however, examine these relations more closely, we will still add a few words on the structure of the salps, whose organisation is in several respects a divergent one. The hind part of the body is in these free and forms a small globular prominence (nucleus) on the lower surface of a wide cylindrical tube laid lengthwise over it, with front respiratory and hind cloacal opening. These two openings answer, as was already mentioned above, quite evidently to the two body openings of the ascidians lying beside one another, which here only stand diametrically opposite one another, the front on the ventral surface, the hind on the dorsal surface of the body. The nuclear surface of the cylinder is the lower and answers to the fixed end of the ascidians. The opposite brain surface is the upper.

What we know of the development of the Tunicata shows various very peculiar relations. The development **[p. 173]** of the salps, at least of the solitary forms, which alone arise by the sexual path, from a fertilised egg (but never themselves get sexual organs and therefore also must be regarded only as the nurses of the chained forms produced in their interior by budding), is still almost completely unknown. The more is such to be regretted, as we are entitled by the investigations of Krohn (1) and Sars (2) to the assumption that it departs considerably from the formation of the ascidians. As in the Cephalopoda, namely, in them not the whole yolk is transformed into the embryo, which moreover seems already from the beginning to resemble the parental animals. The rest of the yolk remains constantly placed outside the body (on the brain surface of the respiratory tube) and is even said, like a formal placenta, to connect the foetus with its mother animal.

In the ascidians, by contrast, the embryo arises directly from a transformation of the whole yolk globe and in a shape which is very different from that of its parents. It resembles a cercaria with globular body and a long-stretched cylindrical tail, by whose powerful movements it swims about freely. The outer skin is already now a pellucid envelope of mighty thickness, although in the interior no structure of any kind can yet be distinguished. After some time the larva fixes itself with the free end of its body and loses the tail. The remaining globular body then transforms itself into the form of a developed ascidian. The mouth opening forms at the same place where formerly the tail was attached.

Now, however, it is the question whether this place determines the front or hind end of the body. That the globular body part is carried forward in movement cannot yet prove that it is in reality the front one. Just as little the later point of attachment. We find, **[p. 174]** on the contrary, probably always that a larva fixes itself with the hind part of the body (polyps, acalephs, echinoderms), not with the front part. The possibility of the latter mode of attachment cannot, to be sure, be denied. However, it still lacks every empirical proof. The Cirripedia, the only animals which seem to belong there, do not properly fix themselves with the head end of the body, but merely by help of the front thoracic legs fused into a common stalk. Moreover the assumption that the so-called tail of the ascidian larvae belongs to the front body end is the less opposed, as otherwise too in the molluscs the appendages of the body (foot and velum) are fastened exclusively on the fore body.

Under such circumstances one might then perhaps have to decide that the free body end of the ascidians is to be conceived not as the hind but as the front, head end, that the respiratory opening therefore answers to the mouth of the Lamellibranchiata. What however makes the relations still more intricate is the observation of Milne Edwards (1) that after the attachment of the embryos and the loss of the tail the whole inner yolk mass of the larva gradually turns about its centre, until the part formerly lying against the point of attachment has taken up the opposite position beneath the initial insertion point of the tail. Only then begins the formation of the individual organs, of the branchial sac, of the intestine and also of the outer openings. Possible now that under such relations the correct conception of the structure in the ascidians lies in the middle between those two views mentioned above. The free body end is, to be sure, probably the front, as already adduced. Yet the branchial sac, so one must perhaps suppose, owes its position in the front section of the body cavity only to a **[p. 175]** secondary change of position. Originally it lies in the hind section of the body.

So far of the morphological interpretation of the ascidians. As to the compound forms of these animals, there still deserves here a special mention the circumstance that these, where they are distinguished by a very regular grouping (e.g. in Botryllus), form not merely, as Milne Edwards asserted, by gradual budding, but rather out of a single larva by the division of the inner yolk mass (1) (that is, through the mediation of an alternation of generations). Even before the larva fixes itself, namely, there begins at the front end of the inner yolk mass a splitting, which gradually advances backward and divides it (in Botryllus) into eight parts, which are grouped wreath-like round a middle cylindrical prominence and each develop into an independent animal, while the central prominence becomes the common cloaca. The outer glass-clear envelope, which from the very first encloses like a common sac the animals of such a colony, grows later also inward into the interspaces, between the individual individuals. Interesting, moreover, it is that in many Bryozoa, as was already mentioned above, a quite analogous relation is found. The more must such circumstance be regarded here, as it perhaps offers a new ground for the kinship of these animals with the Tunicata.

In the classification of the molluscs there prevails a great confusion and uncertainty, especially in the setting up and grouping of the uppermost divisions. The more the outer and inner organisation of these creatures becomes an object of investigation, the more distinctly does such emerge, the more pressing becomes the need of a **[p. 176]** new and rational division. As to the classes of the molluscs set up by Cuvier (from which naturally the Cirripedia are excluded), these need a change in so far as the Tunicata must be raised to a class of their own and separated from the Acephala, while the Brachiopoda are to be united with the Lamellibranchiata, as the Heteropoda and Pteropoda with the Gasteropoda. The four classes arisen in such a way are morphologically and anatomically strictly separated from one another. According to subordinate characters they articulate themselves into a varying number of orders.

The *Tunicata*, which were already acknowledged as a class of their own by Lamarck, de Savigny, Latreille, Goldfuss, Carus, Grant etc., while many other zoologists after the precedent of Cuvier unite them with the Acephala, fall according to the different manner of development and the differences of body shape (1) into the two orders of the *Ascidiae* (Tethydes Sav.) and *Salpae* (Thalides Sav.).

In the class of the *Acephala* we likewise distinguish two orders, the *Lamellibranchiata* (Cormopoda Nitzsch, Pelecypoda Car.) and the *Brachiopoda*, which by their morphological relations appear as two very natural groups of equal standing. The arrangement of the mantle and the shells, as the inner structure, show in them so much that agrees that they certainly may not be completely separated from one another (as besides Cuvier and others Blainville and Burmeister did), although one will also not be able to grant Lamarck right when he ranges the Brachiopoda without more ado among the Conchifera.

Far greater difficulties are made by the further division of the class of the *Gasteropoda*, which in the extent **[p. 177]** in which we consider it here agrees almost completely (only the genus Chiton (1) is excluded) with the Paracephalophora of de Blainville (2) (Cephalophora Meck.). Lamarck united the Gasteropoda, Pteropoda and Heteropoda separated from one another by Cuvier, but at the same time adds to them also the Cephalopoda (3). Still, however, those groups keep here their value as independent orders. The Cuvierian Gasteropoda are at the same time divided into the Trachelipoda and the proper Gasteropoda. In the former group are set together — without special regard to the structure — the case-snails with spiral shell, in the latter the rest.

Similarly we meet recently also in v. Siebold the Gasteropoda or Cephalophora, as they are named with Meckel, divided. The Pteropoda, Heteropoda and Gasteropoda form the three orders (4) of them, although already Blainville has emphasised the very close kinship of the former with the Pomatobranchiata, and the Cuvierian Gasteropoda also comprise several groups certainly standing just as high as the Heteropoda form one of them. By this, however, it is not to be said that I wish to keep the Cuvierian orders of the Gasteropoda as orders in their original extent. On the contrary it cannot be mistaken that here the constitution of the gills does not suffice as sole principle of division, and that many of the orders set up by **[p. 178]** Cuvier have hardly a greater value than that of families.

So in my view the Hypobranchiata, Pomatobranchiata and Pteropoda must be united into a single common order, which I should like provisionally to designate as the order of the *Heterobranchiata*. Very strikingly they are distinguished before all other Gasteropoda by a peculiar arrangement of the sexual organs. In the structure of the nervous system and the digestive organs too they show manifold agreements. Through the Hypobranchiata this group approaches the Gymnobranchiata, which with the Phlebentera (Apneusta Köllik.) form a second order. Outwardly the Gasteropoda belonging here are characterised by the absence of proper gills. Very fittingly therefore perhaps the Dumérilian designation of the *Dermatobranchiata* might be for them. In the arrangement of the sexual parts, as of the nervous system, they likewise possess various divergences from the other groups. The Cuvierian *Pulmonata* (1), *Heteropoda* and *Ctenobranchiata* seem to me very natural orders, distinguished by manifold anatomical relations. Likewise the *Cyclobranchiata*, which make themselves known as an independent order (2) especially by the peculiar structure of their sexual organs.

By contrast, I believe, the Aspidobranchiata must be dissolved. Haliotis evidently belongs to the Ctenobranchiata, Fissurella and Emarginula perhaps to the Pomatobranchiata. The Cuvierian Cirribranchiata show manifold **[p. 179]** analogy with the Heteropoda, as Deshayes (1) has especially emphasised, while finally the Tubulibranchiata might be added either to the Pectinibranchiata or perhaps also to the Heterobranchiata.

The groups, then, which I should like for the time being to reckon as orders to the Gasteropoda are the Cyclobranchiata, Heterobranchiata, Dermatobranchiata, Pulmonata, Heteropoda and Ctenobranchiata.

As fourth and last class of the molluscs appear the *Cephalopoda*, which certainly rightly form a class of their own and may not, as Lamarck, Nitzsch, Goldfuss, Burmeister and others have done, be united with the Gasteropoda (as Cephalica Nitzsch) (2). As orders of them perhaps the *Dibranchiata* and *Tetrabranchiata* (3) may be regarded, although it is not to be mistaken that the distinguishing characters of these two groups are hardly more considerable than those which otherwise are set up as marks of two related families. Possible it is therefore that both groups must here too be regarded only as so many families. Perhaps one would then have to seek among the numerous extinct species of this class (say in the Belemnites) the representatives of another order.

**[p. 180]** **Addendum to p. 35.**

During the printing of the foregoing work the August number of the Annal. des scienc. nat. 1847, with a treatise by Derbès on the development of the sea urchins, has come into my hands. The earlier investigations of Dufossé prove accordingly false. The embryo of the Echini transforms itself, without having first fixed itself, from an at first spherical body into a Pluteus. The later phases of development we know through J. Müller, whose investigations "über die Larven und die Metamorphose der Ophiuren und Seeigel" have just appeared in fuller form. The supposition pronounced by me, that the larvae of those echinus-like echinoderms observed by Müller had been spatangids, collapses accordingly. Likewise the statement of Sars that the madreporite plate is the rest of the earlier stalk. It is rather, as Müller (l. c. p. 30) shows, to be regarded as navel and marks the place at which the pharynx of the Pluteus hung together with the stomach later transformed into the intestine of the developed echinoderm. — On the other side, moreover, the agreement thereby raised above all doubt in the mode of development of the echinids and asterids — the older observations of Sars indisputably need a revision — gives a new ground for the union of these forms in a common class, as I have proposed above (p. 43).
