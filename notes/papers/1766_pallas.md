**[p. 1]** **I. ANTILOPE GRIMMIA.**

[Antiloparum genus.] The genus of Antelopes the methodical zoologists, especially the more recent, confound and mingle with the goats. Nature, however, has made it more distinct than the sheep tribe, which is joined to the goats by a far closer and true consanguinity, and yet is found associated with them by no author. A want of fairness in distinguishing or combining genera is the source of most of the hallucinations among the recent methodists, and makes most systems useless to beginners and in part insipid to the advanced.

[Characteres.] Nature has placed the Antelopes midway between the deer genus and the goat genus. In *habit* they are altogether cervine, indeed almost more elegant. But in *horns* that are solid, bony, sheathed in a horny case, and not always wanting in the female sex, they agree with the goats, the ram and the oxen. They have the spurious wart-like *hoofs* of the feet, as the goats do. In *teeth* they vary; most, however, in these too, and in the sebaceous or lacrymal *sinus* before the eyes, and in the frequent absence of horns in the female sex, emulate the deer. In internal structure they are ambiguous between the deer and the goats, yet come nearer **[p. 2]** to the latter; above all they are furnished with the gall bladder, which is denied to the deer genus.

From this the *difference of the Antelopes* from the deer and the goats can nearly be made out. They differ from the *deer* above all in the persistent nature of the horns, in the smallness of the hind hoofs, in the presence of the gall bladder in the liver, and besides in the want of the little bones in the heart.

They are distinguished from the *goats* above all by habit and by cervine hair; by the direction of the *horns* and chiefly by their *terete*, not flattened shape, such as the goats commonly have; very often too by the presence of the lacrymal sinus, and by the smallness and shape of the lateral incisor teeth.

So by a right of their own they deserve a name and are to be distinguished as a genus. The general name of ANTELOPES, it seems, may be given them, since it is so much the property of no species, either in meaning or in constancy of use, that it cannot be applied to several. For I think it better to apply to the genus a name hitherto vague but already known, and to set it before the species with a trivial word added, than to invent a new name for the genus, or to coin a proper one for each species and be a petty hindrance to other people's memories. Nor can I fail to praise the custom undertaken by LINNAEUS and introduced through the whole science of nature, of joining generic names to trivial ones, whereby the memory of the cultivators of the science is signally helped and the burden of apprenticeship lightened. For if a proper name were imposed on every single species of animals or plants, there is nobody who could fix a hundredth part of the series in his memory. We have an example in ADANSON's barbarous names for shellfish, which nobody wishes to learn or to celebrate. But if in the numerous classes, for instance of Insects, Worms and Vegetables, the *Linnaean* rule is to be followed in framing names (which all concede), why should we not, for uniformity's **[p. 3]** sake, extend it also to the no less numerous series of Quadrupeds and other animals; only let us be fair and judicious in making genera, clear in distinguishing, chaste in preserving, and not mix, traduce or sunder species at our pleasure.

[Antiloparum species.] The *species* of Antelopes roam in great numbers through Asia and above all Africa, some remarkable for size, all for elegance of body, agility and liveliness of limb. Most of their species live gregariously and often go in troops of hundreds. Scarcely any have fallen to Europe, set under a harsher sky. The whole of America too is by nature without the genus of Antelopes, to which the goat tribe also was once foreign, before the species transplanted by Europeans multiplied and spread there. Most true is this observation of the *illustrious* BUFFON, which teaches by a new argument that Nature did not make all the genera of land animals common to America and the threefold old world (a), but gave to each some of its own.

In this fascicle I shall describe rather carefully a very rare species of this genus, which has been scarcely known to zoologists until now. If to its history I prefix a brief enumeration of the species which I myself have had the opportunity of examining whole or in part, and insert notes on the rest **[p. 4]** on the authority of the best authors, I shall perhaps not seem to have undertaken a useless labour, since in no genus among the quadrupeds does zoology labour more and need observations more than in that of the Antelopes.

> (a) *[p. 3]* Genera of quadrupeds proper to America are the Bradypodes, Dasypodes and Caviae, of which more will be said later, and whose kin, as will soon be said, but barely to be associated with them, I know a single species outside America. The Didelphides also and the Myrmecophagae the *illustrious* BUFFON believed proper to the New World, but to me it is established about the African Myrmecophaga and the oriental Didelphis, as will be set out more fully below. Innumerable genera are proper to the Old World: Elephas, Rhinoceros, Antilope, Capra, Ovis, Erinaceus, Manis, Lepus; to stay among the quadrupeds.

[Divisio.] That I may treat the species more distinctly, I shall divide the genus of Antelopes according to the horns into *curvicornes*, approaching the goats; *lyricornes*, most of them commonly called Gazelles; *recticornes*, *contorticornes* and *spiricornes*.

[A. curvicornes.] Of the *curvicornes* some have the horns hooked at the tip toward the forehead, others bent back toward the back. Of these I know only two species (1. 2.), and likewise two in which the horns curve forward (3. 4.).

1. ANTILOPE *leucophaea*. Capra coerulea KOLB. *afr. ed. belg. I. p. 169*. Of this I have seen many skins sent from the Cape of Good Hope, where it is known in common Dutch under KOLBE's name of the blue goat (*Blaauwe Bok*). The *habit*, as in its congeners, seems to have been nearly cervine; the *size* between a stag and a fallow deer. The fore *teeth* in this species are singular, all namely equal, rounded and like infant incisors, set in an arch. The *horns* awl-shaped, falcate-recurved, scarcely divergent, ringed with annular wrinkles, the base smooth and slightly compressed, the tip smooth. The *body* above hoary grey, toward the spine and on the head blackish. The *tail* short, white at the tip; a streak before the eyes, and beneath the body and the feet entirely white. In the shape of the horns the animal celebrated by BUFFON (Vol. XII, p. 210) under the name *Kob* expresses this species. KOLBE reports that the colour of this Antelope alive is a bright bluish, but that it vanishes at once on death.

2. ANTILOPE *Rupicapra*. This seems to me to differ from the *Ibex* not in species only but in genus too, or in the whole habit, **[p. 5]** since beyond the mode of life and the colour of the hair it has nothing at all in common with it; so that it is a wonder that the *illustrious* BUFFON, who among the carnivorous animals loves to make species out of the slightest difference of proportion and variety of colour, should here, and in the Gazelles, have been willing to join animals so foreign in species, and should have thought Nature less constant and quite degenerate among the ruminants. The *Rupicapra* is the only European one among the Antelopes; alone it has the horns erect, hooked backward at the extremity, the hoofs rather long and widely parting, and behind the horns a singular wrinkled *cutaneous sinus*, blind (as I saw in a living one), which BUFFON neglected in his description.

3. ANTILOPE *Dama* of Pliny. Le Nanguer BUFFON. *hist. nat. Vol. XII. p. 213. t. 34*. Of this I have seen only the head with the horns, from which I learned that alone among its congeners it has only *six* teeth in the lower jaw, of which the 2 middle are very broad and end in a straight edge, the lateral small, sublinear. It is African, like most of the following, except the sixth and perhaps the fifth. It should be noted, though, that very many of the African species are common to Asia also.

4. ANTILOPE *reversa*. Mazame or horned Deer of New Spain SEB. *thes. I. t. 42. n. 3*? Capra of New Spain BRISSON. *quadr. p. 72. sp. 16*? Le Nagor. BUFFON. *hist. nat. Vol. XII. p. 326. t. 46*. This I know only from BUFFON's golden work.

5. ANTILOPE *Tragocamelus*. Tragelaphus of Caius in Gesner. RAJ. *quadr. p. 82. n. 10*. Tragocamelus. PARSONS, *Act. angl. Vol. 43. n. 476. p. 465. icon*. A paradoxical species described alive by PARSONS.

**[p. 6]** [A. Lyricornes.] I call *Antilopas lyricornes* those which have the horns shaped, by a double gentle curvature, into the form of the ancient lyre as it is *commonly* painted. The following species make up this phalanx:

6. ANTILOPE *Saiga*. Colus of Strabo. GESN. *quadr. p. 361. icon. quadr. 362*. Ibex imberbis GMELIN. *comm. Petrop. V. p. 345*. Le Saiga BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 198. t. 22. f. 2*. I have seen the horns, by whose subdiaphanous substance and yellowish colour this very rare species agrees with the goats.

7. ANTILOPE *Dorcas*. Dorcas AELIAN. *hist. anim. lib. XIV. c. 14*. Tzeiran of the Turks, Ahu of the Persians OLEAR. *it. I. p. 413*. Le Tzeiran BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 207, t. 3. f. 6*? (horn). I have seen whole skins. The *size* seemed to have been that of the *Cervicapra*. The *horns*, as in the following, slightly compressed, the wrinkles behind nearly obliterated. The 4 middle *teeth* broader. The whole head and neck, with the front part of the shoulders, are of a chestnut or very saturated ferruginous-sanguine colour. But the front of the muzzle is taken by a white longitudinal spot arising at the nose, truncate on the forehead and ending in a small point. *Ears* rather dull, pointed, the edges white, naked within. The colour of the back singular, above ferruginous, almost hoary-silvery, passing toward the sides into black. A black band on the flanks, abruptly bordering the whiteness of the belly, cut out at the shoulders by a ferruginous notch, broadly occupying the thighs and descending, attenuated, as far as the knees, which it occupies with a broad black ring, beyond which the legs are white. The rump *broadly* snowy, even above the tail; whence it truly deserves the name of *Pygargus*. A blackish seam ascends from the hoofs along the white feet. The fore knees very hairy, callous. A fairly good drawing of this species is given in the Dutch edition of the *Linnaean Zoology* published by D. D. HOUTTUYN, *Vol. III. tab. 24. fig. 2*, taken from the *celebrated* JOH. BURMANN's African paintings, **[p. 7]** cited here and there by *Linnaeus*. There, however, it is offered as the *Cervicapra* of *Linnaeus*.

8. ANTILOPE *Gazella*. La Gazelle BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 249. t. 23*. Very different from the preceding in size and colour, known to me only from the head.

9. ANTILOPE *Kevella*. Le Kevel BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 258. t. 26*. Gazella africana RAJ. *quadr. p. 80*. Small, of most tender aspect, most elegant, smelling strongly of musk, frisky, and horned in both sexes.

To this phalanx two species seem to need referring, one of which differs from the preceding in the horns indeed, but is allied by the other marks; the other has the horns lyre-shaped in a fashion, but twisted, and in habit disagrees with all the other Antelopes.

10. ANTILOPE *Corinna*. La Corine BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 261. t. 27. t. 31. f. 3, 4*. Never seen by me.

11. ANTILOPE *Buselaphus*. Buselaphus and Moschelaphus of Caius in GESN. *quadr. p. 121*. Vache de Barbarie. *Mem. pour serv. à l'hist. des anim. Part. II. p. 24. t. 39*. Le Bubale. BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 294. t. 37. 38. f. 2. and perhaps f. 1*. A figure of this too, taken from the Cape paintings which the *celebrated* JOH. BURMANN owns, you will find in the *third volume of the Linnaean natural history written in Dutch, Tabula 24. fig. 3*. From skins I know the animal, which seems to me rather the *Addax* of Pliny than the *Bubalus* and *Bubalis* of the ancients. In *size* it seems to exceed an ass. The head larger, and the feet much less long than in the other congeners, and the hind ones seem to be **[p. 8]** shorter than the fore. The *teeth* all broad, truncate, equal, the middle ones however a little larger. The lower lip black, with a moustache on each side at the margin. The muzzle in front ending in a black band under the forehead. The forehead with a tuft of whorled hair. The *vertex* raised between the ears, and on it the *horns*, black, approximated, their base spread out in front and almost touching, thick, unequally wrinkled, divaricate at the base in the form of a lyre, at first lightly curved forward, but toward the extremity bent strongly back as if broken, and approaching each other, the extremities awl-shaped and very straight, which the animal offers for battle with lowered head. The colour greyish-tawny, more ferruginous on the head and neck, brownish on the back, pale on the haunches and feet. Blackish longitudinal lines, one along the nape and one along the shoulders in front. A triangular streak at the bend of the haunches. *Tail* rather long, black, shaggy and somewhat tufted.

[A. recticornes.] Of the *Recticornes* I know only two distinct ones; they are:

12. ANTILOPE *bezoartica*. Gazella indica RAJ. *quadr. p. 79*. Pazas KAEMPF. *amoen. p. 398*. Le Pasan BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 212. t. 33. f. 3*. (horns.) Known to me from the horns alone, and perhaps not different from BUFFON's *Algazel*. A figure of this animal too, likewise from the *Burmann paintings*, D. HOUTTUYN published, *fig. 1* of the plate cited above.

13. ANTILOPE *Grimmia*. Whose history will be given here.

[A. contorticornes.] You would say the horns of the *Contorticornium* were twisted in a singular manner as if by hand. Of these too I find only two species.

14. ANTILOPE *scripta*. Le Guib BUFFON *Hist. nat. XII. p. 305. and 327. t. 40 and 41. f. 1*. Which I have not yet seen.

**[p. 9]** 15. ANTILOPE *Oryx*. The Oryx, as it seems, of the *Ancients*, at least of OPPIAN *de venat. lib. II. 445. 551*. Alce capensis KOLB. *afr. ed. belg. I. p. 173. icon p. 174*. Le Condous BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 357. t. 46 b*. (horns.) Known to the Dutch at the Cape of Good Hope under the name of the Cape Elk (*Kaapsche Elandt*), almost larger than a horse; of this a whole skeleton is kept in the *Museum* of the MOST SERENE PRINCE OF ORANGE, from which it is clear that the specimen exceeded a horse of middling size. BUFFON has drawn the horns excellently. From eyewitnesses I have learned that the colour of the animal is a very dilute grey, with a black line along the back and the ends of the feet blackish.

[A. spiricornes.] Of the *Spiricornium*, finally, remarkable for spirally twisted horns, likewise only two species have become known:

16. ANTILOPE *Strepsiceros*. Strepsiceros. CAIUS in GESN. *quadr. p. 295*. Le Condoma BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 301. t. 39*. (horns.) A species known to the Dutch dwelling at the Cape of Good Hope under the name *Koedoe*, pronounced Kudu or Koudou, under which title I have seen many horns and a whole head sent. The position and convolution of the *horns*, a foot and a half long, as in A. *Cervicapra*; a suture or keel beginning at the forehead runs along all the spirals; the inner side of the horns toward the base is slightly wrinkled, the colour, before they have been polished, black. The colour of the head (which is larger than an adult stag's head) is a duller, brownish cervine; around the eyes a whitish area, from which a snowy fillet runs out above the muzzle, joined with its fellow at nearly a right angle. A figure of the whole animal, as of the horns, D. HOUTTUYN has given in the already cited *natural history*, too faithfully *Linnaean*, *Dutch, third volume of the first section, tab. 26*.

17. ANTILOPE *Cervicapra*. Gazella africana or Antilope RAJ. *quadr. p. 79. n. 5*. Antilope BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 215. t. 35, 36*. Of this the description and anatomy will be given in the next fascicle.

**[p. 10]** It might seem to some that to this genus also belong the MOSCHUS of *Linnaeus* and TRAGULUS *pygmeus* of Africa, most certainly congeneric with Moschus, the smallest of all cloven-footed animals. But since those little animals are most certainly hornless in both sexes, and differ besides in shape, in the presence of canine teeth in the upper jaw, and in the want of the warts at the heels, I judge with BUFFON that they are rather of a genus of their own.

Nor can the CAMELOPARDALIS be referred to the Antelopes, an anomalous beast, which from a stuffed skin now standing in the *Museum* of the ACADEMY of *Leyden*, I have learned to differ from all the genera of ruminants, even the camels, to which however, BUFFON conceding (*hist. nat. Vol. XIII. p. 12*), it comes nearest, by its 8 spathulate teeth (of which the outermost is deeply bilobed on the outside), the entire upper lip, only two hoofs on the feet, produced backward, the horns covered with skin and ending in a tuft of black hair, the longitudinal mane of the neck and shins, as by the whole shape of the body; and to be an animal of its own genus.

That several species of Antelopes unknown to us roam the inhospitable deserts of Africa (a) I by no means doubt; these await the observers of the coming age, with innumerable other riches bestowed by Nature on Africa and India.

Meanwhile let me here describe and illustrate with a figure a most elegant little animal, the thirteenth of the Antelopes just enumerated; whose female, unless I am mistaken, GRIMM once described in the *Ephemerides* **[p. 11]** *naturae curiosorum Decur. II. ann. 4. obs. 57*, whence it afterwards passed into the series of quadrupeds given by RAY, KLEIN, BRISSON and LINNAEUS (b), and was celebrated as a hornless species, until the *illustrious* BUFFON recognised it as the female specimen of the Antelope to be described here; although he himself had the male known only from a skull preserved with skin and horns, and could describe it only as to these parts, and above all the horns, already drawn in SEBA (*thes. vol. I. tab. 43. fig. C, D*).

> (a) *[p. 10]* Of their number is a most elegant but obscure species, recorded by KOLBE under the name of the variegated goat (*Bonte Bok*), *Afric. ed. belg. Vol. I. p. 170*, to which he attributes the size of our *Antilope leucophaea*, and which he says goes in great herds.
>
> (b) *[p. 11]* Capra sylvestris africana Grimmii RAJ. *syn. quadr. p. 80*. Tragus, Capra fera Grimmii KLEIN *quadr. p. 19. E.* Tragulus Africanus BRISSON. *quadr. p. 97. sp. 4. Epit. p. 97*. Capra Grimmia LINN. *syst. nat. ed. X. p. 70. sp. 10*. La Grimme BUFFON. *hist. nat. XII. p. 307. 329. t. 41. f. 2, 3*.

The animal whose description I here offer, most beautiful, was sent from Guinea to the *Menagerie* of the MOST SERENE PRINCE OF ORANGE. Several specimens of it perished on the journey, and above all all the females, which I was told were hornless. Two males arrived alive in the *Menagerie*, both of which I have seen, and of which the tamest and most familiar one still survives; the other, which died in the winter of 1764, was wild, fleeing those who approached, snorting, and striking with its horns.

Our Antelope is a most timid animal in general, and most agile. It runs with a swift and elegant gait and knows how to withdraw itself by a quick leap. Restless, it usually stands with one of the fore feet raised, as is expressed in the figure (*Tab. I*). It scratches its body with the hind feet, and loves to be scratched, especially between the horns. It greatly fears thunder.

In the Menagerie it is fed on bread and chopped carrot roots, but more gladly eats the tubers of *Solanum esculentum*. When hungry **[p. 12]** it seeks food held out from above by standing erect on its hind feet, as tamed fallow deer do. It ruminates like all its congeners. The droppings of so small an animal equal a man's in thickness, greenish, as if compacted of globules.

This seems altogether to be the Antelope which BOSMAN, in the place cited by the *illustrious* BUFFON, says is found in Guinea and is called by the Negroes the Little King of the Deer: furnished with small black horns and tall slender feet, very light in running and leaping (*Voyage de Guinée pag. 252*). Nor should I deny that the account of another traveller cited by the *illustrious* man under *Antilope Grimmia* belongs here, who observed small does on an island near *Java*, equalling a hare in size, which the natives overtake in running and catch with their hands (*Voyage de* LE GENTIL *Paris 1725. tom. III. p. 73. 93*).

**DESCRIPTION OF ANTILOPE GRIMMIA.** *Tab. I. III. from afar, and IV. fig. 3 a b.*

In *size* it is about that of a two-month fawn of the fallow deer; in *habit* of elegance, namely in slenderness of leg, fit and handy fullness of trunk, shape of head and sharpness of aspect, and lightness of gait, it surpasses the most graceful of its congeneric species.

*Head* nearly like a roe's. *Nose* continually moist, naked and black; with lunate or subreniform longitudinal *nostrils*.

The margins of the *mouth* black, as are those of the subacute *tongue*. The middle *teeth* broad, **[p. 13]** the outer ones slightly oblique, the lateral rather sharp, gradually smaller. Upper *lip* most faintly bilobed, not cleft. Sparse *hairs* on the chin and on each side at the muzzle as a moustache. A hair-bearing *wartlet* on the throat.

*Horns* black, erect on the vertex, parallel, very straight, awl-shaped, rather thick, slightly compressed at the tip, scored with longitudinal striae, and ringed at the base with about four wrinkles (*Tab. IV. fig. 3 a*); on the outside, however, they show a longitudinal band (*fig. 3 b*) where both the longitudinal striae and the annular wrinkles are wanting.

Hairs from the forehead, rather long, stiffish, grey, bristle around the base of the horns and cover it. Between the horns rises a *little flame* composed of black hairs, from which a black band descends down the forehead, widening on the muzzle. This is what GRIMM noted as particularly conspicuous in the female.

*Ears* large, longitudinally and faintly as if three-channelled, sprinkled within with whitish hairs on the more prominent places, otherwise naked and blackish.

*Eyes* rather large, dark. *Lashes* black, larger and more generous on the upper lid. *Hairs* above the eyes rather long, scattered.

Before the eyes an *area* is observed, slightly concave, bald, black; in the middle of which is a callous, moist *furrow*, from which a certain ichorous matter sweats, though sparingly, hardening into black dirt. This too is celebrated by GRIMM and the authors who copied him, and is as it were the substitute for the lacrymal sinus of the deer and the rest of the Antelopes. GRIMM says the matter sweating in this area has a smell midway between musk and castoreum, **[p. 14]** and believes a medicinal virtue to be in it. In ours I found it quite odourless, nor so great a quantity of matter that it could be collected in any notable amount.

*Neck* terete, clothed on the lower part with coarser hair, more loosely and amply than the rest of the body, and has a yellowish-grey colour in common with the head. The throat, however, and the upper part of the neck are white beneath.

The *fleece of the body* is dense, close, somewhat finer and softer than a deer's, yet like it to a rough look; the *colour* in front grey, passes gradually toward the haunches into hoary-brownish, toward the belly turns hoary and beneath at last whitish.

*Feet* slender, blackening toward the hoofs, whence the blackness in the fore feet ascends in a line to the shins. *Hoofs* two on each foot, sharp, black; *spurious* ones are absent, and scarcely a tubercle is noted in their place. The *brushes* too of the fore knees, which are observed in many Antelopes, are wanting in ours.

*Tail* short, linear, white, marked above with a black band. The *scrotum* hangs large and black between the thighs, together with a conspicuous prepuce.

**[p. 72]** **VIII. APHRODITAE.**

[Molluscorum historiam imperfectissimam esse.] Of the *Molluscan* animals living in the sea we know well scarcely the hundredth part. So great, however, is the variety of Nature in their form and structure, and so great the discrepancy not only between genus and genus, but mostly between the extreme species of the genera, that even the most practised in these often stick in doubt to what genus this or that newly found animal of this class should be referred, or by what name they should call it.

Hence it is that the excellent BOHADSCH, not knowing how to refer to the Genera of LINNAEUS most of the marine animals whose description he laboriously drew up, everywhere furnished them with new names, taking the discrepancies of species for generic marks, and unaware that the generic structure of the Molluscs has been modified by Nature through the species into more than Protean forms.

Even LINNAEUS, who was the first who tried to marshal the ranks of the Molluscan animals (coming to him under the too special name of WORMS), seems not everywhere to have perceived Nature's design in constituting their *genera*, and above all to have fallen far from the natural *orders* of the Molluscs.

It is indeed not to be hoped that these natural orders can be determined today, **[p. 73]** since not only innumerable species but perhaps whole genera of the Class of Molluscs lie hidden. Nonetheless the attentive observer already perceives remarkable sparks of the method observed by Nature flashing out here and there. But this is not the place to set these out at length.

APHRODITAE, NEREIDES and SERPULAE are three genera of *Molluscs* constituted by LINNAEUS, closely allied among themselves, and therefore harder to separate as to species, the last of which, however, LINNAEUS placed in an order quite different from the former, and on account of the calcareous tube it makes numbered among the *Testacea*.

[Molluscorum ordines naturales.] Whoever, with a mind free from prejudices, has considered this order of the *Testacea* such as it is handed down by LINNAEUS and all the *conchologists* before him, and has not stopped at the accessory mark of the calcareous shell, but has examined the animals of which these shells are the covering, will necessarily perceive that a threefold tribe of *Molluscs* has here been gathered together by the authors against Nature's will, each of which tribes acknowledges some species lacking a shell among the marine animals. For so the whole family of the COCHLIDES or *univalve Testacea* has the genera of the *Limaces* (a) and the *Sepiae* joined to it by the closest consanguinity, and constitutes with them a natural order, to which perhaps the *Medusae* are to be numbered. Of the BIVALVE *Testacea* (with which ADANSON has judiciously taught that Teredo should be joined) **[p. 74]** the analogue and as it were naked copy is that whose single species BASTER (b) described under the name *Ascidium*, and several BOHADSCH and PLANCUS (c) under the title of *Tethya*. To this order, closely allied and perhaps to be added, will seem to many that one in which I judge the *Priapi* of LINNAEUS, the *Lepades*, the *Echini* and the *Asteriae* ought to be placed, together with the *Encrini*. Most of the SERPULAE, finally, differ from the rest of the testaceans (to which the shell is as it were in place of bone, and constitutes a part of the body) above all in this, that they are not grown to the tube; the *Serpulae*, I say, have a nature and structure in common with *Nereis* and *Aphrodita* and ought to be joined into one order, which makes a step toward the *Zoophyta*, and to which perhaps not ineptly the *Lumbrici*, *Hirudines*, *Ascarides*, *Gordii*, indeed the *Taeniae*, would be attached.

> (a) *[p. 73]* The genus of *Limaces* embraces all the *Dorides* and *Tethides* of LINNAEUS, and so BOHADSCH's *Lernaea* and *Argus*, and likewise, as it seems, LINNAEUS's *Scyllaea*. In marine animals Nature has everywhere varied and adorned the species with stupendous variety; whence the characters of genera here must by no means be narrow. The glutinous foot of the *Limaces*, whose substitute in *Scyllaea* is a furrow, is the essential character of this genus. The ornaments of the various species, or dorsal props, are in place of gills, and in some, like the tentacles, are wanting.
>
> (b) *[p. 74]* BASTER *Opusc. subseciv. lib. 2. p. 84. t. 10. f. A–D*. BOHADSCH *de animal. marin. p. 128. c. 7. tab. 10*.
>
> (c) PLANCUS *de Conchis minus notis. Edit. II. append. p. 109*. You will find Tethya already so called by RONDELET. To the same genus belongs a very rare Zoophyte drawn by RUSSELL in the *Acta anglica* under the name of the pedunculate Priapus, and by EDWARDS in the last volume of his figures, by no means to be numbered among the *Priapi*, which enjoy only a single aperture.

I shall here propose conjointly only the three genera just mentioned, namely the *Aphroditae*, *Nereides* and *Serpulae*, so that their true limits and characters, if it can be done, may at last be brought out, and their consanguinity appear the more clearly.

From the comparison of the species of *Aphroditae* to be described below the character of this genus first can be made out distinctly enough. Of the *Nereides* (to whose genus LINNAEUS had associated some true Aphroditae, as will be said next, and several of whose species the same author had inserted among the *Serpulae* and *Tubipores*), and of the *Serpulae*, we shall see below in their place.

**[p. 152]** **XII. ACTINIA DOLIOLUM.**

[Generis nomina varia.] The genus of marine animals which RONDELET called by the name of *Urticae*, in common with those today called Medusae, has now, as is the present fate of Natural History, obtained various appellations among various authors. HILL leading the way first, BROWNE and BASTER called them *Actiniae*. LINNAEUS described some species of them rather obscurely under the title *Priapus*. And at last the *most learned* GAERTNER appropriated the name *Hydra* to this genus and described five species of it, illustrated with most elegant figures (*Act. angl. vol. 52. p. 75*).

[Divisio in Actinias fixas,] Our genus, for which I have judged that the fit and more usual name ACTINIAE should be kept, can well be distinguished into two phalanxes. In the first phalanx will serve all those species which, glued by a broad base and destitute of an anus, show a short, *soft* body crowned with tentacles. Four species of these the *celebrated* GAERTNER has described excellently, from which two others, very distinct, occurring in the sea that washes Zeeland and Essex, differ.

[Et ambulantes.] The other phalanx contains species clothed in a thick and subcartilaginous **[p. 153]** skin, not fixed by a base, but furnished with an anus at the extremity opposite the mouth, and which walk about by means of peduncles distributed over the whole body after the manner of the Echini and Asteriae, bearing the aspect of the horns of slugs. Such is *Hydra corolliflora* of GAERTNER *loc. cit. tab. fig. 3*. Such an animal is described by PLANCUS under the name *Echinus coriaceus* (*lib. de conch. minus not. ed. II. app. p. 99. cap. 6. Tab. VI. f. D. E. F.*) To the same belongs the *Veretillum* of APULEIUS in the *apologia*, which VALLISNERI (*Oper. tom. III. p. 442*) called *Priapus marinus*, BARRELIER (*icon. 1293. n. 2*) *Cucumis marinus*, BOHADSCH (*anim. marin. cap. 4. tab. 6*) *Hydra*, and PLANCUS (*lib. de conch. ed. II. app. p. 108. c. 17. tab. 8. f. A, B*) *Mentula marina*; the like of which I have received from the Ocean washing the island of Antigua, where it is commonly called by the English fishermen *Sea-prigg*.

To this phalanx of the *walking Actiniae*, which makes the closest affinity of this genus with the *Echini* and the *Asteriae* (a), is likewise to be counted the species which I here bring forward as new and distinct from those mentioned above, and which claims for itself a middle place between the *Hydra corolliflora* of GAERTNER and the *Echinus coriaceus* of PLANCUS.

Among various marine things sent from the Cape of Good Hope in spirituous liquor I found several specimens of this our Actinia, of which **[p. 154]** the largest was the one I had expressed in our *Tabula X*. That it lives free-ranging in the sea is not in the least doubtful from its structure. Nor is more about its way of life known to me. So let it suffice to have sketched its external and internal structure.

> (a) *[p. 153]* Above (*pag. 73, 74*) I set out certain natural *Orders* of the Molluscan animals according to my opinion. Distinct from those there constituted, and a most natural Order, that of the *Centroniae*, as is said *l. cit.*, seems able to be made from the genera *Actinia*, *Echinus*, *Asterias*, and the genus of the *Encrini*, lately detected in the sea and vindicated by GUETTARD in the *Acta Parisina* and by ELLIS in the *Acta Anglica*, of which it is established that more than ten species are given among the petrified remains of their joints, known to the lithologists under the name of Entrochi, Asteriae and Caryophyllitae.

**DESCRIPTION OF ACTINIA DOLIOLUM.** *Tab. IX. fig. 10–12.*

*Body* ashy, five-sided, somewhat ten-faced, more attenuated behind, truncate at both extremities (*fig. 10*).

Three *sides* of the body rather flat, unequal; two somewhat confluent, slightly convex.

Two *angles*, interposed between the flatter sides, more prominent, obtuse, twinned, or channelled by a rough longitudinal furrow, and scarcely visibly porous. The rest obsolete, dotted with numerous subpapillary *little mouths*, disposed rather irregularly in quincunxes or in a double row, smaller and sparser toward the ends of the body (*fig. 10*).

The front extremity of the body (*fig. 11*) cupped with a cutaneous, slightly swollen *prepuce*: girding a slightly convex *disc*, pierced in the middle by the *mouth*, and flowering around its circumference with ten pairs of arms.

The *arms* arise, each from a very short, fleshy trunk, bipartite into an outer and an inner portion, each many-parted and as if clustered, or most granular with crowded dusky villi. They recall **[p. 155]** at first sight the scarcely bursting, most contracted cluster of a flowering Vine.

The hind extremity (*fig. 12*) is truncate-hollowed and pierced by the gaping opening of the *anus*.

**ANATOMY.**

I found the number and nature of the parts nearly the same as BOHADSCH described in his *Mentula marina* or *Hydra*. The cavity of the body was full of liquid.

The *crop* begins at the mouth rather wide, wrinkled within, and gradually made thinner continues into a rather wide *intestine*, muscular with circular fibres, gathered into windings by a mesentery, by which it is attached longitudinally to the middle of the three flat sides of the body. The intestine ends in a very large *cloaca*, occupying the hind cavity of the body and grown to it on all sides by fibres, as it seemed, muscular, and distended. The colour of the intestine was yellowish, and the whole swarmed with sand and crumbs of shells, such as I have noted above that I also found in the entrails of *Nereis cylindraria Capensis*.

Below the oesophagus adhered innumerable *little intestines*, thicker than a thread, slenderer at their insertion, terete, yellow, wandering in serpentine bends, not grown fast, gathered chiefly into the front part of the body cavity. These perhaps prepare the liquid needed for digestion.

From the crop seemed to arise a notable *vessel*, made of thin whitish membrane, very branched, dichotomously subdivided, distributed through the whole cavity of the body, with the ends of the branchlets blind, obtuse, here and there interwoven with muscular fibres applied to the envelope of the body. Not far from its origin a club-shaped *bladder*, marked **[p. 156]** at the point, white, hung attached to this duct. The whole was full of a liquid which had deposited a pale clot here and there in the branches.

A like viscus BOHADSCH found in his *Hydra*, but much less branched and less long. He took it for an ovary, nor am I far from his opinion.

The envelope or case of the whole body is made of a subcartilaginous, whitish *skin*; except only the area of the mouth and the prepuce, which are covered by a thin membrane.

The inner face of the skin shows at the five angles adnate longitudinal *bands*, tendinous, as it seems. Between them, stretched transversely, *muscular fibres* bind the skin, standing apart at regular intervals.

Five flat *muscular bands* besides, attenuated backward, run along the angles, entirely free and grown fast only behind; in front they are inserted into a *crown* supported by five flat calcareous *ossicles*, on which the arms of the animal sit, and which is as it were the analogue of the *lantern in the Echini*, except that it is wider and lacks the teeth with which that is found armed in the Echini.

Those bands serve to draw the crown with the arms back within the prepuce.
