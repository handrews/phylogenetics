**Prodrome of a new systematic distribution of the animal kingdom, by M. H. de Blainville.**

Although I am very far from regarding as entirely finished, and still much less as perfect, this new distribution of the whole of that part of organised bodies which is commonly designated under the name of *animal kingdom* — a distribution begun long ago and on which I am still working every day — I think it no less useful, if not for others, at least for myself, on account of certain particular circumstances that it would be too long and pointless to enumerate, to publish it in tables, that is to say in the most concise form possible, reserving to myself to develop it successively in as many separate dissertations.

Before all else, I must declare that my design has by no means been to innovate; but having considered zoology in a general way, and for a particular purpose, the teaching of the École normale, and having, so to speak, established *a priori* a manner of my own of considering it, I followed the plan I had set myself, without concerning myself with whether other zoologists might have arrived at the same idea and the same result as I. I must however make the preliminary observation that most of the new things, good or bad, that I propose have been set out, without any restriction, in the various public courses I have given since the year 1810 in Paris. Moreover, in the development and perfecting of this method, I propose, in a critical and impartial history of each part of systematic zoology, to set out frankly everything that others have established before me, as I have already done in two memoirs read to the Société Philomatique, one on the molluscan animals and the other on the articulated animals.

I also think I ought to preface this general classification with a summary exposition of the principles that have guided me in this work, and of the course I thought I should adopt.

I began by studying organised bodies, and above all animals, in all the parts of their organisation, under the special aspect of general physiology. This served me to bring back to a certain number of principal types all the anomalies I could meet with, and consequently to account for a host of modifications that an apparatus may have undergone in the long series of animals. This is without question, of all comparative anatomy, the most difficult part, but also the most fruitful in curious results, and perhaps even the one to which the name of *comparative anatomy* ought to be reserved.

I then occupied myself with grouping the animals according to this single consideration, that is to say according to the whole of their organisation, considering them as forming Types that may offer certain anomalies for a determined purpose, without concerning myself in any way with ease of instruction, or with disposing them in a systematic order. But these natural groups once formed, I had to seek to establish this systematic disposition, and for that I tried, so to speak, each of the organs or apparatuses in turn, and when it was possible to convert the grouping into a system, I chose the one which, while breaking the fewest natural relationships, was also the most easily translated to the exterior, when by chance it was not found there.

I should also have much wished to establish a truly rational nomenclature, which I believe really possible in zoology more than in any other part of the natural sciences; but the well-founded fear that it would not be adopted made me, if not abandon, at least postpone this project to a much later time.

It is thus, as will be seen, that I came to put in the first line the disposition of the different parts, or the general form of animals, which is found to agree with that of the nervous system when it exists;

Then the organ that supports this form, or the skin and its annexes;

After that the appendages that are added to it and develop on it;

Finally the different modifications and combinations of these modifications of the appendages, that is to say of the organs of the sensations, of locomotion in its different kinds, of mastication, and up to a certain point of respiration.

So that all the principal subdivisions I propose, and the only ones I regard as altogether good in my system, are established entirely on the organs of animal life; thus there is no longer any question in this prodrome of circulation, of a heart with one or two ventricles, of warm or cold blood, red or white, of aerial or aquatic respiration, double or simple — characters which, besides not being perceptible in themselves without anatomy, are scarcely translatable, and are much less important, that is to say offer zoological characters of much less value than is commonly thought.

As it would have been far too long for my present purpose to give the characters of the subdivisions I propose, and still more the reasons I have had for establishing them, I have confined myself to adding at the foot of each table, and in notes, what I believed most essential, letting myself be guided, so to speak, by the space.

*Observ.* It is easy to see that this table, at which I arrived through particular considerations, disposes the animals nearly in the order established by Linnaeus; that is to say that the insects come before the molluscs, etc. Without claiming here to decide the rank the former ought to occupy, I can announce that there are many more relations than is commonly thought between them and the vertebrated animals, as I propose to show in a work I am preparing on a new way of considering the nervous system and its envelopes. I shall try to show that the head in the vertebrated animals is composed, 1st, of a series of articulations or of fused vertebrae, each developed in proportion to the particular nervous system it encloses, as in the rest of the vertebral column; 2nd, of as many pairs of appendages as there are of these false vertebrae, and which may have different uses; one of them is to serve for mastication or for buccal prehension, as in the insects. As to the observation that in the vertebrated animals only, the jaws move from below upward, it is altogether erroneous, since there are several molluscs in which they act no otherwise, and since moreover in the insects themselves what is called *the lower lip* has no other movements. Besides, there are vertebrated animals in which the upper maxillary bones have a considerable lateral movement, as in several serpents and fishes.

I have included in this table all the bodies called *natural*, in order to show that the two kingdoms of the organic empire have, so to speak, a common term in one of their parts, which I have for that reason named *doubtful*: these are certainly the ones that most need to be studied.

Another small difference from the preceding table consists in considering the *Heteromorph* animals as differing more from the *Actinomorphs* or Radiaires than the latter from the paired animals or *Artiomorphs*; and indeed I am strongly inclined to believe, on anatomical and physiological grounds, that they have no kind of nervous system, whereas it is very probable that one exists constantly in all true animals having a determinate and symmetrical form.

One of the greatest defects of this systematic disposition of animals is without doubt the place one is, so to speak, obliged to give to the molluscs of the genus Cuttlefish, etc., which are animals very remarkable for their animal qualities; however one should reflect that the almost radial disposition and the uses of their tentacles can offer some points of comparison with the polyps, etc.

Lack of space not having allowed me to join to the table of the mammalian animals the explanatory notes it would need, I confine myself to saying here that their disposition is entirely by groups or natural families, considering the whole of the organisation, above all the encephalic nervous system and the bones that envelop it chiefly at its base, and regarding as anomalies the modifications that some animals of certain groups have undergone in the organs of locomotion and of the sensations. I think however that I ought to give notice of a new genus of Didelph animals which I have provisionally named *Phascolarctos*, while waiting for M. Geoffroy, to whom I have handed my description and the figures accompanying it, to be so good as to revise my work and make it worthy, by his cooperation, of entering his great work on the Marsupial animals. Intermediate between the genera Phalanger, Kangaroo and Phascolome, its principal characters are: 6 upper incisors, the two intermediate ones much longer; two lower ones, as in the Kangaroos; four small intermediate [teeth] above, two below; four molars with four tubercles on each side of the two jaws; five toes in front, separated into two opposable bundles, the inner one of 2; five behind, the thumb very large, opposable, without a nail, the two following smaller and united as far as the nail; the tail extremely short. Of the size of a middling dog, this animal has long, bushy, coarse, chocolate-brown hair; it has the bearing and gait of a small bear; it climbs trees with great ease: it is called *Colak* or *Koala* in the neighbourhood of the Vapaum river in New Holland.

The basis of this classification is really the form of the sternum and its annexes, that is of the clavicle (os furculare) and of the anterior ischium (clavicle), as I showed in a memoir read to the Institute on 6 December 1812. But as this apparatus is wholly internal and cannot be translated to the exterior by some organ dependent on it, I have been obliged to have recourse to the proportion of the limbs and to the disposition of the toes, like most ornithologists.

(1) The form of the sternum, etc., confirms the separation of this order, which all the rest of the organisation and the habits of these animals demanded.

(2) This separation of the birds of prey into 2 sections is in keeping with notable differences in the form of the sternum. This consideration confirms the place of the Secretary [bird].

(3) This order, although a little more natural than the one that had been established, because it contains almost all the birds with anomalous toes, has for common character two notches, more or less deep, at the posterior edge of the sternum, etc. (the cuckoo excepted), but without there being other connections to make; thus I have not observed that a particular disposition of the toes was in relation with one of the sternum. Moreover the *Roller*, which has perfectly normal toes, nevertheless has two notches, which brings it close to the *Trogons*, with which the *Rolles* evidently have much in common. The name of Climbers is evidently bad.

(4) In letting oneself be guided entirely by the consideration of the sternum, one would be obliged to put the Cuckoo here, which has only one notch, and to remove from it the *Rollers*, which have two. In the first section are placed the Nightjars, Swifts, Crows, Calao [hornbill], Hoopoe, etc., and in the second all the true Passerines of Linnaeus. The greatest anomaly is that the *Swallow* has the sternum of the second section, and that the Swift differs from it much.

(5) The establishment of the four sections of this order, as of the following one, is made according to a particular form of the sternum, etc.

The work from which this table is extracted was begun, and nearly finished, long ago; it was set out in full in my course of 1812 at the Faculty of Sciences. Its bases are anatomical, and above all drawn from consideration of the skull.

(1) The names *Ornithoids* and *Ichthyoids*, employed in case the reptiles should be considered as a single class, indicate that the first are formed on the plan of the birds, and the second on that of the fishes.

(2) In this order I make a distinct genus of the leathery Turtle, under the name of *Dermochelys*. Its principal characters are drawn, 1st from the nature of the skin, 2nd from the skeleton, whose ribs are neither fused with one another nor joined, by marginal pieces, to the sternum or plastron, which is almost entirely membranous.

(3) I thought I should establish this order, which, from the whole of its organisation, is intermediate between the *Chelonians*, and especially the *Trionyx*, which may well have true teeth, and the *Saurians*.

(4) From the detailed anatomy of most of the genera of this order, I am convinced that it is impossible to separate the *Saurians* cleanly from the *Ophidians*, since in fact there are true serpents that have legs, like the *Bimane*, and true lizards that have none, like the *Slow-worms*; so I make of them only a single order, which I designate by a name indicating the singular disposition of the male excitatory organ, whose two paired parts are not united.

(5) In this sub-order I have distinguished some new genera, among others that of *Monitor*, intermediate between the Tupinambis and the Dragons, whose principal characters are these:

*Monitor* (Sauve-garde). Head rather narrow, tetrahedral, covered with plates; nostrils round and terminal; tympanum large and superficial; tongue extensible, deeply bifurcate; teeth unequal, numerous, applied, the posterior ones sometimes very large, blunt; incisors distinct; no palatines; body elongated, narrow, covered above with small almost verticillate scales, and with small plates below; femoral pores; the tail very long, conical, covered with parallelogrammic, verticillate plates. Sp.: 1. Meriani; 2. Brasiliensis; 3. Maculatus; 4. Variegatus; 5. Peronii.

(6) This order will doubtless have to be suppressed and united with the preceding; for it is probable that the animals it contains do not always keep their gills.

(7) I long ago established in a particular memoir the necessity of considering the *Caecilia* as belonging to this class; in fact, besides the nakedness of the skin, the articulation of the head by a double condyle, that of the vertebrae almost as in the fishes; the absence of true ribs, which leads one to presume a mode of respiration analogous to that which takes place in all the *Nudipellifères*; the form and terminal position of the anus, which indicates that there can be no male excitatory organ as in the true serpents, etc.; the heart is composed of only a single ventricle and a single auricle, and there is a deeply bifid bladder as in the *Batrachians*.

Spec. Commersonii; Lamia; Lividus; Ustus; Heterodon; Vetus; Broussonetii; Glaucus; Caeruleus; Megalops; Heterobranchialis; Cornubicus; Monensis? Vulpes.

8. Cestrorhinus. Char. Teeth etc. as in the preceding; head broad, transverse, hammer-shaped with the body. Spec. Zygaena; Tiburo; Caroliniensis? Pictus.

9. Cetorhinus. Char. Body immense; teeth minute, conical, not serrated; the rest as in Carch. Spec. Gunneri; Peregrinus; Shavianus; Homianus?

(3) The division of the sub-class of the Gnathodont fishes is established on a wholly external character, and consequently a very good one; but it must be admitted that the skin of all the Crustodermes, though anomalous, is not always absolutely crusty, and that the scales in the second tribe are sometimes very small.

(4) The subdivision I establish here according to the existence and number of the limbs, new up to a certain point, is easy and important for the value of the terms. I must however warn that there are fishes which are *apodous* or *dipodous* by a kind of abortion, and that it is not of these that there is question here.

(5) I thought I should begin the order of the tetrapods with those that are abdominal, that is to say have the pelvic fins under the belly and suspended in the flesh, because it is evident that these are the most normal.

(6) This sub-order, not numerous, contains species of fishes that seem abdominal in the strict sense of the term, or in Linnaeus's definition, but are not really so anatomically.

**CL. VIII and IX. Malakentomozoaires, or Mollusc-articulates, or Sub-Entomozoaires** — Class I. Polyplaxiphores or the Oscabrions [chitons]. Class II. Cirrhipodes or the Anatifes [barnacles].

*Observ.* The bases of this new distribution of the molluscan animals were established in a memoir read to the Société Philomatique nearly two years ago, and an extract of it was published in the Bulletin des Sciences for the month of December 1814.

(1) Guided by received opinion, I had admitted as certain, in my memoir on this order (Bulletin des Sciences, February 1816), that the organs of respiration are placed on the fins of these animals, and I had drawn from this the denomination that distinguishes them. Since then I have assured myself, by the detailed anatomy of the Clio and of the Hyale, that it is not so, and that these fins are only organs of locomotion; so that it will be necessary to change this name, and probably the place I assign here to this order.

(2) *See*, for the animals I range in this order and the following, the extract of two memoirs inserted in the numbers of March, April, June, July 1816 of the Bulletin.

(4) This order, established on the structure and use of the organ of respiration, may well not be natural.

(5) The establishment of this order, the separation of the families and genera composing it, were the subject of a special lesson at the Faculty of Sciences, in 1815, immediately after the memoir of MM. Lesueur and Desmarest on the organisation of the *Pyrosomes* and the *Botrylles*, and consequently after their discovery of the aggregated Molluscs.

**CL. X–XVII. INSECTS and WORMS. A. Articulated, Entomozoaires.** Entomozoology or Entomology. Entomologists.

ENTOM[OZOAIRES] with the body in rings:

- Provided with appendages —
  - articulated, or feet in number — fewer than the rings:
    - Class I (1), 6 feet, Hexapods or Insects: Sub-Cl. I, Tetrapterans → Ord. Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, Orthoptera, Hemiptera, Neuroptera, Hymenoptera. Sub-Cl. II, Dipterans. Sub-Cl. III, Apterans.
    - II, 8 feet, Octopods or Arachnids.
    - III, 10 feet, Decapods or Crustaceans: Sub-Cl. I, Acères (2); Sub-Cl. II, Tétracères — Thoracics (Brachyures, Macroures); Athoracics.
    - IV, feet variable, Heteropods (3): Sub-Cl. I, Branchiopods; Sub-Cl. II, Squillaires.
    - V, 14 feet, Tetradecapods: Sub-Cl. I, the Tétracères (Crevettines, Aselles, Cloportes); Sub-Cl. II, the Epizoaires (4).
  - equal to the rings of the body: VI, Myriapods.
  - non-articulated: VII, Sétipodes or Annelids.
- Without lateral appendages: VIII, Apods (5): Sub-Cl. I, the Leeches; Sub-Cl. II, the Entozoaires (6).

In this new distribution of the articulated animals, which is the subject of a memoir communicated to M. Latreille on 19 June 1815, and read to the Société Philomatique on the 24th of the same month, one sees that the principle has been to draw the characters only from the organs of locomotion, or better, from the combination of the different kinds of appendages with which each ring of the body may be accompanied.

(1) From the year 1814, in my course at the Faculty of Sciences, I announced, as the result of researches begun, that in this class the mouth was really formed of the same parts, but in different degrees of development according to the use they were to have.

(2) Under this name I thought I should place here the Crab of the Moluccas, which I regard as intermediate between the Decapods and the Octopods.

(3) This class is doubtless bad, since it could not be characterised in a clear manner; so I regarded it only as provisional: perhaps it will have to contain a part of the animals that Müller named *Entomostraca*; I already think that the Apus must be placed near the Branchiopods.

(4) This sub-class, which I have made the subject of a particular work, will contain, besides the Lernaeans and several new genera that Dr Leach and I thought we should establish, the Calyges, Cyames, Chevrolles, etc., so as to pass insensibly to the Tétracères.

(5) In this last class, although the body still offers a paired and articulated disposition in the symmetrical lateral pores found in the Leech and in several intestinal Worms, it must nevertheless be admitted that the absence of any kind of appendage, and the disposition of the organs of the mouth, indicate a sort of passage toward the Actinomorphs: so they form an intermediate type.

(6) Under the name of *Entozoaires*, which is evidently bad since it is drawn from a circumstance not inherent in the object, and since moreover external species must be placed there, one very probably confounds animals whose structure is very different: compare indeed an Ascaris lumbricoides with a Ligula.

**CL. XVIII–XXIII. RADIAIRES and INFUSOIRES, or Actinozoaires and Hétérozoaires.**

II. Sub-Kingdom (1). ACTINOMORPHS. Actinology. Actinologists.

- doubtful (2): Leeches; Entozoaires; Annulaires.
- true:
  - Class I. Echinodermaires → Ord. I. Cylindroids; Spheroids; Stellerids.
  - II. Arachnodermaires or the Medusae.
  - III. Actiniaires.
  - IV. Polypiaires — Simple; Aggregated: Millépores, Madrépores, Rétépores or Eschares, Cellépores or Cellaires.
  - V. Zoophytaires or truly compound Polyps (3) → Tubulaires; Pennatulaires; Coralliaires.

**CL. XXIV and XXV.** III. Sub-Kingdom (4). HETEROMORPHS or Agastrozoaires. Heterozoology. Heterozoologists. — Class I. Spongiaires. Class II. Agastraires or Infusoires (5). Corallinaires (6).

(1) The organisation of this subdivision of the animal kingdom is not yet sufficiently known to me for me to be able to give anything very certain on the bases of their classification; I think however that the true Actinomorphs could be fairly well kept as M. Lamarck established them, making two distinct classes of the Medusae and of the Polyps, which I name compound.

(2) One sees reappear here the two classes of the *Leeches* and the *Entozoaires*, because I regard them as forming the passage from the *Entomozoaires*, to which they are however closer, to the *Actinomorphs*, to which the *Annulaires* are on the contrary nearer. Under this last denomination I include the Sipunculus and neighbouring genera.

(3) By compound animals I mean particular animals living on a common part that is equally living, with which each is in organic communication.

(4) I thought I should establish this last sub-kingdom for organised bodies that are evidently animal, but have no stomach properly so called. I place the *Spongiaires* there, because I am quite persuaded that these organised bodies have no relation with the *Alcyons*, and that the openings with which they are pierced may be considered as kinds of incipient stomach, etc. It may be that the animals forming certain species of *Madrepores*, like M. Lactuca, etc., belong to this group; indeed they do not seem to have to be rayed.

(5) Under the name of *Infusoires* it is beyond doubt that *Müller* confounded animals of different degrees of organisation; so we include here only those which, having no paired or radial form, enjoy no functions other than external absorption and exhalation.

(6) I have placed the *Corallines*, so to speak, out of rank, because whatever care I have taken to observe them alive, I have not been able to discover in them any sign of animality. It appears indeed that M. Brown [faint; likeliest reading] claims them for the kingdom of vegetable organised bodies.
