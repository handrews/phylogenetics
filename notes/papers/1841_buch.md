On Crinoids.

If fully developed crinoids were common in the St. Petersburg strata, individual segments of their stems would, as usual, be found in large quantities. Since these are, in fact, very rare and only occur in very small numbers, it can be concluded with considerable certainty that the forms of this animal species with numerous arms are not found at all. This then becomes an excellent zoological characteristic of the stratigraphic sequence, and all the more so since other forms take the place of the crinoids found in higher strata, which evidently form the starting point from which the later crinoid forms emerge. They therefore deserve a more detailed discussion and examination than they have received so far.

Here, too, Pander's efforts should not be overlooked. His descriptions and illustrations, even if they do not cover everything, have nevertheless observed many things more accurately and precisely than his predecessors, and they are perfectly suited to guide these investigations.

The Sphaeronites His. (Echinosphaerites Wahl. T. I. F. 14. 15. 16. 17.) are large, round spheres, like oranges, with two opposite poles, says Wahlenberg, which rise slightly above the spherical surface. They are found in sizes ranging from that of an orange down to that of small musket balls, and appear in incredible quantities. In Sweden, entire layers are paved with them, and near St. Petersburg they can be collected by the hundreds without difficulty. Therefore, they attracted the attention of naturalists very early on. Tilas and Linnaeus already knew them; and the excellent entomologist Gyllenhaal gave a model description of them with illustrations in 1772, which surprisingly highlight the keen and comparative eye of the observer (Vet. Acad. Nya Handl. 1772. p. 212). He considered them a type of echinus because of their round shape and because they are composed of many small polyhedral plates. Wahlenberg, however (Acta Ac. Ups. VIII. 52), believed that they did not sufficiently exhibit the characteristics of echinoids and gave them the distinctive name of Echinosphaerites, a name by which they are still largely known today. Pander also used this name and noted that just as crinoids can be considered as euryalids attached to a stem, so the Echinosphaerite is an echinus attached to a stem. For Hisinger, however, this similarity was apparently less convincing, for he completely rejected any association with echinoids and described the Swedish specimens under the name Sphaeronites, and indeed this name deserves preference: for the development of the forms leads to multi-armed crinoids, but in no way to echinoderms.

Tilas (1740), Linnaeus, Wallerius, and Cronstedt did not recognize the organic nature of these forms; they focused solely on the largely spherical shape and the composition of radiating calcite crystals, long cones that meet at their apex in the center and whose bases rest on the surface of the spheres. Therefore, Linnaeus called them "crystal apples" (Crystall-äplar) in his Oelandischen Reise (p. 136), and the mineralogists followed this example. Gyllenhahl, however, was not only the first (1772) to show that the radiating calcite crystals were something entirely foreign to these bodies and that they should be considered and studied as animals, but he also immediately provided a comprehensive and excellent description, to which little can be added even today.

Sphaeronites are therefore spherical bodies composed of a large number of polyhedral plates, shields, or ossicles. An opening, covered by a multitude of extremely small, movable shields, is located at the upper part; in the lower part, opposite the mouth, the sphere is attached to a thin, pentagonal stalk composed of individual segments, of unknown, but probably not very significant, length. On the upper hemisphere, there are also one or two larger openings, one near the mouth, the other, if present, further away. Each ossicle is also covered with a large number of very regularly arranged small pores, two of which are always connected by a groove. There can be no doubt that an incredible number of tentacles, or feelers, similar to those on the ambulacra of echinoderms, emerged from these pores and surrounded the entire sphere.

Gyllenhahl has described two species of these peculiar bodies and distinguished them by characteristic features. However, this has received little attention, even in Sweden, and consequently, knowledge of these animals has progressed little, if at all. What Gyllenhahl calls Echinus (Sphaeronites) Pomum has not been seen outside of Sweden, and is perhaps not very well known even among Swedish naturalists; for the illustrations, even in Hisinger's work, have so far always been only copies of Gyllenhahl's figure. Sphaeronites Aurantium, on the other hand, is the species that occurs in such large numbers near St. Petersburg, where Sph. Pomum, however, does not seem to have been found at all (see T. I. Fig. 14).

The essential difference between the two species lies in the fact that the antennae, or the pores from which they emerge, are irregularly scattered on the scales of Sp. Pomum, but in such a way that two pores are always connected to each other by their own small system (see the copy of Gyllenhaal's figure, Fig. 15, and enlarged Fig. 16), or by a small groove running from one opening to the other. On the scales of Sph. Aurantium, on the other hand, the pores rise from the corners of the polygons towards the center, but before reaching the center, they turn back towards the nearest corner in a parabolic line. Each opening is connected to the one opposite it on the adjacent scale by a deep groove (Fig. 17). This forms rhombuses that are deeply striated along their longer diagonal, and therefore the separation of the scales is often difficult to discern. It then looks as if the entire structure is composed of rhombic platelets, and this is indeed how Hisinger depicted it in his excellent Lethaea Suecica, Plate. 25, Figs. 8 and 9. Gyllenhaal had already noticed this remarkable organization and stated explicitly that the striation extends from one scale to another and does not form a rhombic platelet; however, he did not recognize its true nature; he believed that the tips of these rhombuses united in the center of each scale, which is incorrect. It was reserved for the observant Pander to show that these striations were actually antennal canals connecting the opposite antennal openings, and he had them depicted very beautifully and clearly in Plate 29, Fig. 3a (Plate I, Fig. 17). Since the antennae do not extend to the center of the woodlouse, the antennal canals cannot reach that high either, and the top of the woodlouse's head remains smooth. It is likely that this woodlouse is too thick in the middle for the antennae to have been able to easily penetrate it.

The discovery of these tentacles in such large numbers and in such regular succession on the surface of the Sphaeronites can be considered a very important contribution to our knowledge of the organization of crinoids. While Gyllenhahl, and perhaps others, might have emphasized an analogy with the tentacles of echinoid species, and thus been further strengthened in the opinion that Sphaeronites must be closely related to echinoids, these tentacle canals reappear in exactly the same form on a large number of crinoid species, where their nature has not yet been elucidated. The plates and shields of all Actinocrinites are covered with the same rhombic striations, such as Actinocrinites polydactylus (Bronn Lethaea Vol. IV, Fig. 4), also Rhodocrinites verus (Fig. 2), and especially Scyphocrinites elegans (Fig. 5). They are also clearly visible on Cyathocrinites geometricus (Goldfuss, Plate 58, Fig. 5) and on Actinocrinites laevis (Goldfuss, Plate 59, Fig. 3), which is already so remarkably similar to the Sphaeronites, as well as on Actinocrinites triacontadactylus (Phillips Yorkshire II, Plate IV, Fig. 16) and on Actinocrinites tessulatus (Fig. 21).  It might therefore be possible to find something similar in the living Pentacrinus.

The number of ossicles surrounding the Sphaeronites is so large that one tires of counting them. There may often be over two hundred. There is no consistency whatsoever in their shape and size. They are found ranging from triangular to polygons with ten, or even fourteen, sides (Gyllenhahl, p. 246); however, hexagons are clearly always predominant. Overall, it is not noticeable that the central ossicles are larger than those at the ends. Many smaller ones often seem to surround the larger ones. It is therefore probable that, as the animal grows, the smaller ossicles push their way between the larger ones and separate them from each other. Five ossicles surround the mouth and rise upwards from it like a small cylinder. Those forming the base around the stem are less distinct. However, since they only rise slightly, the side of the stem is easily distinguishable from the oral side. Furthermore, the regular pentagon of the always extremely thin stem is only very rarely difficult to discern.

The openings on the spherical surface of the Sphaeronites are very striking, and they distinguish them fundamentally from all crinoids. Their function is completely unknown and has therefore received very different interpretations from observers. In the center of Sphaeronites Aurantium (and therefore probably also of Sph. Pomum, although Gyllenhahl neither illustrates nor describes them in this species) one sees a very regular pentagon, which is enclosed by five equally regular triangles that rise above it in the form of a pyramid. This five-sided pyramid is always located on the upper, or oral, half, more or less close to the mouth, but it never comes closer than a quarter of the circumference. It is completely absent in no specimen. The triangular surfaces of the pyramid are sharply separated from one another and clearly show that they are valves that can open from the inside and close again. They are smooth, without sensory pores. Gyllenhaal saw the mouth of the animal in this pyramid, and assuming its similarity to Echinus, he called the side on which this mouth was located the lower side, and the opposite half the upper part, thus placing the stem at the top. Wahlenberg and Hisinger followed him in all of this, and even Pander; who now declares the actual mouth to be the point of attachment of the stem, because it is located at the bottom in his specimens, but considers the stem appendage to be an anus. If one carefully examines and compares the development of these forms progressing towards crinoids, it becomes clear that the mouth must be sought at the more inflated pole. It also does not seem entirely logical that a mouth that takes in food from the outside should be closed precisely by the entry of this food. The valves that only open from the inside seem more likely to be intended to allow something to exit from the interior. And so it might well be an opening for ovaries. Very close to the mouth at the top, another opening is visible, of a completely different structure than both the mouth and the delicate pentagonal pyramids in the center. This opening is small, but perfectly round or oval, entirely without any trace of corners; it is surrounded by a slightly raised edge and seems to penetrate deep into the interior. It is always located on the right side of the pentagonal pyramid; the examination of more than a hundred specimens leaves no doubt about this; Gyllenhaal also depicts it in Figures 4 and 8; but not in Figures 6 and 7, which may simply be due to a slight oversight. Hisinger also mistakenly depicted the round opening to the left of the pentagonal pyramid (Plate 25, Fig. 8), as did Pander (Plate 29, Fig. 2a); however, this was probably only because the engraver did not copy the drawing in reverse. This opening has the appearance of an anus, and therefore, analogous to irregular echinoderms, it is located very close to the mouth. Gyllenhahl also considered it to be an anus, and consequently so did Wahlenberg and Hisinger.

According to Pander, the St. Petersburg Sphaeronites are found in middle clayey limestone layers, especially near Jumalasaari, on the banks of the Pulkovka River, and in the ravines near Krasnoye Selo, and in these locations in extremely large quantities. They are also very common in Sweden, especially at Kinnekulle in Västergötland; at Mösseberg, Billingen, and Åseberg; Gyllenhahl informs us (p. 254), when mentioning this, that at these mountains Sph. Pomum is always found at a lower level and in much greater abundance than Sph. Aurantium. The limestone layer that encloses these bodies is smooth on the lower side, but extremely lumpy on the upper surfaces. If one examines these lumps and warts more closely, one finds that they consist entirely of closely spaced spheres of Sphaeronites Pomum. The two types of Sphaeronites are never found together. The higher-lying Sph. Aurantium appears where the limestone alternates with clay slate, in a thin layer of a dark gray, firmer, siliceous limestone.

At Osmundsberg in Dalecarlia, it is found in the slate itself, and also in the area of ​​Christiania in Norway. The specimens from the latter location have smaller segments than those from Västergötland; the antennal grooves are better preserved, concealing the segmental sutures, and then one believes to see a body composed of many strongly striated rhomboids. Hisinger described these forms as Sphaeronites Granatum; however, no essential differences from Sph. Aurantium are found. Both variations are also found together at Bödahamn on Öland and in Furudal and Vikarby in Dalecarlia.

With this beautiful and exceptionally delicate form, we take a significant step closer to the true crinoids. The many irregularly distributed plates of the Sphaeronites have contracted into a few shields, very regularly distributed around the circumference of the whole, and in the lower part, this regularity is very similar to that of the Platycrinites, as well as the Poteriocrinites. Only the upper half remains less compatible with crinoids;— no arms with many fingers and tentacles emerge yet, and lateral openings, which have never been observed in crinoids, still constitute an essentially determining part of the organism's structure.

The Hemicosmites is shaped like an inverted cone, broad at the top, tapering to a point at the bottom, to which a thin stem is attached, of which, however, only individual pentagonal segments are known. The pelvis rises on the stem; four plates or ossicles arrange themselves to form a decagon, which, however, deviates only slightly from a regular hexagon (F. 13.). _[16 in text, but clearly an error]_

On the sides of this hexagon stand six large, elongated plates, or costal segments, which widen slightly towards the top; they surround, polyhedrally, the greater part of the entire body. Six other apical plates are situated between the lateral plates and arch over the entire form. Only a small space remains at the apex, the mouth opening, which is first surrounded by six small plates and then covered by countless tiny platelets. Between two of the large lateral plates (costals) is a large opening, which is closed by five outwardly projecting flaps, just as is so characteristic of Sphaeronites.

This, in its entirety, is the form and the underlying structure of this remarkable organism. However, the individual parts are quite different from one another, yet even in this diversity, the underlying regularity and defining characteristics are unmistakable. They therefore require a precise description, down to the smallest details.

The pelvis, or basal cup. That it is formed from four plates, and that these unite to form a hexagon, has not yet been observed in any other genus of crinoids. Pander's figure, Plate 29, Figure 1, however, despite being very imprecisely and incorrectly drawn, demonstrates that this composition is characteristic of Hemicosmites. Two of these pelvic plates, lying next to each other, each form a symmetrical pentagon, with two equal, larger sides meeting at the apex, and two smaller, also equal sides, each situated between one of the larger sides and the fifth, single, horizontally positioned side. The other two plates are trapezoids, of which the two longer sides are equal to each other and to the longer sides of the pentagon, while the two shorter sides meet at the top at an obtuse angle. Towards the apex, all these plates are swollen into a small protuberance, which, on the inner side, shows an attachment point for the first, larger segment of the stem.

The thorax, the rib segments, or costals. The six segments that form it are divided into two groups; each with three adjacent plates, so that each group surrounds one half of the body. Three of them, on the side of the valve opening, are narrower, the three opposite ones somewhat wider. Two of the narrower ones are located on the upper pentagonal side of the pelvis, the third between them at the division of these pentagons. At the top, the narrow sides converge to a point. Therefore, two of these lateral surfaces are elongated pentagons, the one in between an equally elongated hexagon. However, the base of the pentagon is also slightly curved, suggesting that the hexagon is only concealed in this case as well. The three wide lateral surfaces are all located between the pelvic segments; and they therefore have a point at the bottom and would also be elongated hexagons if the upper point were not truncated by a small surface, thus creating a heptagon.

The vertex. All six vertex segments are wedge-shaped pentagons with two pairs of sides of equal size; they are located at the divisions of the rib segments and are therefore alternating with them. But between the segments that touch the wider lateral surfaces, on the side opposite the valve opening, three long and narrow plates are inserted, which rest on the truncated ends of the wide rib segments. One might consider them accidental, were their position not so precisely defined and so much in harmony and symmetry with the entire figure. The extremely small plates that cover the mouth at the level of the vertex seem to extend into three small proboscises or arms, which are perforated and which could easily be three openings of the mouth.

All these woodlice, so admirably arranged in a symmetrical pattern, rise gently from the circumference towards the center, so that each one actually forms a very flat pyramid, with facets equal in number to the sides of the woodlouse. Accordingly, a striation is clearly visible, parallel to each side, extending up to the central point. These are growth lines, and they prove that each woodlouse receives uniform growth from within, throughout its entire circumference; that is, a fine membrane secreting calcite emerges between the woodlice and at their points of separation. The woodlice of Cidaris and other genera of echinoderms grow in the same way; however, this is not the case with the countless woodlice of Sphaeronites, between which new, very small plates constantly emerge.

The impression of the extraordinary regularity of this wonderful form is considerably enhanced by the striking arrangement of the small openings that adorn each segment. These are small elevations, or tubercles, that rise above the surface; in the opening at their apex, one observes five, perhaps even six, teeth, which, as in echinoderms, appear to have been intended for the attachment of small spines. They are quite different from the pores from which the antennae emerge in Sphaeronites, for these penetrate from the surface to the interior and are never situated on an elevation. These spiny tubercles extend in a straight line from the centers of the rib segments to the three upper angles, but not to the three lower ones; only half of the surfaces are adorned in this distinctive manner. In contrast, three rows of tubercles also originate from the center of the apical segments, but towards the lower angles. They thus combine with those rising from the rib segments to form a highly elegant wreath encircling the entire figure. The tubercles extending towards the lateral angles are simple, 9 or 10 in a row; the middle row, however, is double; and on the broad surfaces, truncated at the top to form a heptagon, they actually separate, and each row ends individually in the angles of the truncated side. On the remaining parts of the segments and on those of the pelvis, tubercles are also scattered, but only in small numbers and without any trace of regularity in their arrangement.

The pelvis entirely belongs to Platycrinites, the thorax to Poteriocrinites. However, the apex is still closed, and no arms protrude from the sides. Yet, five ribs, which run from the pelvis to the apex beneath the plates and elevate them in a roof-like manner, are unmistakable. Such ribs also appear, from below, in Actinocrinites, where they also elevate the plates before breaking out as arms. The nature of crinoids is therefore already present in Cryptocrinites, but it is still hidden internally.

The pelvis consists of three plates, which are joined to form a pentagon; a composition characteristic of several crinoids; it is found in Platycrinites, Rhodocrinites, and Actinocrinites. But only in older specimens; it does not reappear in Juracrinites. Two of these three surfaces are pentagons, the third a rhomboid.

The thorax is surrounded by five costal plates, two of which are located on the broad side of the pentagons of the pelvis; the other three, however, alternate with the pelvic plates.

The apex is again formed by five plates, which alternate with the costal plates. At the very top, the mouth opening is surrounded by very small plates, but it does not always seem to be covered by them. Between the apical and costal plates, there is also a round opening, likewise with
5 valves on it.

This is the character of the genus. — But on some specimens, there are other intercostal plates above the five costal plates in an indeterminate number, which also increases the number of the five apical plates. If this were a distinct species, it could retain the name Crypt. cerasus, and the regularly formed specimens could be called Cryptocrinites regularis.

The side where the valve opening is located is swollen in all specimens, and one of the arms hidden inside passes close to it. The tendency of the arms to break through the shell on this side is evident. The size of these creatures rarely exceeds that of a pea; the stalk that supports them has the diameter of a pin. They have not yet been observed in any locations other than the hills near St. Petersburg.
