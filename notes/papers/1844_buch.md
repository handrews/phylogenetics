March 14. General Meeting of the Academy.
Mr. von Buch gave a lecture on cystoids, introduced by an
examination of the distinctive features of *Caryocrinus ornatus*.
Regarding *Caryocrinus*:
In 1825, Thomas Say described a genus of crinoids—distinct from all others then known—that had been discovered at Lockport, near Lake Ontario. This description, accompanied by an illustration, was published in the *Zoological Journal* (London, October 1825). Subsequently, Blainville (*Actinologie*, p. 253) and Mr. de Castelnau provided further illustrations, though these are inferior to those by Say. Numerous specimens held in Berlin collections, however, allow for a more detailed study of this remarkable form.
The entire animal is enclosed within a casing or calyx composed of small, highly regular plates (or "ossicles"), and this structure is anchored to the substrate by a slender stalk. Resting upon this stalk are four initial plates that form the base (pelvis) of the calyx; although they vary significantly in size, they are arranged with such regularity that any two adjacent plates are identical to one another. If the larger (pentagonal) plates were to be divided down the middle, the resulting halves would be indistinguishable from the smaller plates—in which case the base would effectively consist of six plates. That the larger plates are, in fact, composed of two smaller ones is most definitively proven by the distribution of the antennal pores (sensory pores) across their surfaces—a fusion that may be attributed to the specific expansion of the internal organs toward this side. Indeed, the mouth is located on the upper surface precisely along the line where the larger plates are divided.
— This remarkable phenomenon is common to all crinoids. In cases where the basal plates are not entirely uniform in shape or distribution, the mouth is invariably situated to one side, with an interradial or intercostal plate leading up to it.
In *Actocrinites*, the base of the calyx consists of three small plates, two of which are significantly larger than the third. The interradial plate—a distinctive feature of *Actocrinites*—is positioned exactly where the two larger plates meet, and it is in this same direction, on the apical side, that the mouth is found. This is clearly and beautifully illustrated for *Actocrinites amphora* in Portlock’s *Geology of Londonderry* (Plate XV, Fig. 4). Similarly, in *Poteriocrinus*—whose calyx base is likewise composed of three plates of unequal size—the exact same relationship exists between the position of the mouth and the larger plates.

Rising above these four basal plates (of *Caryocrinus*) are
six lateral plates (*cosiales*); by enclosing a regular
cylinder, they lend the entire structure a distinct elegance—
all the more so because their width and height maintain a
pleasing 2:3 ratio. The plate that supports the mouth is a
regular hexagon with its apex pointing upward, as are the
plates adjacent to it. However, two other plates—situated
to form a triangle with the oral plate—have their upper
apices truncated, resulting in heptagons that feature a very
small upper side; this reveals a quite unexpected symmetry
in the arrangement of these components.
Along the margin of the summit, three double arms and
three single arms arise. The former extend the summit into
a triangular shape: the mouth is situated on one side of this
triangle, while the aforementioned heptagonal plates (with
their truncated tops) lie along the other two sides. These
arms emerge through the centers of six "shoulder plates"
(*radialia axillaria* Müller); additionally, two very small
intercalated pieces are positioned upon the truncated edges
of the heptagons. All of these shoulder plates, however,
terminate at the rim of the calyx. The very flat, dome-like
summit itself is composed of tiny polygonal plates—one
larger plate in the center, surrounded by six others, along
with several smaller ones that completely fill the space
extending to the calyx margin.
Given this, it is difficult to dismiss the claim of the
*Caryocrinites* to be classified among the Crinoidea. Although they may appear to resemble the Poteriocrinites—due to their cylindrical form, their prominent lateral plates, and the scapular plates that alternate with them—a closer examination reveals that they cannot be classified within any existing group. Instead, they stand entirely alone, disconnected, at the very beginning of the series that marks the divergence of the Crinoidea from the Cystidea.
The governing dominance of the number five—which runs so remarkably throughout the entire realm of living nature—is especially pronounced in the Crinoidea, just as it is in all Radiata; this number dictates the specific position that every individual part must occupy. Even though various species of *Actinocrinus* or *Pentacrinus* may spread out an almost countless number of arms, these are invariably just a multiplication of the five primary arms that emerge from within the calyx. Similarly, while *Platycrinus* and *Actinocrinus* may appear to rise from only three basal plates, it is easily demonstrated that two of these are fused, and that, in this case too, the entire base of the calyx must be understood as being composed of five plates.
The situation is quite different in *Caryocrinus*. Here, there is no trace—not the slightest indication—of any arrangement based on the number five. Everything, down to the most minute detail, is determined and governed by the number six—a number that cannot, by any means, be reconciled with five. The base of the calyx consists of four plates of unequal size, which can readily be resolved into six identical and similar plates. Six lateral plates, along with six arms—comprising three double and three single ones—emerge from its rim. All of this is entirely alien to the other Crinoidea. A calyx base composed of four plates does not reappear—except in the single, uniquely shaped, and anomalous case of *Melocrinus* (Goldfuss, pl. 60, fig. 1)—and a calyx with six arms has never been observed in any other crinoid.
Everything governed by the number five develops from deep within the core of life. Everything governed by the number six, by contrast, is a product of the surface—of the external environment—which relates to the internal organs not through development, but solely through protection. When a plate—a point or a small knob—begins to form on the secretory membrane of the surface, it expands in all directions as the secretion continues, thereby assuming a circular shape. As these circles grow and come into contact, they constrain one another and flatten out, resulting in the formation of a hexagonal plate; due to this ongoing constraint, the plate is then compelled to continue its growth exclusively in a hexagonal form. Everything that subsequently emerges on the surface and takes shape—pores and stripes—must conform to this six-sided structure and is determined by it. In the case of the crinoids, however, five arms develop from deep within the base and extend far beyond the calyx; consequently, the governing principle of the number six is ​​restricted solely to the plates—and even that is not strictly the case. For, as they rise from the base of the calyx—even before they break through—the arms compress the lateral plates, raising them in the center and imparting to the entire calyx a distinct pentagonal form. For this reason, Mr. Müller aptly termed these plates—which herald the emergence of the arms long before it occurs—*radialia*, and designated the specific plate upon which an arm actually rests as the *radiale axillare*. These arms carry upward with them the ovaries—structures previously concealed within the calyx and characteristic of all such animals. Now situated well above the calyx, they are attached to the pinnules of the arms (Müller, *Pentacrinus*, pl. V, fig. 17). Being free—no longer compelled to squeeze through a narrow opening—they represent a new form of animal life, establishing the fundamental distinction between crinoids and cystoids.

On Cystoids.

Cystoids are natural bodies that sit atop a stalk, which anchors them to the substrate. Their more or less spherical surface is covered by a large number of interlocking polyhedral plates or ossicles. Between these plates, one finds openings necessary for the animal's life functions; however, none of these serve as points of emergence for arms. The animal is entirely devoid of arms.
Regarding their surface structure, all genera of cystoids share the following characteristics: 1) The mouth is situated precisely at the center of the apex, typically located within a flexible tube covered by small ossicles.
2) Adjacent to this mouth—at a short distance—there usually (though not invariably) appears a smaller anal opening, which pierces the ossicles but is not surrounded by any specialized plates.
3) Further toward the center—yet still on the hemisphere facing the mouth—there rises a five-sided (rarely six-sided) pyramid, situated above a round or oval opening comparable in size to the mouth itself. This pyramid appears to be composed of a corresponding number of valves. It likely covers the animal's ovarian opening.
These openings—other than the mouth—disappear once arms develop from the upper covering; this is easily understood when one considers that the reproductive organs (the ovaries) extend beyond the calyx, alongside the arms. A dedicated ovarian opening within the calyx itself would, in such a case, serve no purpose. Their presence is, therefore, highly characteristic of all genera of the Cystidea.
Since almost nothing of the animal’s internal parts is visible—only its external surroundings can be observed—it is not surprising that the number six predominates, while five appears only in very rare cases. Nevertheless, the latter does manifest in the stalk and the internal nutrient canal within that stalk, which I have invariably observed to be five-sided. In all genera, the stalk itself is remarkably thin, appearing scarcely capable of supporting the body it is intended to bear. In *Sphaeronites aurantium*, the diameter of the stalk opening is only 1/40th of the calyx diameter. In *Caryocystites granatum* (Wahl.), the calyx is fifteen times thicker, and in *Cryptocrinites cerasus*, seventeen times thicker. However, in its 1843 bulletin (Vol. X, no. 19), the St. Petersburg Academy published a paper by Dr. Vollborth that suggests a different conclusion. According to him, the stalks of the St. Petersburg *Caryocystites* specimens, at their point of attachment, reach a full third of the diameter of the test (the main body). These stalks show absolutely no sign of anything resembling a nutrient canal. Mr. Vollborth compares them—perhaps quite accurately—to the enigmatic *Cornulites*. Yet Murchison, to whom we owe the best illustrations of *Cornulites*, himself states that they should be regarded as parasitic animals, given that they are almost always found attached to other shells. One might well surmise the same regarding the St. Petersburg *Cornulites*. At the very least, Mr. ... Vollborth has provided numerous illustrations in which the *Cornulites* is not attached to the underside, but rather—evidently—embedded within the aperture. This is also apparent from the manner of its insertion, as the specimens penetrate the body of the cystoid; furthermore, no basal plates rise up around their ends—which would necessarily be the case were they integral parts of the body itself. Mr. Vollborth’s view runs counter to all analogy and, indeed, to all observation; it demands, at the very least, a far more rigorous investigation and examination than its proponent has accorded it.

1) *SPHAERONITES aurantium* (Tilas, *Vet. Acad. Handl.* 1840, pl. XI, fig. 18; Gyllenhaal, *Vet. Acad. Nya Handl.* 1772, p. 242, pl. VII, figs. 4, 5; Wahlenberg, *Acta Acad. Ups.* VII, p. 52; Pander, pl. XXIX, figs. 2, 3; Duke of Leuchtenberg, pl. II, fig. 17; von Buch, *Beiträge zur Kenntniss der Gebirgsformationen in Russland*, pl. II, fig. 14).

Spherical in shape, it sits atop a very thin, round stalk containing a pentagonal central canal. Six small plates form the base of the calyx. These are surrounded by a large number of additional plates—varying in size and arranged without any discernible pattern—to such an extent that one might count as many as twenty in a single upward-extending row. Most of these plates are hexagonal, though heptagonal, octagonal, nonagonal, and even more complex forms can be found with ease. Opposite the point of attachment to the stalk lies the mouth, situated on a small, tube-like projection covered in tiny scales. Further down—still on the hemispherical oral surface—is the large ovarian opening, which is covered by five valves; just to the right of this, yet very close to the mouth, lies the small, round anal opening, which does not protrude above the surface.
The surface of each plate is covered with lines and striations that run perpendicular to the plate's lateral edges; consequently, there are as many distinct directions of striation as there are lateral edges, with all lines appearing to converge at the center. Pander provided an excellent, accurate, and clear illustration of this (Plate XXIX, fig. 3a). The striations from one plate continue uninterrupted onto the adjacent plate, such that the two appear to form a single, unified structure. This creates a rhombus, characterized by prominent striations aligned with its longer diagonal—hence, these may be termed "rhombic striations." Toward the center of each plate, these striations terminate at an opening, which Pander interpreted as an exit point for small sensory appendages (tentacles). I have followed him here (Beier. zur Russ. Geb. p. 27) and have even ventured the hypothesis that the stripes might define grooves in which the tentacles could lie side-by-side, running parallel to one another, as they extend from one plate to the next. However, when the outer stripes are worn away—as is typically the case with *Sphaeronites* specimens found in their original deposits—and even when the boundaries between the plates remain clearly discernible, no trace of openings can be seen on the surface of the plates. Such openings would necessarily be present if tentacles were attached at the ends of these stripes. Consequently, the terminal openings are merely superficial; they appear to be nothing more than exits from a cavity that runs the entire length of the stripe. — In the *Sphaeronites* specimens found near Christiania, Norway, the stripes are so pronounced that the rhombic shapes they form stand out sharply from one another, completely obscuring the boundaries between the plates. In this form, they have often been classified under the name *Echinosphaerites granatum*. Hisinger has provided an illustration of them (*Lethaea Suecica*, pl. XXV, fig. 8).

2) *Sphaeronites pomum* (*Beitr. zur Best. der Geb. in Russland*, pl. I, figs. 15, 16, after Gyllenhal; Hisinger, *Lethaea Suecica*, pl. XXV, fig. 7).

Much remains unclear regarding this species. In its essential characteristics, it differs from *Sph.* ...not different from *Auranzium*; even the ovarian opening—which Gyllenhal failed to observe—is present in this species. The Duke of Leuchtenberg has described it and provided an illustration with the utmost clarity (*Beschreibung einiger Thierreste der Urwelt*, p. 23, pl. I, fig. 19). The distinction between the two species lies in the pores found on the plates. In *Sphaeronites pomum*, one can discern two pores connected by a groove; there are ten or twelve such connected pairs of pores on each plate. The Duke of Leuchtenberg also included an illustration of this, based on a specimen measuring 3 inches in diameter. *Sphaeronites* may be regarded as the symbol or archetype of all cystoids, as they diverge most significantly from any features reminiscent of crinoids; furthermore, their perfectly spherical shape suggests a development that proceeds almost uniformly from the center in all directions, rather than one oriented toward a specific upward direction, as is the case with crinoids.

CARYOCYSTITES.
The base of the calyx consists of four basal plates: two larger and two smaller. There are three superimposed rows of lateral plates.
3) *Caryocystites granatum* Wahl. (*Echinosphaerites granatum*, Wahlenberg, *Acta Soc. Upsal.* VII, 53; *Sphaeronites testudinarius*, Hisinger, *Lethaea Suecica*, p. 92, pl. XXV, fig. 9a).

The base of the calyx is divided into four parts, following the same pattern as in *Caryocrinites*. Specifically, two larger, asymmetrical pentagons lie side by side, with two smaller quadrilaterals opposite them. If the pentagons are bisected, the result—as in the other case—is six nearly identical quadrilaterals (rhombs with one vertex pointing outward); this suggests that the larger pentagons likely originated from the fusion of two quadrilaterals. Indeed, the ovarian opening is located along the line extending from the division between the two larger basal plates. There are six lateral plates situated on the sides of the basal hexagon, followed by another six alternating with them, and then a third row above that. A fourth, similar row forms the apex. The ovarian opening, located between the third row and the apical plates, is pentagonal but rarely retains its covering. The mouth, situated in the center of the apex, is always somewhat transversely compressed; the round anal opening lies to its side—positioned, as in *Sphaeronites*, to the right of the ovarian opening. A distinctive feature of this genus is that the plates are not merely covered by the rhombic markings but are so deeply concealed that they only become visible when the surface has been heavily abraded or eroded. Some genera named and described by Mr. Eichwald—*Cyclocrinites* (Spasky, *Urwelt Russlands*, p. 48, pl. I, fig. 8), *Asterocrinus* (*Silur. Schichten-System in Esthland*, 1840; Austen’s *Asterocrinus* was named later), and *Heliocrinites echinoides* (Duke of Leuchtenberg, p. 18, pl. They are round bodies displaying no visible signs of a stem attachment, mouth, or ovarian opening—in short, nothing at all that would characterize a genus of cystoids. Mr. Eichwald conjectures—and rightly so—that they could just as well be regarded as fragments of polyps akin to *Favosites* (*Calamopora*), similar to those illustrated by Mr. Pander (pl. XXIX, figs. 4, 5, 6 of his work). The cystoids belong strictly to the oldest formations of the earth's surface—the Silurian strata, or the transition formation. Nothing resembling them has yet been observed in more recent formations, let alone in the living creation. The fact that they constitute the starting point for an entire series of radiate animals is strongly supported by this ancient and isolated occurrence, and *Caryocrinites* provides striking evidence of how the transition from cystoids to crinoids could have taken place. — Once the arms have succeeded in emerging, the diversity of forms within this group of animals increases with extraordinary rapidity. It reached its peak
in the Carboniferous Limestone.
The rigid calyx, which in cystoids envelops and conceals
the entire animal, recedes increasingly; in *Pentacrinus*,
it forms little more than the base upon which the internal
parts find a point of support. In Jurassic formations,
the number of genera rapidly diminishes again, yet the
differentiation into individual species is all the greater.
Finally, in the upper Jurassic strata, the animal breaks
free from the stalk that had previously anchored it to the
seabed; in the form of *Comatula*, it is now capable of
locomotion. *Apiocrinites ellipticus* is the only crinoid of
the Cretaceous period that can still be compared to older
forms, and *Pentacrinus caput-medusae* remains in our seas
as merely a sad remnant of the splendor of the magnificent
sea lilies that inhabited the oceans of the past. Nature
has completely abandoned this evolutionary path. Yet in
*Pentacrinus europaeus* (*Comatula rosacea*)—discovered
in 1827—it seems to demonstrate the entire course of this
evolutionary process through the transformations of a
single species. (Müller, *Pentracrinus*, p. 7.) “At the
“beginning, the animal resembles a club (it is a cystid); it
“is attached by a broad base and puts forth a few transparent
“pinnules from its apex. None of the hard parts are visible,
“save for an indistinct outline of the calyx. At a more advanced
“stage of development, as the stalk elongates, the segments
“begin to appear; the pinnules become more prominent, and
“the bases of the arms as well as the cirri become discernible,
“whereupon the arms lengthen. (It becomes a crinoid.)
“Once fully developed, the animal detaches itself completely from its stalk;”
it becomes a *Comatula* and now floats freely in the sea, no
