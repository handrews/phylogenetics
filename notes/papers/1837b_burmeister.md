**[p. 383]** **Third Chapter. Types of the Animals.**

§ 528. The organs named form, in their union, the body of the animals, whose forms must now occupy us.

Every natural group of organisms shows a certain ground law by which the form of its members is determined, and which we denote by the expression *Typus*, or others by *Uridee* [primal idea]. The type is, so to speak, the hovering form of the individualities, or the abstract of the forms concretely given in natural bodies. So the animal body appears to us in its simplest shape as a round vesicle, which lets us recognise a small opening (the mouth) and, around it or over the whole surface, outwardly fine mobile hairs (cilia). This formation the simplest Infusoria have. From it a twofold divergent direction in shaping proceeds, of which the one works more toward mass, that is extension on all sides, the other more toward length, that is the linear dimension. The massive animal bodies keep the original globe form and show, as always, a single relation of all organs to the centre and the axis of this globe, at one pole of which the mouth opening lies. This mouth opening, now, as in the polyps, turned upward, now, as in the medusae and radiates, directed downward, forms as it were the centre about which all organs have been deposited, and so becomes also the midpoint upon which the first outer organs refer themselves, in that they grow out as radiations of the body in the surroundings of the **[p. 384]** mouth opening. In this the body at first keeps a soft constitution, later becomes firmer and separates, outside or inside, carbonate of lime for its protection or support; its form however remains globular (Actinia, Ctenophora, Echinodea), or becomes disc-shaped (Discophora, Crinodea, Asterodea), finally more rod-shaped (Holothurodea) with a predominant length dimension, in which case the mouth opening moves to the front end, surrounded by the tentacles.

§ 529. The form just indicated forms the transition to the more differentiated shape in which one can always distinguish a front and a hind, right and left, upper and lower. It is the peculiarity of all more highly organised animals, and expresses itself in the circumstance that the body can now be divided into two symmetrical halves only along a single direction, whereas such a division was possible in the earlier, lower animals along several or very many directions. Endowed with this peculiarity, the body of the next animals (the Molluscs) nevertheless keeps its old massive type, but in such a way that the length dimension running from front to back somewhat exceeds the others in extent. At the same time a rivalry arises between the ends of this dimension, in that the front end draws to itself the nobler organs of sensation and leaves to the hind end the less noble, vegetative ones, so that here, besides the anus, the entrances of the respiratory and reproductive organs also become noticeable, while in front sense organs, namely eyes, come forth. We call the front end at this stage the head, the rest of the body the trunk. The latter arches more upward, flattens below, and so shows in each different dimension another direction of its developmental striving. Slow, by the way, is the movement in such a shape, since particular organs of movement are still lacking, and the flat or wedge-shaped ventral disc, by pressing itself against any support, is the only aid for the striving after change of place.

§ 530. So scanty a surrogate, however, cannot last long, and Nature soon sees the necessity of coming to its aid in another way. It sets upon this end of the body a wholly altered type and stretches **[p. 385]** it at the cost of all other dimensions to the utmost in length. Already by the winding of its body such an animal will be able to move, the more so if at the same time it is articulated, that is has constrictions at intervals, where the skin is softer and so the mobility greater. So the type of the articulated animals comes forth. It evidently stands higher than that of all the groups mentioned before, because the articulated animals, besides the symmetrical body form, have also a higher capacity for free movement, the striving of all animal development, in themselves, and so can at the same time be read as a feeding and reproducing limb.

On this their lowest stage the articulated animals do not stay long; rather they soon seek, by forming independent organs of movement, to come nearer their goal. Since they are at the same time the ones in which animal organisation passes from the water on to the land, the supposition is not remote that the organs of movement will follow this transition and attach themselves to it, which supposition experience soon justifies; for all articulated animals furnished with true articulated organs of movement fall by their type into 3 great groups, which are:

1. Articulated animals with water-movement organs, or fins. (Crustacea.)
2. Articulated animals with earth-movement organs, or legs. (Myriopoda, Arachnoda.)
3. Articulated animals with air-movement organs, or wings. (Insecta.)

After the setting out of these 4 main types of the articulated animals the mass of forms must be exhausted, since there are no further outward relations of dependence by which it could be conditioned; and from within also no further difference can come, since this too is cut off by the assumption of the articulated type. If, then, a new higher development is to be possible for animal organisation, this can happen only under a new, divergent type.

§ 531. This new type offers itself in the vertebrate animals. If, namely, the animals of the first groups led the trunk with its structures to a higher completion, **[p. 386]** while the articulated animals, by contrast, set their goal almost only in the development of the organs of movement, there remains the higher completion of the head or sense organs as the task for animal development. The development of these organs is undertaken, then, by the vertebrate animals. Since thereby a new main division of the animal kingdom is necessarily conditioned, they show as such a peculiar type in the presence of the inner bony scaffold or skeleton, which, formed in all on a concordant ground plan, suffers manifold modifications only through the adaptation of this ground form to definite outer relations and to the progressive development expressed in all, from which then the different types of the main groups result. The outer relations are the predominantly determining ones in the vertebrate animals too, in that through them the organs of movement and the outer coverings, the most conspicuous parts of the body, have obtained a certain necessary form. So the following 4 types arise:

1. Vertebrate animals with water-movement organs or fins. (Pisces.)
2. Vertebrate animals with water- and land-movement organs, and a whole bodily organisation set equally between water and land life. (Amphibia.)
3. Vertebrate animals with air-movement organs or wings. (Aves.)
4. Vertebrate animals with earth-movement organs or legs. (Mammalia.)

§ 532. The presence of an intermediate or transitional group need not disconcert us here. Since, namely, the transition from water life to land or air life is much sharper and more cutting than from land life to air life, Nature could set it up no otherwise than by forming an intermediate group, which is organised for both elements at once. Such an intermediate group is found at exactly the same place among the articulated animals too, and is represented by the Crustacea, as can already be recognised in that most of them, namely the members of the higher tribes, possess fins (under the hind body) and legs (on the thorax) at once, so bear in themselves the organs of movement of both elements; these crabs can also really live in the water and on the land just as well as the amphibians, **[the chapter continues on p. 387 with § 533 and is not translated further]**

**[p. 417]** **Sixth Chapter. Systematics of the Animal Kingdom.**

§ 555. Already in setting out the natural system of plants it was remarked that it is the task of that system to prove the stages of development of the plant kingdom (§ 189). The same task the natural system of the animals has to solve with regard to the animal kingdom, and so for the animal systematics the same principles hold throughout which were developed for the plants (ibid.). As there the stages of development of the most perfect vegetable must agree with those of the plant kingdom, so here too the stages of development of the animal kingdom must be expressed in the evolutionary course of the most perfected animal form, in which however no exact formal agreement is to be thought of, but rather an approach to the prevailing character of organisation, so far as it is possible under the highest animal type once assumed. Only with this necessary restriction can the divisions of the animal kingdom be designated as arrested stages of development, for only in their type does the progressive higher unfolding express itself; they themselves are complete in themselves and were to climb no higher stage.

§ 556. Now we have already set out at an earlier place the main types of the animal kingdom (§ 533) and shown that upon the irregular the regular follows, and upon this the symmetrical type, which 3 are to be regarded as the ground forms of the animals. Of these the third again shows 3 main types, namely the unarticulated, the outwardly articulated and the inwardly articulated, which likewise stand above one another in the order given. According to this there would be 5 main groups of the animal kingdom offered to the unprejudiced observer.

§ 557. This way of regarding is however a merely outward one, which does not yet penetrate into the essence of the animal types set out; but science already makes here the demand just expressed, wherefore we have next to seek to answer the question: **[p. 418]** *What are the causes by which animal diversity is conditioned?*

Already in setting out the law of diversity (§ 16, 17) in Nature generally we found as its cause the several ground elements with which Nature works. So the nearest cause of animal diversity would be to regard the majority of the animal organs, and as the task for animal development the successive development of these organs from a homogeneous ground, similar to that in the life of the embryo, would offer itself. For since the formation of contrasts is the character of all organic development (cf. p. 153 and § 540 b), the homogeneous ground in which the first contrast becomes visible must be the beginning of organisms in general, and so of the animal organism also. Every new contrast, however, forms at the same time a new organ, and as soon as a new organ is there, there is also already a new stage of organisation, hence a new animal form given; but we shall find main divisions there where all the organs of one system appear as finished structures, and so the development of a new system begins. — According to this consideration there must, after the 4 animal systems of generation, nutrition, movement and sensation, be given 4 animal main groups, each of which is represented by one of the systems named. Here, however, is to be noted:

1. The animal exists only through the union of all 4 systems, so no animal can wholly lack one of the systems named.
2. The animals cannot have an equal development in all 4 systems, for then all animal forms would have to be equal and equally perfect.

There remains, then, no other assumption than:

3. The animals must, though in possession of all 4 systems, nevertheless manifest a different development of each single system, in order to be able to appear as different forms with progressive development.

Therefore an animal group may very well be designated according to the prevailing development of this or that system, without one having to assume on that account that only this system and no other were present.

If we attend next to how Nature has followed these principles, **[p. 419]** it soon appears that the symmetrical inwardly articulated or vertebrate animals are characterised by the highest development of sensation and the senses, the symmetrical outwardly articulated or articulated animals by movement and its apparatus, the symmetrical unarticulated animals or Molluscs by the height of their nutritive or reproductive organs, that by contrast in the regular and irregular animals the organs of movement and sense are wholly lacking, while the nutritive and reproductive apparatus stand at a fairly equal but imperfect stage of development. According to this there would in fact be 4 main groups of the animal kingdom, which would be:

1. Animals without organs of movement and sensation, but with vegetative ones, and with irregular or regular type.
2. Animals with predominantly developed vegetative organs and symmetrical type.
3. Symmetrical animals with predominantly developed organs of movement.
4. Symmetrical animals with predominantly developed sense organs.

Only this development admits a striking reduction, in so far as the first two groups prove themselves closely related by the equal development of two systems, and differ merely in the form type. The type, however, we may take into account the less, since the development of the system of the animals is to proceed from the organic systems of animality, hence we naturally should unite both groups and obtain only three main divisions. For the assumption of these 3 there is also a quite special inducement in the circumstance that the body of the perfected animals consists of 3 main sections, and so the animal kingdom, if it is to unfold in a like manner to the most perfected animal form, must likewise set out 3 main groups. So we take these three on, and name them after the systems and body parts to which they correspond, as follows:

I. Main group. **Belly-animals, Gastrozoa.** Animals without symmetrical organs of movement and without equally developed senses.

II. Main group. **Limb-animals, Arthrozoa.** Animals with symmetrical **[p. 420]** organs of movement and outer articulation, but imperfect senses.

III. Main group. **Head- or Backbone-animals, Osteozoa.** Animals with symmetrical organs of movement, inner articulation and perfect senses.

§ 558. In this way, then, the main groups of the animal kingdom would be found; it is asked further by what relations the subordinate divisions in each arise. For the belly-animals a significant diversity is already given by the threefold type which we perceive in them; this diversity is further increased by the different execution of the regular type, as indicated above (§ 533), so that these fall at once into 4 main groups or classes, which are:

A. Animals with irregular type, digestive apparatus, but without reproductive parts. 1. Cl. Infusoria.

B. Animals with regular type, nutritive organs and female reproductive parts.
 a. The mouth opening turned upward. 2. Cl. Polypina.
 b. The mouth opening turned downward or forward. 3. Cl. Radiata.

C. Animals with symmetrical type, nutritive organs and reproductive parts. 4. Cl. Mollusca.

The grounds for such a development lie in the necessity in the course which organisation takes in general. For from the irregular form, brought about by the still prevailing indeterminacy of the formative type, the more ordered type results first in the regular, in that it brings forth by uniform reference of all organs to a centre the irregular form from the irregular. If in this outer shape a contrast arises, then from it with necessity the symmetrical type goes forth, in which all organs lie in such a contrast in position and arrangement; at the same time, however, with the symmetrical type the mass of forms on this stage must be exhausted, since after the symmetrical arrangement of the organs present, only through the accession of new, hitherto undeveloped organs, can a new animal stage be called forth. Hence all 3 types form a common group. For the union of these animals designated as belly-animals into one main division speak further the like places of abode, **[p. 421]** in that all, with the exception of a few lung-snails, are water dwellers, then the like way of life, in so far as most nourish themselves on animal matter, finally the like construction of many elementary parts, namely of the muscle fibres, which lack transverse striation; lastly the like constitution of their only here so frequently occurring hard concretions of carbonate of lime; as also many other formative moments, less striking to the eye, which are especially pronounced in the generative process.

§ 559. If we come next to the division of the limb- and backbone-animals, there shows itself as an essential difference of these groups from the preceding a like ground type going through every group, bound up with equally many systems and organs, so that no differences coming from within, which could condition subdivisions, offer themselves any more. The concordant number and position of the organs is especially so great in the backbone-animals that really from the imperfect fish up to the human organism like organs and a like relation of them are met with, and only in the equally higher or equally deeper development of all 4 systems are the differences of the classes found. In the articulated animals it is in fact not hard to prove a similar relation, especially if one attends to the organs of movement and the nervous system, which most strike the eye; so here too a like ground type in all, and only a progressive uniform development of what is present.

*Whereby, then, are the classes called forth in these two groups?*

The answer to this question must proceed from the investigation of the relations under which the limb- and backbone-animals exist in Nature; for since the difference cannot come from within, it must probably be forced upon them from without. As such outer conditions for the modification of the type we recognise without difficulty the 3 media of water, earth and air, which, since the belly-animals are water dwellers, could for these be unimportant, hence also not determining. Since now the articulated animals are destined first for these media, and also have obtained the organs of movement as determining characters, and these are **[p. 422]** precisely those which must be most altered by the difference of the medium: so it lies open to day that the main differences in the type of the articulated animals can be brought about only by the different media for which they are destined. We obtain therefore:

1. Water-articulates, with fins.
2. Land-articulates, with legs.
3. Air-articulates, with wings.

On closer examination, however, a fourth group is found, which has fins and legs at once, and hence evidently stands between the first and second in the middle. It seems also to result as necessary from theoretical consideration, in that the transition from water to land life would otherwise appear too abruptly and suddenly, and so a leap in progressive development would have to become perceptible here. To avoid this leap Nature created a transitional group whose character is the presence of limbs for movement in both elements. So we obtain anew 4 classes of the second main group, which are:

1. Water-articulates. Articulated animals without articulated organ of movement, but with suckers, bristles, warts and skin flaps for creeping and swimming. 5. Cl. Vermes.
2. Transitional group. Articulated animals with fins, feet, legs and gills. 6. Cl. Crustacea.
3. Earth-articulates. Articulated animals with legs and lungs or tracheae. 7. Cl. Arachnoidea.
4. Air-articulates. Articulated animals with 6 legs, mostly wings and tracheae. 8. Cl. Insecta.

§ 560. The backbone-animals finally are, in regard to the inner and outer relations, in the same case as the articulated animals, indeed they are in so far still more constrained, as all show a like number and position of the limbs, and are determined merely by the higher development of the sense organs. We need not wonder, then, that for them too the media remain as the only main differentiating means, and that likewise four classes, determined by the same relations, occur; they are:

1. Water-backbone-animals, with persistent gills and fins. 9. Cl. Pisces.

**[p. 423]** 2. Transitional group, with transient gills, persistent lungs, and legs. 10. Cl. Amphibia.
3. Air-backbone-animals, with wings, feathers and lungs. 11. Cl. Aves.
4. Earth-backbone-animals, with legs, hairs, lungs and suckling. 12. Cl. Mammalia.

Why here the earth animals are the highest, while among the former the air animals were, is soon understood; for there movement is the main task, which is possible only in the air most freely and on all sides; here by contrast, where the development of the sense organs is in question, the animal needs a firmer and surer standing place, in order to become sharer, through this prevailing all-sided use of its senses, [in perfection].

§ 561. Herewith we believe we have solved our task of having to prove the classes of the animal kingdom as equally many necessary stages of development of it, and at the same time have furnished the proof that our systematics is no arbitrary and forced one, but is a quasi self-imposing, hence truly natural one. We have in this well regarded the efforts of others, but on the whole not particularly followed them. That nevertheless almost the same classes recur here which most newer systems have, lies in this, that these groups, precisely because they are natural, impose themselves upon everyone watching and seeing, in their peculiar delimitation; also we see in general not so much a merit in founding groups as rather in having proved in what relation the groups stand to one another, and what the leading idea is which has been expressed and made perceptible through the system. It is indeed illuminating: that from the monad up to man a like character prevails, namely the animal one, that all have wished to make themselves noticeable from one everywhere like and pervading formative type, rather the primal types have offered themselves to us as several, and the derived ones as innumerable. And the connection of these, not their agreement, we wished to show.

§ 562. For easier survey there follows here the system of the animals once more, but determined according to the mere differences of form.

**Seventh Chapter. Systematic Presentation of the Animal Kingdom.**

**First Main Group. Belly-animals. Gastrozoa.** Anim. zoophyta et mollusca *Cuv.*

§ 563. The members of this group are animals of very different organisation, which however agree in the following points.

Their outer surface is always a soft, almost always slimy, slippery skin, which shows a great inclination to calcareous production, and so forms shells, armour and scaffolds, which lie partly free on the surface, partly are deposited in the substance of the animal body. Always these coverings or scaffolds consist of carbonate of lime, which is deposited in layers under the form of small crystals (§ 97, p. 62) in the gelatinous animal matter and bound by it into a whole. In the naked ones the skin is partly covered with small mobile cilia, which in water excite a constant whirl, and bring about partly the movement of the animal, partly the flowing-in of nutritive matter. Because of this property the outer skin is to be regarded as a perfect mucous membrane (§ 514 a). Other outer organs are indeed often present, but not in all; where they occur, they appear as variously formed appendages, agreeing in substance with the body, often very contractile, in the surroundings of the mouth, in which at the higher stages a definite numerical law is always observed.

Among the inner organs the digestive apparatus stands out most, and is probably never lacking. At first it forms **[p. 427]** a mere simple cavity, without anus, which then narrows more and gets small vesicle-shaped side pouches, or instead of the vesicles sends out numerous branched tubes passing radially through the body; then it gets an anus, becomes more winding and more clearly intestine-like, on which intestine first liver, then salivary glands and peculiar secretory organs become visible.

Respiratory organs are lacking at first, so that the outer skin must take their place, which goes very well, since it possesses all characters of a respiratory surface; later there comes to it the inner body surface, in which the entrails lie, to use; then there appears as the first respiratory organ a sac going out from the anal opening, prolonged inward and branched, which excellently recalls the allantois; on this there follows the enormously widened mouth cavity, and finally peculiar, mostly outer but covered by skin folds, gills form. With the diversity of the respiratory organs the form of the vascular system stands in intimate relation. Where the respiratory organ is generally spread, the vessels are lacking; where peculiar local organs of respiration show themselves, the vascular system is also present. The blood is always whitish, rarely orange (sea urchin) or bluish.

Peculiar reproductive organs are wholly lacking in the lowest groups; they are replaced by the methods mentioned above (§ 535, 2). Of the sexual parts one notices first ovaries and last testes, both usually in the same individual; only the most perfect forms show separate sexes.

The nervous system appears, where it can be clearly perceived, as a pharyngeal ring, from which at first in regular form like radii, later symmetrical branches go out, whereby a distinct thickening of the ring above the pharynx represents the brain, from which the sensory nerves spring. Sense organs are lacking in most, more distinct, very sensitive ones for touch perception; yet in all groups red points at various places on the body are perceived, which are interpreted as eyes, an assumption for which the analogous colour of these organs in many articulated animals may count as the only inducement.

**[p. 455]** **Third Class. Ray-animals. Radiata *Lam.***

§ 593. Disc-, globe-, sac-, tube- or mushroom-shaped animals of soft, naked substance, or supported by a calcareous mass; the mouth in the mobile ones always directed downward or forward, in the fixed ones upward. All have a regular type, and according to a definite numerical law (4 or 5) organs regularly distributed around the central stomach cavity.

By this character we unite the two classes of the Medusae (Acalepha) and ray-animals (Echinodermata), which since Cuvier have kept and maintained this separation, following the example of Lamarck, again into one, and regard the union as justified by the agreement in the following chief features of their whole organisation.

**[p. 456]** Body form regular, almost always with central mouth opening, which leads into a simple or manifold blind stomach cavity, or into a winding intestinal canal, which however still has a simple or several anal openings, placed either opposite the mouth or laterally on the circumference of the body. In the surroundings of the mouth tentacles, partly short, retractile, simple or branched, partly long, appearing only changeable and as chief parts of the whole body, both according to a definite numerical law, namely 4, 5, 8, 10 or 15. In the latter case the tentacles are hollow, and sometimes, as in Actinia, perforated, and suck in nourishment, in which case then the mouth opening is lacking. They hold inner organs too, so they are preferably the germ or egg sacs, whose number thus harmonises with that of the tentacles. Besides these tentacles fine, mostly branched threads are found, which are capable of a special extension and serve to feel and touch, hence are called feelers (tentacula); they sit more on the circumference of the body, especially on the edge, when the body is disc-shaped. Often these feelers have stalked suckers with which they can fix themselves; in other cases the surface of the body is wholly or partly set with them, which itself however has no cilia, which excite a constant whirl. Besides there are found as outer organs calcareous spines of various size, but only in those where a lime secretion is met at all. These have then a protective surface, but the armour pieces lie not free on the skin, but in it, and are, like the lime scaffolds of the polyps, products of the mantle rather deposited inwardly. — Of inner organs, apart from the digestive apparatus already mentioned, which is proper to all, little generally valid is to be noticed. Glands have not been found on it, except in some the difference between stomach and intestine, which leads to the anus. Where the intestine is lacking, the stomach sends out radially branched canals, which open into a marginal canal that has various openings outward. — Respiratory organs occur in few; if lacking, the outer body surface takes their place, or the water enters through openings in the body cavity and respires there; the peculiar respiratory organ by contrast opens into the anus, and appears as an inner branched, **[p. 457]** many-vesicled cavity. Vessels are present only where either a separate respiratory organ is found, or the inner surface represents it. Rings around the pharynx are the centres from which these vessels go out, whose number again harmonises with the generally determined numerical law. — For reproduction all have eggs or germ sacs; the halving or budding do not fall to them any more, but the male organs are not yet present, perhaps in the Holothuria. For the execution of the manifold movements of the whole body, as of the individual parts, they have distinct muscular apparatus; their great sensitivity by contrast is conditioned by a radial nervous system going out from the pharyngeal ring. Red points at peripheral places of the body are here too held for eyes.

All members of this class are inhabitants of the sea and nourish themselves on animal matter. Many shine, others have a sharp juice in all body parts, which produces burning, violent inflammations easily passing into gangrene, indeed death on being eaten; they are therefore of no importance for human economy, only one family (Holothurodea) furnishes the dwellers on the coast lands washed by the Chinese-Japanese sea with a favoured food (Trepang).

According to the lack or presence of the lime scaffold they fall into 3 main groups as follows:

A. Without lime scaffold.
 a. Substance clear, gelatinous, form regular. I. Acalepha.
 b. Substance fleshy, outwardly leathery, form cylindrical. III. Holothurodea.
B. With lime scaffold. Form regular. II. Echinodermata.

**First (9.) Order. Acalepha *Cuv. Esch.* Jellyfish [Quallen] *Oken*. Medusina *alior.***

§ 594. Form varied, but almost always distinctly regular, with equal diameters in one plane. The substance soft, gelatinous, at certain places firmer, with distinct muscle striation; no lime scaffold, but often firmer, cartilage-like parts, in which lie wide cavities (swimming cavities); sometimes even the cartilage layer is calcareous. The body never divided into main lobes, but **[p. 458]** notched at the edge and furnished with larger tentacles, which sit on the lower surface and follow the numbers 4 or 8; always they are hollow, and open into the stomach. Besides are found fishing threads with stalked suckers, or simple and hollow suction tubes; few genera have lateral skin flaps, called swimming lobes. Digestive apparatus simple, sometimes even the stomach is lacking, and branched canals run from the centre out of the mouth, usually 1 simple or 4-pocketed stomach is present, sometimes 4 separate stomachs, from which probably simple or branched canals go out and open into a marginal canal. This has openings at regular intervals. All so organised lack the simple anus, by contrast certain genera have it with simple digestive cavity, from which no canals go out. Blood vessels are not distinct in all; in many they form a fine net under the skin, in the third tribe (Ctenophora) one notices 8 radial vessels, which open into a ring around the pharynx. Peculiar respiratory organs are lacking; the surface as a whole or in part takes their place. Reproduction by germs, which arise in 2–4 or more, in the surroundings of the stomach cavity, deposited sacs, and are not seldom hatched in pockets on the tentacles; the young ones with cilia, by which they move. — Nervous system without peculiarity, but clearly recognised only in Cydippe.

Fr. Eschscholtz, System der Acalephen. Berl. 1829. 4. m. Kpf.

**I. (XX.) Tribe. Siphonophora. Tube-jellyfish.**

§ 595. Form manifold, sometimes irregular, since the body often consists of 2 or more pieces, of which some are mere swimming cavities. No central stomach cavity, so also no simple mouth; instead of both numerous suction tubes, which suck in the nourishment and lead it to the body. They have no tentacles, but fishing threads set with suckers or spiral threads, which feel for the nourishment and seize it. All in tropical seas.

1. (37.) Fam. **Diphyidae.** The body consists of a front cartilage piece, on which hangs a thread with equally many suction tubes and egg sacs (hence called the suction-tube piece), and **[p. 459]** with which a second, hind swimming-cavity piece is loosely connected. Shape mostly elongate, the two pieces of equal or unequal form and size.

Diphyes. Both pieces equally large and similar, the front one also with a swimming cavity, the thread-shaped appendage set with equally many suction tubes, egg stalks and fishing threads in groups. The suction tubes open into the canal of the thread, and this into a small cavity which lies above the swimming cavity in the front cartilage piece. — D. regularis, pieces quite equal, 1″ long, the hind one with 5 teeth at the entrance of the swimming cavity; on the thread up to 30 organ groups. South Sea. (Meyen in nova act. ph. med. Vol. XVI. suppl.)

Eudoxia. Both pieces equally large or unequal, then the hind one larger, the front one without swimming cavity, with 1 suction tube, 1 egg stalk, but several tentacles. — E. Bojani, hind piece 3 times as large as the front, with 4 teeth at the entrance of the swimming cavity. Atlant. Ocean.

Gen.: Ersaea, Aglaisma, Abyla, Cymba.

2. (38.) Fam. **Physophoridae.** The soft body has, as in the preceding, a thread-shaped form and ends above in an air-holding bladder; under this sit on it numerous cartilaginous swimming cavities and beneath them the suction tubes or fishing threads in a main group, sometimes covered or enveloped by skinny scales. They swim vertically, the air bladder upward, and dive by sudden emptying of it; they love the high sea.

Physophora. Air bladder not large, pear-shaped; swimming cavities stalked, in 2 rows; fishing threads whorled, each at the base with a bladder full of moisture, which, by being pressed into the cavity of the thread, causes its extension; in the middle of the whorl the shorter suction tubes. — Ph. muzonema, 4″ long, 6 yellow swimming cavities, fishing threads yellow, middle of the bladder blue, likewise the suction tubes, on whose bases the high-red egg stalks. Atlant. Ocean.

Rhizophysa. Swimming cavities not yet observed, hence the upper part of the body appears naked; fishing threads at the base without bladder, with spiral threads set. — Rh. planostoma Peron., Voy. Atl. t. 29. f. 3., rose-red; under Madagascar in the world sea.

Gen.: Apolemia, Hippopodius, Epibulia, Agalma, Stephanomia.

3. (39.) Fam. **Physalidae.** The body consists only of a single large bladder, on whose underside many suction tubes and fishing threads sit, the latter with unstalked suckers. Swimming cavities lacking. Between the suction tubes the egg sacs. There.

**[p. 460]** Physalia. Bladder elliptical, on the back with a comb, consists of 2 skins, of which the inner forms partition walls in the bladder; at the front pointed end an opening, which according to v. Olfers (Abhandl. der Berl. Akad. 1831) perforates only the outer skin, according to others both; in the first case it would be the anus, since on pressure a brown fluid comes forth, and it leads into a cavity on the underside into which the suction tubes also open; in the latter by contrast the entrance for the air into the bladder. — Ph. caravella (Ph. Arethusa Eisenh., Ph. pelagica Lam., Cystisoma atlantica Lesson), bladder 1′ long, reddish, with toothed comb; suction tubes violet, branched, fishing threads blue, several feet long. Atlant. Ocean. On touch causing violent burning and inflammation.

Gen.: Discobale.

4. (40.) Fam. **Velellidae.** Body disc-shaped, on the back side an elliptical cartilage or fine lime plate, in which many small air cells; on it usually a thin comb. On the underside in the middle a large and around it many small suction tubes, in whose surroundings short fishing threads of equal or different form.

Velella. Comb cartilaginous, with calcareous core, obliquely on the calcareous elliptical back plate, this with concentric lines, which indicate the discs in the interior, of which it consists. — V. indica, elliptical, the edge notched; shell from right to left in oblique direction. Indian Ocean.

Rataria has a soft comb, Porpita none.

**II. (XXI.) Tribe. Discophora. Disc-jellyfish.**

§ 596. The disc- or bell-shaped body has neither swimming cavities nor swimming bladders, but moves merely by rhythmic contractions and expansions of the disc, which is called the hat. On it 4 or 8 tentacles in the middle of the underside, which however are lacking in many genera, and numerous simple fishing threads, partly between and on the arms, partly on the circumference of the hat. Stomach cavity not distinct in all, but canals which run to the edge and open into the marginal vessel mentioned. In these canals the numerical law four prevails. The edge often notched and divided into 8–16 blunt lobes, in the notches 8 peculiar dark organs, on which above 1 stalk with a red point (eye). Around the stomach at equal distances 4, rarely 8 (Cassiopea), often no **[p. 461]** egg receptacles, which lie on the roof of a cavity which opens outward freely. The numerous species are found near the coasts and swim ½–2′ deep under the surface in oblique position, the upper side of the hat turned forward.

A. Cryptocarpa. Without egg receptacles and marginal points.

5. (41.) Fam. **Berenicidae.** They have no stomach, but 4 canals going out from the centre, also no mouth, but suction tubes. Tentacles lacking. Disc quite flat.

Berenice. Disc with fishing threads on the edge. — B. rosea, 2″ in diameter, the 4 vessels against the edge 3-parted, blue; fishing threads very long, at the base with round yellow bladder. South Sea.

Eudora has no fishing threads, otherwise like Berenice.

6. (42.) Fam. **Aequorina.** Body disc-shaped, flat, without tentacles; in the middle a stomach, with wide protruding mouth and lateral extensions in the body.

Aequorea. The stomach sends many simple canals in radial direction to the circumference of the disc; on the edge fishing threads, none on the surface. — A. Forskaliana, 1′ in diameter, against 100 canals, under each a skin fold; fishing threads as long as the diameter of the disc. In the Mediterranean.

Aegina. Hemispherical, the stomach with 8 sac-shaped appendages; several simple fishing threads in the intervals of every two stomach sacs. — A. citrea, the sac-shaped appendages notched in the middle; 4 fishing threads, each in a furrow on the back side of the disc; 2″ diameter. In the north Pacific.

Gen.: Mesonema, Cunina, Eurybia, Polyxenia.

7. (43.) Fam. **Oceanidae.** Hat bell-shaped, in the middle with freely hanging, tubular, below open stomach, from which canals arise at the base, which run through the hat.

Oceania. Stomach small, sends simple canals like radii out, at the mouth 4 short lobes; on the edge as many fishing threads as canals, none on the surface. — O. conica, disc pointed, inside 4 ribs; against 40 fishing arms. At Gibraltar. Several species of this genus, like: O. lenticula, microscopica and pileata, shine very strongly.

Thaumantias. Stomach larger, sends 4 canals out, which widen into bladders; numerous fishing threads around the circumference with bladdery base, none on the surface. — Th. cymbaloidea, canals suddenly bladdery, disc ½″ diameter. — Th. hemisphaerica, canals gradually bladdery, 2″ diameter, shines. North Sea.

Gen.: Callirrhoe, Tima, Cytaeis, Melicertum, Phorcynia.

**[p. 462]** 8. (44.) Fam. **Geryonidae.** Hat stalked downward, the stalk perforated by canals which lead to stomach sacs in the disc and open with pores at the end of the stalk. On the edge fishing threads.

Geryonia. No tentacles, in the disc several (4, 6, 8) separate heart-shaped stomachs, on the edge just as many fishing threads. — G. proboscidalis, 2″ diameter, clear; stalk cone-shaped, 6 bladders and fishing threads. Mediterranean.

Lymnorea. With tentacles at the base of the stalk and numerous fishing threads on the edge. — L. triedra, hemispherical, warty; the marginal threads short and thin; stalk long, 3-edged, with 8 arms. At New Holland.

Gen.: Favonia, Eirene, Saphenia, Linuche, Dianaea.

B. Phanerocarpa. With germ sacs and 8 dark marginal points. Ehrenberg remarked in Med. aurita on the back of these points a stalk which carried a red eye, and in the cavity under the stalk a kind of nerve knot, surrounded by crystals. In the stalk juice circulation was noticeable. In the middle between every 2 points an opening in the marginal canal (anus).

9. (45.) Fam. **Rhizostomidae.** They have no mouth, but 8 tentacles, which are run through by canals and porous on the surface; these suck in the nutritive matter and lead the juice to the stomach.

Cassiopea. Tentacles divided to the base, no fishing threads; 8 germ sacs around the stomach. — C. lunulata (C. rhizostomidea Til.), disc edge notched, the germs large, in 3 rows; diameter up to 2′, greenish. North Sea.

Rhizostoma. Tentacles grown together at the base, no fishing threads, 4 germ sacs. — Rh. Cuvieri, the disc bell-shaped, on the edge with 16 notches; the tentacles 3-edged, almost winged, thickened at the end, otherwise only hanging down. On the coasts of Europe, but not in the Baltic.

Gen.: Cephea (like the preceding, but with fishing threads).

10. (46.) Fam. **Medusidae.** They have a central mouth and usually 4 arms beside it. All show 4 germ sacs, and most fishing threads, partly on the disc, partly on the circumference.

Medusa. Disc flat; 4 frizzy arms as long as the radius; fishing threads short, only on the circumference. — M. aurita, 3–4″ diameter, reddish, the germ sacs yellow, tentacles with lateral frizzy folds. In **[p. 463]** all seas of Europe. (On their structure cf. Ehrenberg in J. Müller's Archiv, 1834, p. 572 and following.)

Cyanea. Likewise, but the arms thick, flat, keel-shaped, the fishing threads longer than the disc. — C. capillata, up to 8″ diameter, reddish, the edge with 16 notches of alternately equal depth; the 4 stomach pouches with folded appendages of yellow colour; arms longer than the radius. In Europe.

Pelagia. Hat almost globular, 4 arms grown together at the base; stomach with 16 side sacs; but without canals, 8 fishing threads going out from the middle of the alternating stomach pouches. — P. noctiluca, reddish, clear, disc flat, warty; stalk at the base of the arms long. Mediterranean; 3″ diameter, fishing threads 1′ long. Shines strongly.

Gen.: Ephyra, Chrysaora, Sthenonia.

**III. (XXII.) Tribe. Ctenophora. Comb-jellyfish.**

§ 597. Body egg-shaped, sometimes band-shaped, with inner large stomach cavity and wide mouth opening which is directed forward in movement; opposite it a second opening, from which the swallowed water flows off. Around the mouth or stomach base a vascular ring, from which main trunks spread at equal distances on the outside of the body; other vessels by contrast run on the side of the stomach and open likewise into the ring; both connected by fine branches. On the surface rows of fine swimming lobes, which are in constant motion; but no swimming cavities, air bladders or tentacles, by contrast in many lobes or fishing threads, now beside the mouth, now at the hind end. Egg sacs Grant first found as 2 masses formed of red granules beside the stomach; their exits Mertens followed into the cavities from which the fishing threads come forth.

11. (47.) Fam. **Beroidae.** Body without fishing threads or skin flaps, merely with the 8 rows of small swimming lobes. Stomach cavity closed behind, mouth very wide; opposite it 2 warts, on which swimming lobes. The vascular ring around the pharynx, from which 8 vessels spring; on the stomach according to Mertens 2 vascular appendages.

Beroe. The swimming-lobe rows are free and stick in no skin fold; they themselves, if shorter than the space between them; of the 8 rows 2 and 2 are approximated to each other. — B. punctata, against 4″ long, **[p. 464]** flat-round, the 4 rows of swimming lobes on the broad side shorter than those on the narrow, all going out from the hind end. In the Atlantic Ocean.

Gen.: Medea, Pandora (the rows in sunken folds).

Probably the genus Noctiluca Surr. Lam. (Slabberia Oken, Mammaria Tiles. Ehr., Physematium Meyen) also belongs to this family, body globular, bladdery, with simple mouth, from which radially branched canals go out over the whole surface; beside the mouth a tube rolling spirally, set with curled fishing threads (? bristle!). In the interior of the bladder sometimes a red granular mass is visible, which one takes for the egg receptacle. — N. miliaris (Mamm. scintillans aut.), microscopic, the bladder clear, kidney-shaped, the yellow mouth in the notch; in all seas by millions, shines very strongly, and is the usual cause of the sea-glow.

12. (48.) Fam. **Mnemiadae.** Body without fishing threads, but usually with small processes at the mouth opening, and large lobes running out from the hind end, on which, as on the body, the rows of swimming lobes. The stomach has the hind exit and the ring vessel lies at its hind end, embracing the exit.

Mnemia. At the hind end 2 main lobes, on which 4 swimming-lobe rows and 4 others on the body; beside these 2 lancet-shaped processes, which arise beside the mouth as threads. — Mn. Schweigeri (Alcinoe vermiculata Rang), 2″ long, bluish, the ribs shimmering in rainbow colours; the surface set with dark granules. In the bay of Rio Janeiro.

Calymma. Like the preceding, but the main lobes without swimming-lobe rows, these only on the 4 free, thick threads, which likewise spring from the hind end, sit under the lobes, and project toward the mouth. — C. Trevirani, the only known species; as large as a duck egg, flat-round. South Sea.

Gen.: Axiotima, Eucharis.

13. (49.) Fam. **Callianiridae.** Like the preceding, but the body has only 2 long branched fishing threads, which come forth from 2 canals running down beside the stomach, open behind.

Cydippe. Body egg-shaped or globular, without lateral wing processes; fishing threads simple, with fine side branches. — C. pileus, globular, bluish, the fishing threads white; diameter of the body 1″. North Sea. (Cf. Grant in Transact. of the zool. soc. Lond. 4. I. P.)

Cestum. Body band-shaped, the swimming-lobe rows on the long **[p. 465]** edges, the mouth opening on the upward-turned narrow side. Fishing threads simple, with short side branchlets. — C. Veneris, equally thick, the lower edge simple; 4′ long, 3½″ high. Mediterranean. — C. Najades, middle thickened, on the lower edge a folded double skin seam. South Sea.

Gen.: Callianira (like Cydippe, but winged and the fishing threads branched).

**Second (10.) Order. Echinodermata *Lam.***

§ 598. The globular, disc- or star-shaped body has a calcareous scaffold, which is enveloped by soft skin and whose pieces are connected by it. The lime masses lie, then, partly under the surface, forming a firm shell, partly they penetrate into the substance too, and present a scaffold composed of like pieces, in which cavities remain for the organs. In both cases the surface is usually clothed with mobile calcareous spines of various size. The inner organs are more developed, and follow, like the main lobes of the body, the numerical law five. The mouth lies in one family above, in the others below, and has no special tentacles in its surroundings. Inside it is armed in some with 5 teeth. The stomach is either a blind cavity with side pouches, or it sends out an intestine which runs winding through the body; an anus is then present and mostly peripheral. Separate respiratory organs are lacking; the water enters through suction tubes in the surroundings of the mouth into the interior of the body and washes the blood vessels directly. These go out, like the nervous system, from the pharyngeal ring. The reproductive organs mere ovaries, which lie 1 or 2 each in the 5 main lobes of the body, and open with 5 or 10 holes in the surroundings of the upper pole. For locomotion of the free mobile ones serve small, stalked, very extensible suckers, called feet, which are distributed over the body in definite rows (ambulacra) and are set in turgor by a peculiar vascular system going out from a central ring. Ehrenberg remarked juice circulation in the stalks of these so extensible disc-shaped feet, also saw in many Asterias species on the tips of the 5 main lobes red points, which he holds for eyes.

**[p. 466]** The echinoderms inhabit the sea, but do not swim, like the jellyfish, but creep slowly along on the bottom; some sit fixed immovably. They love the coasts, especially the flat ones.

Tiedemann, Anatomie der Röhren-Holothurie, des pomeranzenfarbenen Seesterns und Stein-Seeigels. Landshut 1816. Fol. m. K.

**IV. (XXIII.) Tribe. Crinoidea. Hair-stars.**

§ 599. The lime scaffold is internal and consists of infinitely many circular or 5-edged, flat small discs (fossil called trochites or nummulites), which are connected by soft skin. They form in the middle of the 5-rayed star a bowl-shaped cavity in which the inner organs lie hidden. The rays of the body are once or several times dichotomously branched, always rejuvenated, hence the discs become smaller. These are perforated in the middle, for the passage of the vessels.

14. (50.) Fam. **Encrinoidea.** Body attached, carried on a stalk which springs from the middle of the body, and sits fast below on the rock, immovable. The last member of the stalk bears 5 flat lime plates, on which 3 rows of similar plates follow; they form the head or the body; the 5 plates of the last row bear the arms. Above the bowl is closed by soft skin.

The numerous fossil forms are treated by J. S. Millar, a natural history of Crinoidea. Brist. 1821. 4.; — living there are still the genera: Encrinus Lam., stalk 5-edged, everywhere equally thick, the bowl pieces connected by jointing, mobile, the arms formed from a simple row of joints. Stalk with simple tendrils, set in whorls at intervals. — E. caput Medusae (Isis asterias Linn.), arms 3–5 times forked, stalk 1′ high, star over 1′ diameter. Antillean sea. — Pentacrinus, stalk round, simple, but from the bowl 5 whorl-shaped jointed tendrils. P. europaeus, arms forked, feathered; ¾″ high; on sertularians in the harbour of Cork. (Heusinger's Ztschr. f. d. o. Phys. II. 55.)

15. (51.) Fam. **Comatulina.** Like the preceding, but no stalk, in its place many jointed tendrils going out from the middle of the bowl, with which the animal holds itself fast to rocks. Only genus:

Comatula. Arms once forked, prickly feathered; some of the tendrils **[p. 467]** laid around the body form fishing arms before the mouth. — C. mediterranea, blood-red or flesh-red, on the underside of each arm a skin fold in which the knob-shaped, very contractile feet. Mediterranean. (Cf. Heusinger's Ztschr. f. d. o. Phys. III. 366.)

The fossil genus Marsupites, not furnished with tendrils, sets up a family of its own, leading over to the following tribe.

**V. (XXIV.) Tribe. Sea-stars. Asteroidea.**

§ 600. Without stalk or tendrils; the animals creep slowly along on the bottom by means of the feet, which take up the middle of the arms on the underside. No anus, so a simple sac-shaped stomach with side pouches. Mouth without tooth apparatus, the lime covering not internal, formed merely of flat, free or grown-together lime plates.

16. (52.) Fam. **Euryalidae.** The arms thin, round, above scaly, below with shields, beside them the feet; at the base of the arms on each side a cleft, through which the water enters the body cavity; five ovaries, whose opening is not yet observed.

Euryale. (Gorgonocephalus Leach.) Arms several times forked, on the underside with lateral spines in transverse rows and shields on the edge, between which in the middle the feet. Several species in tropical seas, e.g. E. verrucosa, disc broad, on the back with wrinkled radiating ribs; the arms below flat, warty in double rows, the warts small. East Indies.

Ophiura. Arms unbranched, sit freely on the 5-edged body, below with simple or several shield rows, hence the feet on the edge, beside them spines in transverse rows. — O. lacertosa, arms round, smooth, very long; the feet short, pressed on. On the coasts of Europe. — O. fragilis, arms flat, combed with spines on the edge; back of the disc prickly. North Sea.

17. (53.) Fam. **Asteridae.** Body star-shaped, the arms indistinctly separated from the disc, hollow, for taking up the ovaries; on the middle of the underside the feet in several rows, between them short tubes which suck in water. Back side warty, rough; the belly side on the edge of the arms with plates, the edge prickly. Only genus:

Asterias, whose numerous species are found in all seas, e.g.: A. aurantiaca, disc broad 5-rayed, flat; back with prickly warts, the edge jointed and prickly. Colour orange. Coasts **[p. 468]** of Europe. — A. papposa, usually 13-rayed, on the back and on the edge with tufted spines; colour brownish. There.

**VI. (XXV.) Tribe. Sea-urchins. Echinodea.**

§ 601. Body globular or hemispherical, sometimes very flat-arched, with calcareous, wholly closed shell under the skin, which leaves free only the mouth, anus, egg exits and the stalked feet. The shell consists of 10 main rows of grown-together 5-edged shields, and is humpy outside, smooth inside. On the humps articulate lime spines which are held fast by the covering skin. The stalked feet stand mostly in double or 4-fold rows (ambulacra), and run either from pole to pole, or form 5 short, in themselves closed, strips on the back surface, in whose middle lies a lime plate perforated by the anus or the 5–10 ovary openings. The inner organisation is marked by the presence of a 5-toothed chewing apparatus, a mostly long winding intestine, and the position of the 10 paired suction tubes around the mouth opening. The anus does not fall to all; where it is found, it lies either in the upper pole, or in the zone, or on the edge, indeed on the underside of the cone, in which case the mouth too appears peripheral. By this position the regular form gradually passes into the symmetrical, as Agassiz first remarked (Isis 1834, p. 254), and then the mouth marks the front, the anus the hind end. The sea urchins are likewise found in all seas and grow very slowly, in which the new lime plates form around the anus; their food is small molluscs.

18. (54.) Fam. **Ganymedidae.** They have no anus.

Ganymedes. Without ambulacra, in the upper pole, opposite the mouth a 4-edged pit, mouth 5-edged; on the surface pits in which the spines. — G. pulchella, ⅛″ diameter. On the coast of Kent. (Gray in Lond. and Edinb. phil. magaz. and journ. of sc. p. 74.)

As fossil form belongs here: Glenotremites Goldf.

19. (55.) Fam. **Echinidae.** With large free anal opening and meridian-like ambulacra; the mouth opening wide, narrowed by soft skin, and on it tentacles.

Echinus. Body globular, quite regular, the mouth in the upper pole; the humps of the shell small, without navel in the middle. — E. esculentus, **[p. 469]** shell circular, hemispherical, pointed above, weakly warty; the spines pointed and short. North Sea.

Echinoneus. Body symmetrical, more flat; mouth below in the middle, in its neighbourhood the anus. — E. semilunaris, oblong egg-shaped, mouth obliquely transverse; on the vertex 4 ovary openings. Antillean sea.

Living gen.: Cidarites, Nucleolithes; fossil: Galerites, Ananchytes.

20. (56.) Fam. **Spatangidae.** Body flatter, with sharp edge, more or less symmetrical; humps and spines very small. The ambulacra form 5 elliptical or lancet-shaped figures in star-shaped position on the middle of the back; they are unequal, the single one stands then over the mouth, the 2 and 2 equal ones laterally beside it, so that the anus falls into the space between the two hinder ones.

Clypeaster, still rather arched, the underside hollowed; mouth in the middle, anus on the edge. — Cl. rosaceus, egg-shaped, blunt in front, bulging out above the anus; ambulacra very broad. In America.

Scutella. Body flat; with sharp edge, sometimes notched there; ambulacra wholly closed, flower-shaped; mouth in the middle, anus beside the edge. — Sc. dentata, circular, middle field notched, toothed on the hind edge. Indian Ocean.

Spatangus. Body egg-shaped, rather arched. Mouth toothless, transverse, with lips, approximated to the front end, anus toward the hind end. — Sp. purpuraceus, heart-shaped, 4 lancet-shaped ambulacra; shell with humps in zigzag. North Sea. — Sp. atropos, short, egg-shaped, 5 narrow ambulacra, the front ones very deeply pressed in. In the Canal.

Gen.: Cassidula, Fibularia.

**Third (11.) Order. Star-worms. Scytodermata \*). Holothuriae *autor.***

§ 602. Body seldom globular, mostly long stretched, cylindrical, sometimes articulated as if by constrictions, covered by firm, tough, leathery skin; mouth at the front end, anus at the hind one or on the belly. Surface various, now smooth, now ringed, most frequently humpy, rough, sometimes with some lime substance in the skin. The mouth end more or less protrusible, with simple or feathered also star-shaped retractile tentacles in definite number (5, 8, 10, 20), or instead of them many small, short, **[p. 470]** changeable warts. Not all genera have the extensible stalked suction discs or feet of the previous order, but under the skin a very developed musculature, which consists of 5 or more band-like longitudinal muscles, which fix themselves at the 5 lime teeth, as in the surroundings of the anus, and a second, outer layer formed from transverse ring fibres. The inner organisation shows a long winding nutritive canal, whose entrance is usually surrounded by 5 calcareous teeth, and which at a second place perforates the skin. It is accompanied by blood vessels which stand in connection with those coming from the respiratory organ, through a pharyngeal ring. For the feet there is a second, wholly separate one, which is destined for their extension. The respiratory organ is not always present; where it occurs, it is a grape-shaped cavity which opens into the widened end of the intestine, and through the anus takes water in and pushes it out. — The sexual organs lie more in the front part of the body and open freely on the back, a stretch behind the mouth; all show various folded egg sacs, into whose long exit several (1, 2, 10, 15, 22) small glandular organs, the testes (?), open. The species in all seas on the coast, many stick in the sand. — Cf. G. F. Jaeger, de Holothuriis. Turici 1833. 4. — J. F. Brandt in recueil des actes de la séance publique de l'acad. imp. des scienc. de St. Pet. 1835.

**VII. (XXVI.) Tribe. Pedata.**

§ 603. On the surface of the body the stalked suction discs or feet show themselves, which serve for locomotion, but in different form and position. Most possess a large branched respiratory organ, which lies partly free in the body, partly is held fast by a mesentery-like skin fold. All have a thick leather skin and retractile tentacles.

21. (57.) Fam. **Pentactidae.** The quite equally formed feet stand in 5 or 6 longitudinal rows from mouth to anus at equal distances from one another on the cylindrical body.

Here the gen. Pentactes (Hol. pent. Müll.) and Cucumaria Less. Tentacles various, in some branched feathered (Chladodactyla Br., C. crocea Less., Hol. Gärtneri Blainv.), in others simply feathered (Dactylota Br., Hol. laevis Fabr.), in still others shield-shaped **[p. 471]** (Aspidochir Br.); finally oblong-linear, warty on the outside (Oncinolabes Br., has no respiratory organ).

22. (58.) Fam. **Holothuridae.** The feet are irregularly distributed over the body, but more frequent on the belly edge than on the back. Those of the back have very usually no disc, but only a short stalk coming out of a wart.

a. All feet of equal form, with a suction disc.

Sporadipus Br. Body cylindrical, thick; feet very numerous; 20 shield-shaped tentacles. Anus round. — H. peruviana Less.

b. The back feet conical and warty, without disc, less numerous than the sucker-shaped stalked belly feet. These belly feet stand partly in 3 (Stichopus Br.) or 5 (Diplopeiderus Br.) rows, partly irregularly, as in:

Holothuria. Tentacles shield-shaped; body long, round, with flattened belly side and round anus, the back almost always warty. Here many species; e.g. H. tubulosa, H. elegans, H. impatiens, H. ananas, H. monacaria and others.

Bohadschia, like the preceding, but the anus star-shaped.

Mülleria, likewise, but the anus with 5 teeth, on which the 5 longitudinal muscles sit.

Trepang. Body round, 6–8 shield-shaped tentacles; feet scattered on the belly. — T. edulis, in China, is eaten.

23. (59.) Fam. **Psolidae.** The stalked feet stand only on the flat belly side; body thick, arched above, narrowed in front and behind; the skin very hard, rough. Tentacles branched.

Psolus. Skin soft, wrinkly. — H. phantopus Müller.

Cuvieria. Body likewise covered by calcareous scales; respiratory organ double. — H. squamata autor., on the coasts of Europe.

**VIII. (XXVII.) Tribe. Apoda.**

§ 604. The surface of the body shows no stalked suction discs, but is either quite naked, or covered only with tentacles, warts or spines at the mouth or elsewhere. Most seem to lack a peculiar respiratory organ; many resemble, on account of the thin, apparently ringed body form, the worms, and gladly burrow in the mud of the coast, like these.

24. (60.) Fam. **Cucumarina.** Body globular, with meridian furrows, between which almost horny warts; at both poles an **[p. 472]** opening, the upper the mouth, surrounded by 3 circles of round, worm-shaped, short tentacles.

Minyas, only gen. M. cyanea, blue; atlant. Ocean.

25. (61.) Fam. **Synaptidae.** Body cylindrical, smooth or warty; tentacles around the mouth.

a. With distinct, branched respiratory organ.

Chiridota. Body smooth, worm-shaped, 15–20 shield-shaped tentacles; respiratory organ consists of small cylindrical, above split tubes. — Ch. discolor, Ch. verrucosa and others. Gen.: Liosoma, Lithoderma (if here?)

b. Without respiratory organ.

Synapta. Tentacles simply feathered; body worm-shaped, with fine skin and small hooks. — S. mamillosa, South Sea. S. oceanica and others.

26. (62.) Fam. **Siphunculina.** Body round, fine in the transverse, equally thick or thickened in front. No tentacles and lime teeth around the mouth.

Priapulus. Body equally thick, in front with an elliptical, furrowed process, in which the mouth; at the hind end the anus, from which comes a grape-shaped organ whose use is unknown. Intestine as long as the body. Pharynx set with small teeth in rows. — Pr. caudatus, 2–3″ long. North Sea.

Siphunculus. Body club-shaped, in front a thinner, cylindrical process set with soft warts, which can be drawn in, soon behind it the openings of the 2 ovaries. Anus behind the mouth, on the belly, intestine wound in the body; no respiratory organ. — S. laevis, 1′ long, finger-thick. Mediterranean, in sandstone coasts \*).

27. (63.) Fam. **Talassemidae.** Body round, not ringed, in front with cone- or spoon-shaped mouth, without tentacles and teeth; surface everywhere smooth, partly with bristles. No respiratory organ, rather the water enters through openings in the body cavity and washes the organs freely.

Gen.: Bonellia (Isis 1823, 398). — Echiurus Cuv. (Isis 1818, 878.) — Talassema (Pall. spic. zool. X. 8. t. 1. f. 6.). — Sternaspis (Otto de Sternasp. Vratisl. 1820. 4. Thalassema scutatum Eisenh. Isis 1818, 2080. Taf 26.).

Although all 4 genera are still insufficiently known, **[p. 473]** the descriptions adduced nevertheless show a true kinship with the Holothuriae, but not with the annelids, clearly enough.
