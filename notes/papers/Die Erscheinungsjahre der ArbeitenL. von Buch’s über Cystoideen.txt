Bucn's preliminary communication appeared in the "Report on the Proceedings of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin Suitable for Publication. From the Year 1844", pp. 120 to 133. The title of this outline agrees with that of the subsequent treatise {apart from some insignificant orthographic variations}, except that the title in the "Report" ends with the words "of Caryocrinus ornatus", whereas the formulation in the treatise, as already mentioned above, is "of Caryocrinus ornatus SAY". A diagnosis of cystoids (which, admittedly, later underwent significant improvement, particularly through Jaekel) already appears in the "Report." From the facts presented, Dr. Stubblefield believes he can conclude: 1. that the separately paginated version does not represent a reprint, but rather a preprint of the work published in the "Abhandlungen" of the Berlin Academy, and 2. that no proof has yet been provided to me that this version, whose title page bears the year 1845, was actually published in 1845 and not 1846.

Assuming the first assumption is correct, which should be difficult to deny, the second point can really only be considered of theoretical interest. For in the present case, one cannot definitively assert that the separately paginated special edition is a "publication." Were the preprints available on the open market? Hardly. It is much more likely that they were reserved for Buch's private correspondents. The "legal" status of preprints has been clearly illuminated by Rudolfrichter (1948, 141) with the following words:

"Previews, that is, reprints of journal articles and individual chapters, are only considered publications if they appear independently and for sale in bookstores; not, however, if they are only accessible to a select few before the publication of the journal or the entire book. All such prints do not constitute publications, but merely reprinted manuscripts. The essential characteristic of a publication is therefore not the technology, but its general, impartial accessibility. This is realized as soon as the printed work is available for purchase by everyone in the general book trade."

When citing BvcH's main work on cystoids, the preprint must therefore be disregarded, and 1846 should be accepted as the year of publication. As already explained above, the priority of the year 1844 onwards does not affect the concept of cystoids.

BvcH's other contributions to the knowledge of cystoids can be summarized briefly. His interest in these organisms seems to have been awakened in the years before 1840 during the processing of Russian fossil material (which, incidentally, also led to the establishment of the eocrinoid genus Cryptocrinites) and is documented in particular by communications to learned societies as well as by letters to colleagues published later. These are of limited scope and should not pose any bibliographical difficulties. However, the treatise "Contributions to the Determination of the Mountain Formations in Rügen" is also available in preprint (1840) and printed edition (1841).

Appendix: Name form Cystidea or Cystoidea?

The higher taxonomical categories do not necessarily fall under the priority law [see R. Richter 1948, 85 to 86. In the "Copenhagen Decisions on Zoological Nomenclature", ed by F. Hwmmino, London 1953, it is proposed (paragraph 64) that the order/class group should also be regulated by the priority principle, but only after the entry into force of the new rules of zoological nomenclature. This would be preceded by the establishment of a list of suitable order and class names, taking into account, among other things, general usage, by specially selected specialists (paragraph 62, 1).] Therefore, it is not a conditio sine qua non that a name must bear the form that was given to it by the originator of the The scientific term was given. Therefore, the principle "Quod scripture, scripture" does not apply here, as it does for groups of herbivores and species. Accordingly, a change in the form of the name for purely philological-orthographic reasons is permissible. Incidentally, in the present case, Buca is the scientific but not the nomenclatural author, since he did not create a formally valid name for the organisms in question, but always refers to them as "cystides," never as "Cystidea" or the like. Anyone searching the literature for orthographic variants will find a rich yield.
The following Latinized forms occur: Cystideae [Forbes 1848], Cystidea [HALL 1847 (Palaeontol. of New York, 1), BILLINGS 1854 and later, ANGELIN 1878, BATHER 1899 and later, CUÉNOT 1948, 1953, among others. m.], Cystoidea [NICHOLSON 1879 (Manual of Palaeontol.), ZITTEL 1879 (Handb. d. Palaeontol., I, 1), JAEKEL 1895 and later, STEINMANN 1903 (Einfiführung in d. Palaeontol.), Springer 1918 (in ZITTEL-EASTMAN), ABEL 1920 (Lehrb. d. Paläozool.), ZITTEL-BROILI 1926, BASSLER 1938 (Foss. Catal. I, 83) and later, CHAUVEL 1941, REGNÉLL 1945 and later, MOORE, LALICKER & FISCHER 1952 (Invertebr. fossils), SHROCK & TWENHOFEL 1953 (Principles of Invertebr. Palaeontol.) et al. m.], Cystoidia [DELAGE & HÉROUARD 1908]. In addition, the following forms are found: Cystideen, Cystoide (Germanized); Cystideans, Cystids, Cystoids (Anglicized); Cystidês, Cystidées, Cystoïdes (Gallicized); Cistidee (Italianized); Cistideos (Hispanicized), and possibly others.

Among the Latinized forms, only "Cystidea" and "Cystoidea" can be considered as designations for a category of the order/class group. However, as Dr. S. LUNDSTRUM (Lurid) kindly informed me, unfortunately neither form is perfect in a spradalid sense; the former is even incorrect. Forming the name from the Greek *kystis*, bladder, and *eidos*, form, shape, one obtains—by adding the compound element to the (transcribed) stem *cysti-*—either "Cysti-idea" (a contraction of the double *i* would be contrary to the rule here) or "Cysti-o-idea," forms that have never been used in palionological literature. An inflection occurs only exceptionally, which presupposes a stem ending in *-id*. A soldaer gives the form "Cystid-o-idea", from which, theoretically, the name "Cystoidea" could arise through haplology. However, another possibility presents itself, namely the derivation of the first element from grieda, kyste. This word means "sponge bread" (a term unknown to me). The meanings "bladder" and "cup" are further, although schwada, lexicographisda, are attested, but must be considered suspect. Nevertheless, the form "Cyst-o-idea" can be regularly formed from it, the form that (with corresponding nationalized forms) has become by far the most common in specialist and handbook literature—not least from later years. Finally, it should be noted that the spelling "Cystoidea" can not only be justified philologisda, but also has the advantage of being formally analogous to the names of the other main groups ~ Blastoidea, Carpoidea, Crinoidea etc.
