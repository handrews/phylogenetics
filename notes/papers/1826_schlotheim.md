Description of some illustrated species of Echinosphaerites and Trilobites. Plate J.

Undoubtedly, it is the duty of every collector of scientific objects, who aims at higher purposes and does not merely wish to boast about and play with his rarities, to bring everything that is not yet properly known and sufficiently investigated to public attention. This seems particularly necessary in the case of fossil collections, where so frequently new, enigmatic creatures of the primeval world are found, which not only facilitate the closer knowledge and determination of the geological strata in relation to their relative age, but also make us acquainted with extinct members of the great chain of beings, which have, as it were, disappeared, and which now, upon closer examination and scrutiny, seem to fill in the missing links in that infinite series. Since time and circumstances do not currently allow me to write extensive treatises on such subjects, I may, from time to time, publish some such communications and observations in Isis, in order to encourage further investigation of them, and thereby elicit information and clarification from other proven naturalists, which might otherwise have been withheld from us for even longer. First, I want to begin with the Echinosphaerites, which recently received their very fitting name from Wahlenberg, and belong to a most remarkable family that seems to connect the numerous genus of Echinites, through Miller's Marsupites, and through the rare Ecrin. florealis of Petref. K. Parkins, organ. rem. Vol. II. T. 13. f. 36. 37. Encrinites Godoni Defrancii, with the Encrinites, and consequently with the aforementioned Comatula, as intermediate links.* Some beautiful specimens in my collection of this rare Encrin. floreal. from Huntsville in North America, and from the Mendips, Gloucestershire, show great similarity in their external form, and especially in the structure of their rays, to the Echinites, which is why several naturalists, and even Miller, have not unjustly classified them with the Echinites; but the arrangement of their plates, and the stalk-like extension of the anus, again brings them extraordinarily close to the Encrinites. It should therefore not surprise us at all if we find stalked Echinites among the animals of the primeval world, just as we have already become acquainted with stalked starfish through the Encrinites, which, through the Marsupites, and through the so-called unstalked Encrinites with short, tapering arms (Miller's Crinoid. p. 134), closely resemble the Echinosphaerites due to the structure of their plates and their already very spherical shape, especially since the anus of the latter seems to be connected, in part, with a nutritional and excretory canal running through the entire animal, entirely in the manner of the Encrinites.

The Echinosphaerites, although already mentioned early on by Walch in Naturforscher 8, p. 259, Tab. 5, fig. 1, 2, under the name Östracion, and cited and illustrated by Gyllenhal in Vet. Acad. Handl. 1772, by Tilas, and more recently by Hisinger in the same publications, as a peculiar type of echinoid, are still little known overall and have not yet been sufficiently studied or described with regard to their various species discovered so far. The most important treatise on this subject was provided by Wahlenberg in act. reg. Soc. scient. Upsal. Vol. VIII, p. 52, who also gave them their current very appropriate name. He lists three species: E. granatum, aurantium, and pomum, and says the following in general about this family:

The detailed view and description sufficiently confirm my previously stated assertion that they constitute a remarkable intermediate link, a transition from one family to another. Although they do show a great similarity to the Alcyonians, especially in some species, their other characteristics make them fundamentally different, and in their fossilized form they exhibit a calcite shell perfectly similar to that of the echinoids, which, unfortunately, is all too easily damaged when being extracted from its very hard matrix rock. Therefore, only very rarely are specimens found that still retain the mouth and anal openings. To date, they have only been found in mountain strata of the transitional formation in Sweden, Norway, the area around Reval, and on the island of Ösel, and well-preserved specimens are always among the greatest rarities. If the specimen depicted in Fig. 6, which was found in Mecklenburg and apparently originates from Cretaceous deposits, really has to be classified as an Echinosphaerite, then we would indeed have a member of this family from the youngest sedimentary formations, which, however, also differs sufficiently from its older relatives.

After these preliminary remarks, I will now proceed to a more detailed description of the individual species. I owe the communication of this beautifully preserved Echinosphaerites for the purpose of illustration to the exceptional kindness of Dr. Buch in Frankfurt. According to Wahlenberg, it is said to occur mainly on Öland, and near Wikarby and Furudal in Dalecarlia, but it almost seems as if the present specimen originated from the island of Ösel.

Whether it is truly Echinosph. granatum Wahlenberg, as claimed, I cannot state with complete certainty, as I have not been able to compare it with the illustration provided by Hisinger; however, his more detailed description makes it highly probable. It differs from the other species mainly in its pear-shaped form and in the protruding corners of several of its plates, which indeed give it some resemblance to a garnet crystal, and hence its name. The center of the plates, from which the fine rays radiate to the side surfaces, is slightly raised, thus forming the aforementioned corners. Its mouth opening, consisting of numerous very small, elongated openings, through which the animal most likely extended its arms or suction threads, forms an elongated quadrilateral and is located close to the stalk-shaped anal end. Fig. a. is a side view, Fig. b. from below towards the anus, and Fig. c. from above, where the remarkable Encrinite-like curvature,
in the form of a single trochite, with a small apparent opening in the middle, becomes visible, which certainly seems to form a nutritional canal extending to the anus. This phenomenon deserves a continued and very thorough investigation, and will probably only be sufficiently clarified upon the discovery of several suitable specimens. For this reason, one might doubt whether it should not be classified among the Encrinites; however, its mouth opening would decidedly assign it its proper place among the Echinospherites.

Echinosphaerites Alcyonium Fig. 2.

From the transitional clay slate mountains of the area around Christiania in Norway, and especially from their subordinate limestone layers. It is usually filled internally with small calcite druses and occurs in various sizes. Its entire surface is composed of small, irregularly five-sided, slightly convex plates, each with a small, often barely noticeable, button-like elevation in the center, which appear completely smooth to the naked eye; and only under strong magnification do faint rays radiating from the center become visible. Unfortunately, neither the anus nor the mouth opening is visible in even the best of my specimens, and both were probably destroyed when the piece, which is damaged on one side, was extracted. I owe this specimen, along with several other interesting fossils, to the kindness of Professor Esmark. By several authors, it was considered to be Echinosphaer. Aurantium Wahlenberg, with which, however, the illustration cited by the latter in volume 8, p. 259, T. 5, f. 1. 2. of the "Naturforscher" does not properly correspond. From younger transitional limestone of the Reval area, also damaged on the posterior side, but with a preserved rounded anal opening, which is located in the middle of the shield closing at the pointed end. The probably damaged oral opening is not visible. Of completely ovoid shape, with relatively large, almost fused shields, whose sutures, as well as the five main rays radiating from the center, are covered with flatly arched, slightly protruding ribs, between which finer rays spread from the center. The shields are sometimes irregular, sometimes slightly larger and smaller; and while they generally appear five-sided, six-sided ones also appear due to apparent bending of the ribs. Sometimes there are also 6 main rays that are stronger and more rib-like, and often in some specimens the finer intermediate rays are hardly noticeable.

Echinosphaerites ovum.

Also of ovoid shape, and extraordinarily similar to the preceding one, only the shields are smaller, without protruding rib-like main rays, and more finely striated, so that its striation is often only visible through a magnifying glass. The anal opening, as in the preceding ones, is located in the middle of the last shield at the pointed end. Although the present specimen is completely preserved except for insignificant small areas, and is only slightly abraded in a few places, no trace of the oral opening could be detected. It has also been found in the younger transitional limestone of the Reval region.

It occurs frequently in Sweden, and in almost all areas of the transitional formation, but is very rarely found in a well-preserved state. Only at Kinnekulle in West Gothland are complete specimens occasionally found. The specimen from my collection, pictured here on page 5, figure a, is from Laby in Sweden and is well-preserved, therefore it corresponds perfectly with Wahlenberg's description. He says, for example, "it is completely round, with a slightly prominent beak, and therefore it best represents a small apple. The surface is rough and punctate, but hardly inscribed with lines, much less marked with any raised ridges. The mouth and anus are located on a common tubercle and almost merge. All the features of this species are less expressed than in the preceding ones, and therefore it seems to constitute only the beginning of the organization of the Echinoideae."

While I do not intend to dispute or invalidate Wahlenberg's observations and determinations with the following remarks, I cannot refrain from mentioning a conjecture suggested to me by the Echinosphaerite depicted in Fig. 5. b. It belongs to the completely preserved specimens from the Reval area and almost entirely matches E. pomum in terms of form. At first glance, most specimens appear only rough, without any visible plates or the usual rays; however, upon closer inspection, especially with sufficient magnification, the plates and rays become fully visible. The plates appear as small, elongated quadrangles with extraordinarily fine rays, which always emanate from the center of four such adjoining quadrangles; but it seems very likely that the tip of one is always somewhat blunted and then forms the second side. In the same way, a small, elongated quadrangle consisting of numerous rounded depressions, similar to the mouth opening of Echinosph. granatum, is visible near the anus through the magnifying glass, and perhaps this is also its mouth opening. Of course, the dots are very small and easily overlooked, and only by comparing several specimens could it be determined whether one is subject to an illusion here, or whether this observation is entirely correct. Since several Echinosphaerites are often more or less covered with Escharites, so-called sea crusts, as is also the case with E. pomum; It is possible that its delicate surface was mistakenly considered punctate, and the very fine scales and rays were easily overlooked. However, should its described characteristics be consistently confirmed, as indicated by Wahlenberg, then Figure 5, b. would constitute a distinct new species and would require a separate name.

Fig. 6 shows the previously mentioned, highly problematic, fossilized Echinosphaerite in flint, from Cretaceous marl deposits in Mecklenburg. It retains its fine, regular, shield-like impressions on only one side, while the rest of the surface is more or less abraded. The detailed illustration obviates the need for further description, and only the discovery of more complete specimens will allow us to determine to which family it actually belongs.

Fig. 7 is also too incompletely preserved to allow for a more precise identification and description. The very prominent, rib-like main rays do show differences from E. costatus (Fig. 3), but this fragment could be so worn that, in more complete specimens, it might ultimately prove to be identical to the latter.
