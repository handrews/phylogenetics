The new eocrinoid genus described by Chen and Han (2014) from the Furongian (Upper Cambrian) Guole Formation of Guangxi, South China, is named Guoleeocrinus. [1, 2, 3] 
Taxonomic Classification
The genus is classified within the following hierarchy:

* Phylum: [Echinodermata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eocrinoidea)
* Subphylum: [Blastozoa](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353859583_A_new_eocrinoid_from_the_Guanshan_Biota_Cambrian_Series_2_Stage_4_with_implication_of_the_development_of_different_attachment_modes_in_early_Cambrian)
* Class: Eocrinoidea
* Order: Gogiida [4, 5] 

Morphological Features
Based on the description of the type species, Guoleeocrinus microlepis, the genus is characterized by the following features:

* Theca (Body): An elongated, nearly cylindrical theca that is composed of numerous small, irregularly arranged plates.
* Brachioles (Feeding Arms): It possesses numerous slender brachioles that are arranged in a specific pattern around the oral area to facilitate suspension feeding.
* Stalk and Holdfast: The genus is notable for its long, flexible stalk consisting of multiple columnals, which terminates in a distal holdfast or attachment structure.
* Sutural Pores: Like many early gogiid eocrinoids, it features sutural pores between its thecal plates, which are thought to have functioned as respiratory structures. [3, 6]

* Theca: It has an elongated, nearly cylindrical theca composed of numerous small, irregularly arranged plates.

Yes, that is correct. In the original 2014 publication, Chen and Han explicitly assigned Jingxieocrinus guoleensis to the order Gogiida, while leaving its family-level classification unassigned (effectively treating it as incertae sedis at the family level). [1] 
Taxonomic Placement in the 2014 Paper
The authors utilized the following hierarchy to describe the new genus:

* Class: [Eocrinoidea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eocrinoidea)
* Order: Gogiida
* Family: (Not assigned)
* Genus: Jingxieocrinus (gen. nov.)
* Species: Jingxieocrinus guoleensis (sp. nov.) [1] 

Why the Family was Left Unassigned
The decision to skip a family assignment is common when a new fossil discovery possesses a unique combination of traits that do not perfectly align with existing families like Eocrinidae or Gogiidae. For Jingxieocrinus, the key characteristics noted by the authors include:

* An elongated, nearly cylindrical theca.
* The presence of sutural pores (epispires).
* A long, flexible, and polyplated stalk.

While these features are hallmarks of the order Gogiida, the specific arrangement of plates and the nature of the attachment structure led the authors to establish the genus without forcing it into a pre-defined family.

In the 2014 paper by Chen and Han, the etymology for both the genus and species names is tied to the specific geographic location where the fossils were discovered.
Genus: Jingxieocrinus

* Origin: The name is derived from [Jingxi County](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/08094d_&q=Yes+please), located in the Guangxi Province of South China.
* Components: It combines "Jingxi" (the locality) with the suffix "-ocrinus", a standard suffix used in the nomenclature of eocrinoids and crinoids, originating from the Greek word krinon, meaning "lily" (referencing their flower-like appearance). [1] 

Species: Jingxieocrinus guoleensis

* Origin: The species name is derived from the Guole Formation (and the associated Guole area) where the type specimens were collected.
* Components: It combines "Guole" with the Latin suffix "-ensis", which means "from" or "belonging to" a specific place. [2, 3, 4] 

Together, the name translates roughly to "The lily-like animal from the Guole [Formation] of Jingxi."

In the Guole Formation (specifically the Guole Biota), Jingxieocrinus guoleensis lived as part of a diverse marine ecosystem. This site is significant as the first Furongian (Upper Cambrian) Burgess Shale-type biota discovered, filling a major gap in the fossil record of soft-bodied preservation. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
The fauna found alongside Jingxieocrinus includes:
Shelly and Benthic Fauna [4] 

* [Trilobites](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0gs3g&q=yes+please): These are the most common fossils in the formation. One notable find is an injured specimen of Shergoldia laevigata.
* [Brachiopods](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0bvdq&q=yes+please): A rich "Billingsella-Palaeostrophia Fauna" exists here, including genera such as Billingsella, Palaeostrophia, Plectotrophia, and the new genus Guoleella.
* Other Echinoderms: In addition to eocrinoids, well-preserved specimens of homalozoans (carpoids) and [stylophorans](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/02pl8cc&q=yes+please) have been recovered.
* [Hyolithids](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0h_b1w1&q=yes+please): Small, conical-shelled marine animals. [4, 5, 6, 7] 

Soft-Bodied and Exceptionally Preserved Organisms [1] 

* [Graptolites](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0gtjl&q=yes+please): Specifically early [benthic graptolites](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871174X22000154) (Graptolithina).
* Worms: Fragmentary remains of [priapulids](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0bvk2&q=yes+please) and other "soft-bodied" vermiform organisms.
* [Arthropods](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=/m/0zkm&q=yes+please): Various non-trilobite arthropods, some showing traces of soft-tissue preservation typical of Lagerstätte sites.
* Algae: Non-calcified algal remains are also present, indicating a productive photic zone environment. [1, 2, 8, 9] 

The environment is interpreted as a nearshore, shallow-water benthic regime with a calcareous muddy substrate, though some deeper-water elements are also present in the biota. [6, 10]

The palaeogeographic connection between the South China Block (where the Guole Biota is found) and Laurentia (North America) during the Cambrian is a subject of significant research, as these regions share surprising faunal similarities despite their distance. [1, 2] 
1. The Biogeographic Link
While South China and Laurentia were geographically separated, they shared several key characteristics:

* The North China Craton (NCC) as a Bridge: Recent evidence suggests the North China Craton may have acted as a "biogeographic link" between Laurentia and East Gondwanan blocks (including South China).
* Ocean Currents: Low-latitude, westward-moving ocean currents are believed to have facilitated the migration of marine larvae—including those of eocrinoids like Jingxieocrinus—between these major paleocontinents.
* Tectonic Archipelago: A newly formed archipelago of volcanic islands and offshore terranes likely served as "stepping stones" for biological exchange during the Furongian (Late Cambrian). [3, 4, 5] 

2. Shared Biological Features
The Guole Biota is particularly famous because it is a Burgess Shale-type (BST) Lagerstätte. This type of exceptional preservation was previously thought to be almost exclusive to Laurentia (e.g., the Burgess Shale in Canada and the Great Basin in the USA). [6, 7] 

* Faunal Overlap: Both regions hosted diverse assemblages of Gogiid eocrinoids, trilobites, and soft-bodied organisms like priapulid worms and early arthropods.
* Evolutionary Synchronicity: Major extinction and radiation events, such as those affecting trilobites at the end of the Early Cambrian, appear to have been globally synchronous across both South China and Laurentia, suggesting they were part of a connected global marine system. [2, 8, 9, 10, 11] 

3. Environmental Similarities
Both the Guole Biota and many Laurentian Cambrian biotas inhabited shallow-marine, low-energy environments near the equator. These warm, nutrient-rich "carbonate platforms" provided ideal conditions for the diversification of stalked echinoderms like Jingxieocrinus. [12, 13, 14]
