On *Pyrgocystis* (*Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen.) *coronaeformis* Rievers from the Rhenish Lower Devonian

By RICHARD DEHM, Munich

Summary

The *Pyrgocystis* specimen from the Hunsrück Slates of Bundenbach (p. 9), originally described by J. Rievers, is examined in greater detail. Based on the distinctive features—specifically a basal sacculus composed of small platelets, the more or less keeled thecal plates, and the slender tube that expands conically towards the top—the establishment of a new subgenus is proposed, with *Pyrgocystis* (*Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen.) *coronaeformis* Rievers designated as the type species; *Pyrgocystis octogona* R. Richter, 1930—from the Lower Devonian greywacke slates of Bornich near Weisel on the Rhine—is closely related.

Introduction

It was not granted to Mr. Johannes Rievers—Retired Senior Postal Inspector in Enkirch on the Moselle—to carry out a detailed investigation of his *Pyrgocystis* specimen from the Bundenbach Slates (p. 9) himself. The specimen’s exceptionally complete preservation—a rarity, unfortunately, among *Pyrgocystis* finds—combined with its superb preparation (partially on both sides) by Mr. Rievers, alone suffices to warrant and justify a closer examination. Added to this are the striking distinctive features that keep alive the question regarding its relationship to the other *Pyrgocystis* species. I would also like to take this opportunity to express my sincere thanks to Ms. Henny Rievers-Blev of Enkirch on the Moselle for kindly making this beautiful specimen available for study.

A. Morphology: Ambulacral Crown, Theca Tower, and Basal Sacs

In the crown-shaped upper part of the animal, the ambulacral region (Pl. 2, Fig. 2) is well preserved, albeit not in every detail. Two ambulacral grooves are visible in their entirety, while two others are represented by a single row of plates each; on the prepared reverse side (Pl. 2, Fig. 3), these two

marginal rows—along with an additional, central groove—are clearly discernible; thus, all five ambulacral grooves, together with their marginal plates, are present. Unfortunately, the compression and indistinct preservation of the small plates within the interambulacral fields make it impossible to determine the anal interradius with certainty. Nevertheless, I would like to attempt at least a provisional orientation of the specimen, designating the central interradius on the upper surface—which is favored in terms of both embedding and preservation—as the anal interradius; based on this assumption, the following features can be identified on this upper surface: to the left, Radius II with a single ambulacral row; next, Radius I with both rows; then the anal interradius, containing several very small platelets; followed by Radius V, again featuring both ambulacral rows; and finally, at the right margin, the single ambulacral row of Radius IV. Correspondingly, on the reverse side of the plate, Radius II is visible in part; Radius III appears somewhat splayed apart; and Radius IV is again visible in part.

The ambulacral crown is directly comparable to that of *Thecocystis sacculus* Jaekel (1899, p. 43, Pl. 1 Figs. 1a, 1b) from the Lower Silurian of Cincinnati, Ohio; the paired marginal plates are very robust and tumid in form; their number is 12—perhaps 13—just as in *Thecocystis* (according to the illustration). On radii V and I, one can discern—in places and in regular repetition—small pits, tubercles, and ridges on the ambulacrals; these features, the precise nature of which remains unclear, constitute part of the fine structure of the ambulacral grooves. Due to displacement, it is no longer possible to determine whether the ambulacrals within a given radius were arranged alternately or oppositely. The length of a single ambulacrum, measured along its outer curvature, is approximately 12 mm.

In interradii IV–III and II–I, no plates are discernible; they were likely very thin in construction. Only in the central field—presumed to be the anal interradius—can small plates be observed under high magnification; however, their specific arrangement can no longer be determined, nor, in particular, are there any traces of an anal pyramid. Furthermore, the state of preservation does not permit the identification of any specific opening that might have served as the entrance to the stone canal. Continuing the work of Bather, Foerste, and others, Kesling (1960, pp. 139–192) was able to demonstrate—or render highly probable—the presence of a hydropore situated within the anal interradius (between the anal pyramid and the apex) in 15 genera of Edrioasteroidea; for *Pyrgocystis*, however, such a demonstration remains outstanding.

The main body of the theca—the "tower"—is formed by rows of strongly adorally imbricated plates. The size, outline, sculpture, and arrangement of these thecal plates are not uniform along the entire length of the tower. Naturally, the varying stresses experienced during the compaction of the originally three-dimensional tube during burial play a role in this—albeit likely only a minor one. In the uppermost section (Pl. 2, Fig. 2)—immediately beneath the ambulacral crown—four or five rows are situated very closely together, followed by two rows that are less densely spaced, and finally one row that stands almost free; notably, the overall diameter decreases from top to bottom. In 1930, R. Richter identified a distinct upper, conical section characterized by less densely spaced rows of plates in his specimen of *Pyrgocystis octogona*. However, due to numerous fracture lines in these apparently rather thin plates, it is impossible to determine precisely how many transverse rows of plates constitute this particular section of the theca.

Descending from the nearly exposed row of plates, numerous rows of strongly overlapping plates follow; each consists of two alternating rows of four plates apiece; their number totals 2 × 36 = 72 with certainty, though an additional row is also possible. In the upper section, they are entirely flat; their surface—much like that of the plates in the conical section—is very finely longitudinally striated, with 20 to 25 slightly anastomosing striae occurring per millimeter. Spines or spine bases are nowhere present. Toward the middle of the column’s length, the anterior margin of the plates becomes thickened; it is formed by two slightly granulated ridges meeting at an obtuse angle; simultaneously, a faint, short median keel develops within the angle formed by these two thickened ridges—though not quite as pronounced as in *Pyrgocystis octogona* (R. RICHTER 1930, p. 294, Fig. 1h). The lowest six to eight rows are once again strongly overlapping and were evidently composed of entirely smooth plates. The diameter of the tube diminishes slightly in its lower third, only to increase again by a few millimeters toward the basal sac.

Whereas in typical *Pyrgocystis*, specimens with a preserved basal region exhibit a tapering down to a small attachment surface (BATHER 1915, Pl. 3 Figs. 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 10; RUEDEMANN 1925, Pl. 13 Figs. 2, 4, 5), the Bundenbach form possesses the peculiar sac-like appendage described by RIEVERS (p. 11) (Pl. 2 Fig. 4). Its surface presents a textured appearance, characterized by fine, granular ridges in the upper section and fine spicules in the lower section. In the central region and along the margins, one observes small, elongate, triangular to polygonal plates ranging from 0.3 to 0.8 mm in diameter, each surmounted by tiny spicules. The transition from the basal sac to the theca is not abrupt; rather, it is mediated by plates that progressively increase in size and gradually align themselves in a parallel arrangement.

B. The Systematic Position of the Species *coronaeformis* within the Genus *Pyrgocystis*

Most species of the genus *Pyrgocystis* are known from the Lower Silurian (Ordovician) of North America (*sardesoni* BATHER 1915, genotype), Scotland (*grayae* BATHER 1915), and Russia (*volborthi*, *gracilis*, and *pulkovi* HECKER 1939), as well as from the Upper Silurian (Gotlandian) of Gotland (*sulcata*, *procera*, and *cylindrica* ANGELIN 1892), England (*ansticei* BATHER 1915), and North America (*batheri* RUEDEMANN 1925). Furthermore, JAEKEL (1927, p. 4; see also REGNÉLL 1960, p. 165) mentions "an apparently very primitive new form from Norway."

In those species where the basal region is preserved, it appears attenuated, composed of fewer and smaller plates, and equipped with a small attachment surface for sessile fixation. The morphological distinction between these species may become even sharper as our knowledge of them improves.

The type species, *sardesoni*, is distinguished by a dense armature of spines along the ambulacra and by 22 very closely spaced rows of plates; *grayae* is distinguished by 10 rows of plates; the three Russian species by 8–9 rows of plates; *sulcata* and *batheri* by straight rows of plates separated by furrows; and *ansticei* by a rapidly increasing diameter. *Procera* and *cylindrica* are distinguished by a cylindrical tube and less overlapping plates. All these species attain only a modest height—6–13 mm where measurable, and an estimated 20 mm where inferred.

Apart from this, the Lower Devonian *Pyrgocystis octogona* R. Richter is characterized by its impressive height—exceeding 46 mm—by the possession of a conically expanding upper section, and by the presence of a median keel on its plates. The equally Lower Devonian Bundenbach species *Pyrgocystis coronaeformis* Rievers undoubtedly stands closer to *P. octogona* than to the other species. Given our incomplete knowledge of *Pyrgocystis octogona*, any comparison must be restricted to the characteristics of the theca. The following similarities can be noted:

Substantial theca length = 57 mm;
average size of the visible portion of the plates approximately 1.3 mm;
a faint median keel present on some of the central thecal plates;
the theca expands slightly conically in its upper section (a feature possibly also indicated in *Pyrgocystis grayae* Bather 1915, Pl. 3, Figs. 1–2).

In addition, the following differences distinguishing *P. coronaeformis* from *P. octogona* should be noted:

The theca is noticeably broader (b): 8–10 mm compared to 4.5–6.0 mm;
the theca is simultaneously taller: 44 mm compared to approximately 33 mm;
consequently, the theca is less slender: the length-to-width ratio is 5:1, as opposed to 6:1;
the conical section is lower and expands less toward the top—specifically, the ratio of cone to tube is 0.19:1, compared to 0.39:1.

Although these differences preclude the specific identification of these two forms as a single species, the similarities they share—particularly when compared to the Silurian forms—cannot be overlooked. When one further considers the peculiar basal sac (present in place of a standard attachment surface) observable on the Bundenbach specimen, it becomes necessary to maintain these forms as distinct entities. Given the current state of our knowledge, it does not yet appear warranted to establish a distinct genus; doing so would undervalue the most striking shared characteristic—the "tower." It should suffice to erect a separate subgenus, *Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen., within the genus *Pyrgocystis* Bather.

Diagnosis of *Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen.: *Pyrgocystis* (characterized by a tower-shaped theca formed of adoral-imbricating, alternating rows of plates, and by five ambulacral grooves featuring robust marginal plates) with the following distinctive features: theca large and slender, expanding conically toward the apex; thecal plates in part possessing reinforced margins and median keels; basal sac composed of small plates. Type species of the subgenus: *Pyrgocystis coronaeformis* Rievers (1961). Derivation of the name: from *Rhenus* (Latin for "the Rhine") and *pyrgos* (Greek for "the tower").

C. The Phylogenetic Position of *Pyrgocystis* (*Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen.)

R. RICHTER (1930, pp. 285–286) demonstrated that, from the Lower Silurian to the Upper Silurian, the evolutionary trajectory within the genus *Pyrgocystis* proceeded from 22 plates per double transverse row of the theca, through 10, down to 8; the species described by HECKER (1939) from the Russian Lower Silurian—possessing 8 to 9 plates—fit well into this conceptual framework. The two Lower Devonian species, *octogona* and *coronaeformis*, with their plate count of 8, likewise align with this pattern. However, it does not seem possible to me

**17**

to simply place the Bundenbach species *coronaeformis* at the terminus of a lineage that would have extended from primitive Edrioasteroidea through the aforementioned Silurian *Pyrgocystis* forms. For the basal sac of *Pyrgocystis* (*Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen.) *coronaeformis* bears a strong resemblance to the basal structures found among early Edrioasteroidea. *Thecocystis sacculus* JAEKEL (1899, p. 43, Pl. 1 Figs. 1a, 1b)—from the Lower Silurian of Cincinnati—is comparable not only in its robust, tumid ambulacral plates but also in its possession of a basal region composed of small plates; based on the irregular tumidity and the seamless displacement of the margins of the attachment surface, JAEKEL inferred that the integument was formerly flexible; the basal sac of the Bundenbach form exhibits marginal ridges that likewise suggest a degree of flexibility. Furthermore, similarities may exist between the early edrioasteroid *Thecocystis* and *Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen. regarding the general habitus of their basal regions. ...persist, after Jaekel points to an "acorn-shaped individual lacking a basal attachment surface — figured by Haig (1871, Pl. 6, Fig. 4) — of which the author states that it 'appears to have been quite free.'"

Only after further discoveries—particularly from the Upper Silurian—will it be possible to see clearly whether *Rhenopyrgus* nov. subgen. traces its origins back to the typical *Pyrgocystis* of the Lower Silurian, having acquired its basal sac as a late evolutionary innovation, or whether the basal sac represents an ancient ancestral trait, thereby necessitating a completely independent lineage for *Rhenopyrgus*.
