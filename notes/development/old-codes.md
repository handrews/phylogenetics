# More random Gemini conversations

Your guess is absolutely correct. If you are reading a taxonomic paper from the late 1800s, the concept of what made a nomenclatural act "valid" was vastly different, highly relaxed, and often borders on what modern taxonomists call "nomenclatural anarchy". [1]
During the late 19th century, authors replacing a preoccupied genus rarely laid out explicit new combinations for every single species. They operated under a set of historical contexts and specific loopholes that the modern [International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN)](https://code.iczn.org/) has since had to retroactively fix:
## 1. There Was No Unified "Code"
The first true international agreement (Règles internationales de la Nomenclature zoologique) wasn't adopted until 1905. In the late 1800s, scientists were fractured into regional factions following conflicting rulebooks: [1, 2]

*
* British and American scientists largely followed the Strickland Code (1842).
* French scientists followed the Chaper/Blanchard Rules (1881).
* German scientists followed the Carus Rules (1894).
* American ornithologists used the AOU Code (1885). [1, 3, 4]
*

Because these systems were essentially advisory "gentlemen's agreements," individual authors routinely customized practices to suit their own logic. [1]
## 2. The Rule of "Implicit" Combinations
In the late 1800s, if an author published a nomen novum for a genus and wrote something like: "The genus X-us is preoccupied, so I propose Y-us to replace it," they considered their work done.
They expected the scientific community to inherently understand that all species previously belonging to X-us were now part of Y-us. Authors rarely spent the ink or typesetting costs to explicitly list out and print Y-us speciesA, Y-us speciesB, etc.
## How the Modern ICZN Handles This Retroactively
Because thousands of species were moved this way without a formal "comb. nov." declaration, the modern Code has a specific retroactive safety net for names published before 1931.
According to ICZN Article 11.9.3, a species-group name must be published in combination with a genus name, but that genus name can be explicit or implicit by context. [5]

*
* The "Deemed" Rule: If a 19th-century author established a new genus name to replace a preoccupied one, modern nomenclature databases (like ZooBank) deem the new combinations to have been established at that exact moment, even if the author never explicitly typed out the new binomen strings. The author of the new genus name is credited with authorship of the new combination. [6]
*

## The Catch: Gender Agreement Confusion
Because 19th-century authors didn't explicitly spell out the new combinations, they also didn't correct the suffixes of the specific epithets to match the grammatical gender of the new genus. If a masculine genus (-us) was replaced by a feminine replacement genus (-a), the active combinations effectively existed in a state of grammatical limbo until subsequent catalogs or revisions physically printed the corrected spellings (e.g., changing X-us albus to Y-us alba). [7]
