In 1740, the Swedish geologist and mining official Daniel Tilas (https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fg%2F121v7y70&q=Daniel+Tilas) published a foundational paper in the history of earth sciences titled "Mineral-historia öfwer Osmunds-berget uti Rättwiks Sochn och Öster-Dalarne". [1] (https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tilas)
This work is celebrated for introducing several concepts crucial to modern geology:

1. Birth of Boulder Tracing (Glacial Indicator Mapping)

Tilas's 1740 report contains the earliest recognized methodology for boulder tracing (or drift prospecting). He observed that loose, mineralized boulders scattered across the landscape could be traced back to their bedrock source by mapping their distribution. Even though he wrote this more than a century before glacial theory was widely accepted, his principles remain the baseline for modern-day mineral exploration in glaciated terrains. [1] (https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tilas), [2] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229754408_Subaquatic_flow_tills_A_new_interpretation_for_the_genesis_of_some_laminated_till_deposits)

2. Early Petroleum and Stratigraphic Observation

The paper provides a detailed stratigraphic description of the Osmundsberg area in Dalarna, Sweden. Tilas mapped and described the occurrence of native petroleum and hydrocarbon deposits trapped in the rock layers. Geologist Hollis Dow Hedberg later highlighted this paper as one of the oldest systematic attempts to document the relationship between stratigraphy and petroleum. [1] (https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsl/books/book/2022/chapter/106718828/Early-geological-studies-and-mapping-in-Sweden), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tilas), [3] (https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tilas)

3. Coining of the Term "Feldspar"

In this exact 1740 treatise, Tilas became the first person to use the Swedish word "Feldtspats" to describe the common rock-forming mineral. This was later adopted into English as feldspar, which is today recognized as the most abundant mineral group in the Earth's crust. [1] (https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-the-first-use-of-the-term-%C2%BBFeldtspats%C2%BB-etc.)-by-Zenz%C3%A9n/e3c196490a9497ed52cf4667cf419d39eaece93b), [2] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tilas)

Both pagination formats are authentic, but the 202–209 pagination is the original, first-edition printing from 1740. [1] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1068797117300056)
The discrepancy comes down to how the Swenska Wetenskaps Academiens Handlingar (Proceedings of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences) was distributed and later bound: [1] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1068797117300056)
• Pages 202–209: This is the pagination from the original, standalone issue published for the first quarter of the year (Januar, Februar, Martius) in 1740. [1] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1068797117300056)
• Pages 194–201: This pagination appears in the second edition (reprint). Because the early volumes of the Academy's proceedings were highly sought after, the Academy completely reset the type and reprinted the earliest volumes later in the 18th century (most notably a major combined second-edition printing in 1741 and subsequent years). Because the typeface and margins were slightly modified for the reprint, the text shifted backward by exactly 8 pages. [1] (https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsl/books/book/2022/chapter/106718828/Early-geological-studies-and-mapping-in-Sweden)
